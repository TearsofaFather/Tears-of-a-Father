## Chapter 12: The Morning Paper

> *Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets.* — Amos 3:7

**Word study: *secret* — סוֹד** Hebrew *sod* (Strong's H5475). A secret, a counsel; at its root, a circle of close friends sitting together and talking in confidence. God does not shout His plans across a crowded street. He tells them to the ones who sit with Him. "The secret of the LORD is with them that fear him." (Psalm 25:14)

In the days of Jeroboam king of Israel, there was a herdsman in Tekoa named Amos, who kept sheep and gathered sycomore fruit, and had no thought of being a prophet (Amos 1:1; 7:14). The LORD took him from behind the flock and sent him north to Bethel, to the king's own house of worship, to tell the people what was coming (Amos 7:15). Amaziah the priest came out to stop him: "O thou seer, go, flee thee away into the land of Judah, and there eat bread, and prophesy there: But prophesy not again any more at Bethel: for it is the king's chapel, and it is the king's court" (Amos 7:12-13). And Amos answered, "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me as I followed the flock" (Amos 7:14-15). Then he told them the worst of it. The days would come, saith the Lord GOD, when He would send a famine in the land: not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD (Amos 8:11).

\* \* \*

Walt Simmons woke at a quarter after five, the same as every morning for as long as he could remember, without an alarm.

He lay a minute listening to the house. The furnace ticked. A car went by on the wet street. On the other side of the bed, the quilt was smooth and tucked tight under the pillow, just as his wife had always made it, and as he still made it every morning, though nobody slept on that side anymore. He had stopped reaching for her in the dark years back. He still noticed every morning that he didn't.

He got up in stages, the only way his knees and the wired breastbone from Brandick would let him. He put on his robe and his slippers and went down the stairs one at a time with his cane hooked over his wrist and his free hand pressed flat to the fresh scar under his robe, twelve days out of Brandick.

The murmur the doctors had been listening to for ten years had turned out to be a valve giving way. On the first Sunday in November, David from two doors down had found him on his kitchen floor with the newspaper still in his hand and called the ambulance, and the ambulance took him to Brandick Memorial, the old heart hospital on the hill, because it was the nearest cardiac unit. A surgeon there opened his chest that night and rebuilt the valve with her own two hands. Brandick had been mending hearts since before Walt was born. There was a brass plaque in the lobby with the founding family's name on it, and under it a line from Scripture that he had read three times on his way out the door: *I will restore health unto thee, and I will heal thee of thy wounds.* (Jeremiah 30:17) He had come home that Thursday against the surgeon's advice, forbidden to drive or lift anything heavier than a coffee cup. He had lifted the percolator every morning since.

In the kitchen he filled the dented pot, set it on the burner, and stood by the stove until it began to knock. Then he went out on the porch for the papers.

There were three of them, the same three he had taken for decades: the *Post*, the *New York Times*, and the *Wall Street Journal*, each in its own plastic sleeve, scattered across the steps where the boy had thrown them from his car. He was not supposed to bend. He bent for each one anyway, with one hand pressed flat to his chest. It was still dark. The streetlights were on, and the air smelled like wet leaves and somebody's chimney.

Across the street, at Ezra's house, the porch light was burning.

It had been burning every night since the summer. Walt knew who left it on. He had seen Mia do it, standing on her toes to reach the switch by the front door in her nightgown, every evening at dusk. He had never asked her who it was for. He thought he probably knew.

He went back inside and poured his coffee, black, into the brown mug with the chip in the handle. He spread the papers across the dinner table in the order he always read them. Then he took the small black Bible out of the pocket of his robe, where he had left it the night before, and laid it open beside the *Post*. He set a red pen next to it. He had been reading the Bible and the morning paper side by side at this table for longer than some of his neighbors had been alive.

The red pen was a habit from the shop, where a mistake that got past him would be printed half a million times by morning. He had never lost it. Every morning he read the paper with a red pen in his hand, and he corrected it. He circled the misspellings. He fixed the dates. Twice he had mailed his corrections to the editor, and twice the editor had printed them.

Lately the pen had found a second use.

\* \* \*

The *Post* had the Jerusalem story on the front page again, below the fold.

A week had passed since the Council for Universal Moral Law closed in Jerusalem, and the delegates had gone home. But they had left something behind. The paper said a working committee would stay on in Jerusalem through the winter to draw up "a binding framework for shared moral standards among nations," to be signed in the spring by every member state. A spokesman called it "the first truly global covenant." There was a photograph of the committee seated at a long white table, smiling.

Walt read the article twice. He found no misspellings in it. He picked up the red pen anyway, and in the margin beside the word *covenant* he wrote, in small square capitals: *DAN. 9:27.*

*And he shall confirm the covenant with many for one week… (Daniel 9:27)*

On the business page, under a story about grocery prices, the *Post* said bread had gone up for the eleventh month in a row. The wheat harvest had come in short on three continents. Walt circled the price of a loaf and wrote beside it, in red: *REV. 6:6. A measure of wheat for a penny.*

He turned the page.

The *Journal* was the one he understood least and read most carefully. On the second page of the markets section, under a picture of a man in a gray suit with his hand raised, the headline said that Adrian Crowe's exchange was offering a single digital settlement system to the central banks of forty-one countries, free of charge for the first five years. *One ledger for the world,* Crowe was quoted as saying. *No more borders on money.* Walt did not understand half of the article. He understood the verse, though. He wrote it out in full in the margin, because it was too important to abbreviate: *…that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark… (Revelation 13:17)*

Below it on the same page was a smaller story. A consortium led by Søren Halvard, whose satellites carried a third of the world's phone calls, had signed a ninety-nine-year lease with the government in Nuuk for the whole north coast of Greenland: rare-earth mines, deepwater ports for the new Arctic shipping lanes, and ground stations for his satellites dug into the ice, where the cold would run his servers for nothing. Two American presidents had tried to buy the island and been told it was not for sale. Halvard had not offered to buy it. He had offered to run it. Walt wrote in the margin: *DAN. 11:39. Divide the land for gain.*

He turned the page, and his coffee had gone lukewarm and bitter, and he didn't get up to warm it.

\* \* \*

At twenty to seven, the front door across the street opened, and Ezra came out.

He was carrying a travel mug and his tool bag, and he walked down to the truck without looking left or right, as he did every morning now. Ezra's color was good and his shoulders easy, and Walt, who had watched him walk to that truck every morning for years, had not seen him carry himself like that since before Angela got sick. Ezra opened the truck door. Then he stopped, and turned around, and looked back at the porch light still burning over his front door in the overcast morning. He stood there. Then he went back up the steps and switched it off, and came down again, and drove away.

At ten after seven, Mia came out with Loretta, in her purple coat with the hood up, swinging her lunch box. She saw Walt in his kitchen window, as she did every morning, and she waved with her whole arm. Walt held up the *Post* and waved it back at her, same as always. It was the best part of his day, and he had never told anyone that.

The school bus came and took her. The street was quiet again.

Walt looked down at the Bible lying open beside the paper. He had been reading the minor prophets all week, one a day, as he always did in the fall, and this morning it was Amos. He had read it fifty times. He read it again now, as he used to read a proof.

*Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets. (Amos 3:7)*

He looked from the verse to the newspaper and back again. Jerusalem. A covenant for the nations. A lamp for the children of Abraham. One ledger for the world. Every bit of it was printed in that small black book in his hand, and had been for two and a half thousand years, and every bit of it was on the front of the morning paper.

\* \* \*

Kambria knocked at a quarter to eight, still in her agency fleece from an all-night run at the lab, with a bag of groceries on her hip.

Since Brandick she came by twice a week on her way home from the hospital, and she brought him milk and bread and the oranges he liked, and she took his blood pressure at the dinner table whether he wanted her to or not. He still insisted he didn't need any of it, and the cuff was waiting on the table whenever she came in.

She wrapped it around his arm and pumped it up, and while she watched the needle she read the margins of the papers upside down. He saw her eyes go from *DAN. 9:27* to the circled loaf of bread to the long line in red across the *Journal*.

"One forty over eighty-eight," she said. "You've been drinking coffee."

"I've been drinking coffee since Nixon."

She let the air out of the cuff and turned the *Journal* around so she could read it right side up. She read the article about Crowe, and the verse he had copied beside it, and said nothing. Then she took the kitchen scissors from his drawer without asking, cut the article out along the margin, dated the back of it in her slanting hand, and slid it into the back of the ninth notebook, the one that lived in her bag.

"All my life I've read that Book," Walt said. "Every morning, right here, with the paper next to it." He tapped the Bible with one crooked finger. "Kings and battles. Babylon and Persia. Things that happened a long time ago to people who weren't me." He ran his eye down the margins. "I used to read the Bible like history, Kambria. Now it reads like the morning paper."

Kambria took the chair by the window, across from him. She looked tired, and older than she had looked in the summer, but her eyes on him were kind.

"Walt," she said. "Do you pray about it?"

He picked up the red pen and put it down again.

"I read it," he said.

"That's not what I asked."

"The Lord knows what He's doing. He doesn't need an old man telling Him how to run things."

"It isn't about telling Him anything," she said. "It's about talking to Him."

Walt folded the *Journal* in half, and then in thirds, edges squared, as he folded everything. "You'll be late getting to bed," he said. "You've been up all night."

She searched his face. Then she got up and put the milk in his refrigerator, and the oranges in the bowl on the counter, and at the door she turned around.

"I'm praying for you, Walt," she said. "Every night. You can't stop me."

"Never tried," he said.

She went out. He heard her car door close out front, but not the engine. She sat out there sometimes before she drove, with her head bowed over the wheel. He knew what she was doing, and he did not look.

\* \* \*

He sat at the table with the papers folded and the Bible open and the kitchen clock ticking over the stove.

He had come close once. Every night for weeks he had sat in a plastic chair beside a hospital bed at Capital General, with his wife's hand in his and the machines breathing for her. Near the end she had whispered, *Walter, will you pray with me?* and he had said, *You pray, honey. I'll listen.* She had prayed for him.

On the last day the doctors came in together, and he had known what they were going to say before they said it. He had buried her that spring, and after the funeral he had come home to this kitchen and opened this Bible and gone on reading it every morning. He had never stopped reading it. He had never once talked to the One who wrote it. Reading, he could do. Reading never told him no.

He had read every word of the prophets fifty times. He could tell you the dimensions of the image on the plain of Dura and the number of Daniel's weeks and the names of the nations that would come down out of the north. And until Kambria started coming by, the chair across the table from him had sat empty for years.

He turned, without quite knowing why, to the ninth chapter of Daniel, where he had taught Kambria to count the weeks on the bench a few weeks back. He found the verse he had skipped over that day, the one that came before all the numbers.

*In the first year of his reign I Daniel understood by books the number of the years, whereof the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah the prophet, that he would accomplish seventy years in the desolations of Jerusalem. (Daniel 9:2)*

That was him. That was exactly him. He had understood by books. He had done the arithmetic.

He read the next verse.

*And I set my face unto the Lord God, to seek by prayer and supplications, with fasting, and sackcloth, and ashes… (Daniel 9:3)*

Walt read it through twice. He sat with the red pen in his hand. He knew there was a word missing from his margins, and that it was not a verse.

Then he closed the Bible, and put it back in the pocket of his robe, and got up and washed his cup.

He was drying it at the sink when he saw the car.

It was a gray Chevrolet Malibu, parked at the curb three houses down, a man's gray sleeve resting on the driver's door, on the far side of the street. He had noticed it earlier that morning, when he went out for the papers, and thought nothing of it; people parked where they liked. It had not moved since. Its engine was running. He could see the thin white breath of the exhaust in the cold.

Kambria's little car pulled away from the curb and went down the street toward Rhode Island Avenue.

A moment later, the gray sedan pulled out and went after her.

Walt stood at the sink with the cup in one hand and the dish towel in the other until both cars were gone. Then he went back to the table and picked up the red pen. He had read the plate as it passed. He read everything. He wrote it in the margin of the *Post*, beside the story about the Jerusalem framework, in square capitals.

It was the first thing he had written in a margin in all those years that was not a verse or a correction. He sat looking at it, and hoped he was a foolish old man.
