## Chapter 18: The Wall

> *After many days thou shalt be visited: in the latter years thou shalt come into the land that is brought back from the sword, and is gathered out of many people, against the mountains of Israel, which have been always waste…* — Ezekiel 38:8

**Word study: *latter* — אַחֲרִית** Hebrew *acharith* (Strong's H319). The last part, the end, what comes after. It is the word Moses used for "the latter days," and the word Job used when God blessed "the latter end of Job more than his beginning." It does not mean someday. It means the closing chapter of a story God has already begun.

*Son of man, set thy face against Gog, the land of Magog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal, and prophesy against him… And I will turn thee back, and put hooks into thy jaws, and I will bring thee forth, and all thine army, horses and horsemen, all of them clothed with all sorts of armour, even a great company with bucklers and shields, all of them handling swords…* (Ezekiel 38:2, 4)

\* \* \*

The last pin went in at six-fifteen on Wednesday morning, before the sun was up.

Kambria stood back from the wall of her spare room with the box of pushpins in one hand and a mug of coffee going lukewarm in the other, and she looked at what she had made.

It had started in September as a single sheet taped beside the window: Ezekiel 38, in large type, the names circled in red. Gog. Magog. Persia. Gomer. Togarmah. She had pinned a headline beside each one as it came, until the paper met the other wall, the one headed *WHO PAYS?*, with the Meridian filing and the Verity brochure and Adrian Crowe's raised hand. Now the two walls ran together at the corner, and she could no longer say where one story ended and the other began.

Across the top, on masking tape: *In the latter years thou shalt come into the land that is brought back from the sword.*

Under *Meshech and Tubal*, Moscow rearming Tehran. Under *Persia*, satellite photographs of mountain tunnels bombed shut in spring and dug open by autumn. Under *Gomer* and *Togarmah*, Israeli jets over a Syrian airbase, and a warning from Washington. Under *Cush* and *Put*, the Houthis reaching into Sudan, Turkish drones outside Tripoli. And at the bottom, pinned last, the story she had read at four-thirty that morning: a Russian general named Dmitri Volkov, in command of a new joint force, shaking hands with Iranian officers on a runway while the transport planes stood behind him with their tail ramps down.

*Thou shalt come from thy place out of the north parts.*

She had argued Ezekiel 38 with Angela for most of her adult life. Was Magog Russia, or something older? They had never agreed, never needed to, because it had always been far off, a picture drawn in ink that dried long ago. Now every name in the chapter had a headline under it.

She set the coffee on the sill and laid her palm flat over the general's face. She was not afraid. She felt the calm she knew from the bench, on the morning a culture finally turned after weeks of nothing.

Then she went to get Walt.

\* \* \*

He was waiting on his porch across the street from Ezra's house when she pulled up, in his brown suit jacket and his tweed cap, with his cane hooked over the porch rail. He had been up since five. He was always up by five.

"You said it was important," he said, before she had her door open. "You sounded on the phone like you'd been up all night."

"I have been."

"I can tell." He worked his way down the steps one at a time, gripping the rail, and she came around and took his elbow at the bottom. He let her. Nine days ago he would not have let her; nine days ago he had not been a man with a freshly stitched breastbone under his brown suit jacket. The murmur the doctors had been listening to for ten years had turned out to be a valve giving way, and on a Sunday morning David from two doors down had found him on his kitchen floor with the newspaper still in his hand and called the ambulance. The ambulance took him to Brandick Memorial, the old heart hospital on the hill, because it was the nearest cardiac unit, and a surgeon there opened his chest that night and rebuilt the valve with her own two hands. The Brandick nurses had been at it since before Walt was born. There was a brass plaque in the lobby with the founding family's name on it, and under it a line from Scripture that he had read three times on his way out the door: *I will restore health unto thee, and I will heal thee of thy wounds.* (Jeremiah 30:17) He had come home on Thursday against the surgeon's advice, forbidden to drive or lift anything heavier than a coffee cup, and he stopped on every step to let his heart catch up with him. "Is it the hospital? Did they call you?"

Walt had said one thing about it, in the hospital, to the nurse who came to turn him. He had asked for the Bible on the bedside table and opened it by feel, without his glasses, to a psalm he had known since he was a boy. *My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever.* (Psalm 73:26) "Mine failed," he told her, with a dry little laugh that hurt his stitches. "They tell me they fixed it. I'll believe it when I know whose strength it's running on."

"No. Nobody from the hospital is going to call me." She opened the passenger door for him. "I want you to see something, Walt. At my house. I can't bring it to you. It's on a wall."

He looked at her a moment longer, with his sharp gray eyes. Then he got in.

Across the street, Ezra's truck was in the driveway. The porch light was off. In the upstairs window, the one that had been Jalen's room since he was four, the blind was halfway up, and Kambria thought she saw someone move behind it. She looked away before she could be sure. *Thursday,* she told herself. *Tomorrow.*

She drove to Brookland, and neither of them spoke. Walt held his cap on his knee. At the light on Michigan Avenue she checked the mirror, as she had checked it every few blocks for a week now, and the road behind her was empty except for a city bus.

"You're looking for the gray car," Walt said.

"Yes."

"I watched for it this morning. From my kitchen." He kept his eyes ahead. "It hasn't been on our street since Monday."

"It was in Petworth yesterday afternoon."

Walt said nothing, but his hand closed on the head of his cane and stayed closed.

\* \* \*

She left him in the doorway of the spare room and went to make fresh coffee, because she knew he would want to read it without anyone watching him read.

When she came back he was standing just inside the door with his hat in one hand and his cane in the other, reading the wall from left to right and top to bottom, one headline at a time. His lips moved a little. Every now and then he leaned closer to read the small type under a photograph. She stood behind him with the two cups and waited.

He read for seventeen minutes. She timed it on the kitchen clock without meaning to.

When he got to the bottom, to the general on the runway, he stopped. He stood with his chin up, reading it again. And Kambria watched the color leave his face. It went out of it from the top down, from his forehead to his jaw, until his skin was the color of the newsprint he had handled all his life.

"Sit down, Walt."

"I'm all right."

"You're not. Sit." She set the cups on the desk and pulled the desk chair around for him, and after a moment he lowered himself into it. His hands were shaking. She took his wrist without asking and found the pulse with two fingers, as she had a hundred times in the last month at his dinner table, and counted. Fast, but steady.

"I was there," he said.

"I know."

"No. I mean I was *there.*" He lifted his cane and pointed with the rubber tip at the clipping under *Persia*, then at the one under *Meshech*. "Not on this wall. In the paper. All those years I set those names, in lead and then in light. Every war on that list, I put the headline on." He swallowed. "And before that, I was in one. Vietnam, 1971. They drafted me at nineteen and put me in a fuel truck, and I drove it through a country I couldn't have found on a map. You'd hear the rockets come in at night, and you'd lie in the dirt and listen for the boom. Some nights you could see the tracers go up. Little streaks of fire." His jaw worked. "One night a rocket came down on a tent line. Twenty-eight of ours. Kids from Pennsylvania, most of them."

Kambria waited. He was not finished.

"Ezra's father died in the Gulf," Walt said. "Calvin Patterson. Artillery, up north, near the border. I didn't know him, not then. I found out years later, when I moved in across the street and Angela told me." He looked at the wall. "I came home from mine. He didn't come home from his. I used to watch that grown man shoot baskets in his driveway, with his father's watch on his wrist that didn't work, and think, *That could have been my kid.*"

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody does. I don't talk about it." He was quiet. "Thirty-five years. And every one of those names is on your wall again. Persia. The Russians sending them the missiles. The Turks building ships." He shook his head. "I told myself it was over when that war ended. Over and done."

"You set a lot of headlines that turned out not to be the end."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh. "I did. Peace in 1973. Cease-fire in 1991. *Mission accomplished.*" He rubbed his face with his free hand. "Every one of them, I believed."

She handed him his coffee, and he held it in both hands without drinking it.

"Read me the part I missed," he said. "You've got a look on your face. There's a part I missed."

\* \* \*

There was.

She opened her Bible to Ezekiel 38 and sat down on the edge of the daybed across from him, so their knees almost touched.

"Everybody reads the list," she said. "Verse thirteen." She turned the book around. "*Sheba, and Dedan, and the merchants of Tarshish, with all the young lions thereof, shall say unto thee, Art thou come to take a spoil?*"

Walt read it twice. He was the only person she knew who always read a thing twice.

"Arabia," he said. "Sheba and Dedan."

"They're not in the army. They stand off to the side and protest. The nations that fought Israel for fifty years are saying *Don't.*" She tapped the page. "Ezekiel wrote that list twenty-six hundred years ago, before there was a Saudi Arabia, or a Turkey, or an Iran. And it lines up with the evening news, down to who's left out."

"The Gulf states signed peace deals with Israel," Walt said, almost to himself. "Five, six years ago. I read it in three papers. *Sheba and Dedan, standing apart.*"

The color had come back into his face, and with it the reporter's look.

Kambria touched the clipping under *Gomer*. "One more thing. Ezekiel never says the people of those nations are wicked. He says their rulers come with a great company." She looked at the soldiers in their rows, a woman in a headscarf carrying groceries past a mural, a boy on a bicycle in Istanbul. "I pray for every name on this wall. For Israel, because God said He'd bless those who bless her. And for the mothers in Tehran and Ankara who never asked for any of it."

"Angela used to say that."

"She said it first."

Walt set down his coffee, put both hands on the head of his cane, and pushed himself up.

"I could quote you Ezekiel 38 in my sleep," he said. "And I have never once prayed for anybody on that wall. Not one."

"It's not too late."

"No," he said. "I suppose it isn't."

He reached for his cap.

"Leave it," Kambria said. "You're staying for lunch."

She had put the food in the oven on low before she left that morning, under foil, because she had known he would say exactly that. Catfish with lemon and garlic. Macaroni and cheese, the creamy kind with no crust, because the crusted one was for tomorrow. Cabbage fried slow with kielbasa. The discharge papers said no salt, so she had used lemon and pepper and smoked paprika, and trusted them.

Walt bowed his head without being asked. "Lord, thank You for this food, and the hands that made it, and every one of these days You've seen fit to add to mine. Amen."

He took one bite of the cabbage and stopped, eyes closed.

"My mother made it like this. Thursdays, the day before payday, when there was more month than money." He opened his eyes. "I haven't tasted that in fifty years."

"Eat," Kambria said gently.

*Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith.* She thought it and did not say it. Some verses were better eaten than said.

"Angela would have said the fish needed hot sauce," Walt said, pushing back his plate.

"Angela would have been right."

They laughed, carefully, Walt with one hand flat against his breastbone, and for the length of a meal two people who had lost the same woman were not afraid.

\* \* \*

Before she drove him home, she went into the kitchen and took down the blue concordance.

He watched from the dinner table while she cut through the packing tape on the spine with a paring knife and slid the thumb drive out from between the binding and the cover. It was small and black, with a white label she had written herself: *PETWORTH / VERITY / PALE.* She had made a second copy on Monday night at the twenty-four-hour print shop on Rhode Island Avenue, and that copy was already in an envelope at the bottom of her purse, addressed to a reporter at the *Post* whose name she had found under the Meridian story. She had not mailed it. She could not yet tell whether mailing it would protect anyone or get someone hurt.

She taped the drive back into the spine with fresh tape until it lay flat again, and then she put the concordance in a grocery bag and set it on the table in front of Walt.

"I need you to keep this for me."

He looked at the bag, and then at her. "What is it?"

"It's all of it. Every Petworth file I have. The samples, the program, the company that paid for it, a file with the first case in it dated a week before the first case happened." She sat down across from him. "If I had my badge, I'd take it to the right people. I don't have my badge. And I'm not sure anymore who the right people are."

"Kambria." His voice was very low. "Is that why the car's been following you?"

"Yes."

"Then why on God's earth would you give it to me?"

She had thought about that all night, lying awake, and she gave him the answer she had found.

"Because they're watching me, Walt. They're watching my house and my car and my phone. They've been in my spare room once already." She held his eyes. "Nobody watches an old man's dinner table. Nobody thinks an old man with a cane is the one with the evidence. You keep it on the shelf with your concordance, right next to your own. A man with two concordances is just an old man who forgot he already owned one."

He almost smiled. It didn't last.

"And if something happens to you?"

"Then you give it to Ezra." She said it steadily. She had decided that too, in the night. "Not the police. Not the CDC. Ezra. And you make him read Angela's notebook first. The black one, in his hall closet. Make him read the notebook before he plugs in the drive. Otherwise none of it will make any sense to him."

"Ezra," Walt said. "Who told you to get off his porch."

"Ezra. Who said yesterday I could come for Thanksgiving." She heard her own voice catch on it, and steadied it. "He said it himself. Jalen told me."

Walt looked down at the grocery bag.

"All right," he said finally. "But nothing's going to happen to you. You hear me? You're going to bring that macaroni tomorrow, and you're going to sit at that table, and I'm going to eat too much and fall asleep in Ezra's recliner with my mouth open, and you're going to be embarrassed for me."

"I'd like that."

"So would I." He pulled the bag toward him and laid his hand on top of it. "So would I."

\* \* \*

She spent the afternoon in her kitchen, cooking, because cooking gave her hands work while her mind went where it needed to go.

The macaroni and cheese was Angela's recipe, written out in Angela's round hand on an index card that had gone soft and spotted from years of Thanksgivings: three kinds of cheese, evaporated milk, a little mustard, a crust of buttered crumbs on top. Kambria had made it every year since Angela got sick. She grated the cheese by hand because Angela had always said bagged cheese had sawdust in it. She did not know if that was true. She grated it by hand anyway.

At two o'clock her phone rang, and the screen said *JALEN*, and when she picked up it was not Jalen.

"Miss Kambria?" It was Mia, breathing hard, as if she had run to the phone. "Jalen said I could call you. Are you bringing the cheesy noodles?"

Kambria laughed out loud, and the tightness she had carried since the wall that morning loosened. "I am making them right now, baby. Right this minute. I've got cheese all over my hands."

"The orange kind? With the crunchy top?"

"The orange kind with the crunchy top. Your mommy's kind."

There was a satisfied pause. Then Mia lowered her voice, as she did whenever she told a secret. "I made up a story," she said. "For Daddy. I'm going to tell it to him tonight at bedtime. It's about a lamp."

"A lamp?"

"I can't tell you. It's for Daddy first." Kambria could hear her smiling. "But you'll like it. It has you in it. A little bit. You're the lady with the oil."

Kambria stopped grating. She stood at the counter with the cheese in one hand and the phone against her ear and her eyes suddenly full.

"Mia," she said. "Who told you about the lady with the oil?"

"Mommy," Mia said, as if it were obvious. "A long time ago. When she was sick. She said some ladies were ready and some ladies weren't, and the ready ones had oil. She said you were a ready one." There was a muffled sound, and Jalen's voice in the background, and then Mia again, in a hurry. "Jalen says I have to go. Bye, Miss Kambria. Don't forget the crunchy top."

"I won't forget."

The line went quiet. Kambria set the phone down and stood at the counter with her hands on either side of the bowl, and cried, briefly, for Angela, who had told a five-year-old about the wise virgins from a hospice bed. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve and finished the cheese.

\* \* \*

While the dish baked she sat at the dinner table with the ninth notebook and her phone and read the news, because she had promised herself she would watch everything until the end, whatever the end was.

All of it came from Dubai. Adrian Crowe was hosting a gala that night in his tower to launch the Global Ledger: one account for every person on earth, one currency, no cash. The guest list ran for a column. Bishop Sterling. Lucas Thorne. Dmitri Volkov, the general from her wall, who would be drinking champagne in a glass ballroom before the day was out. At the bottom, a line from Crowe: *"Your hand is your account. Peace is no longer a hope. It is an arithmetic."*

She opened the notebook to a clean page and dated it. *November 25, 2026.* Under it she wrote *Dubai. The Ledger. Your hand is your account.* And in the center of the page, the verse that had waited in her all afternoon.

*Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins, and that ye receive not of her plagues.* (Revelation 18:4)

It was what she would have said to every person on that list, if she could have stood at the door of the ballroom and said one thing. *Come out.* She closed the notebook and went to take the macaroni from the oven.

\* \* \*

The gray Malibu came back at nine-forty that night.

She saw it from the kitchen window while she was washing the grater and the mixing bowl. It rolled down Twelfth Street with its headlights on and stopped across from her house, under the streetlight that had been out since summer, and the headlights went off. The engine kept running. She could see the faint plume of the exhaust in the cold.

She dried her hands on the dish towel. She stood at the window for a full minute, deciding.

She could call the police. She had called them once, on Saturday, after she came home from New York to find her notebooks squared on the desk, and a young officer had stood in her spare room and looked at the walls covered with headlines and Bible verses, and then looked at her with polite, careful eyes, and written something in his notebook, and left. She could pull the blinds and go to bed and lie awake again. She could do what she had done every night for a week: watch, and wait, and write the plate number down one more time.

Or she could do what Angela would have done.

She took her coat off the hook by the door. She took a paper plate from the cupboard and cut a square of the macaroni out of the corner of the dish, the corner piece with the most crunchy top on it, because that was the best piece, and covered it with foil. She put her Bible in her coat pocket. Then she unlocked the front door and walked down her steps and across the street in her slippers, in the cold, straight to the driver's window of the gray sedan, and knocked on the glass.

Nothing happened. She could see her own reflection in the dark window: a tired woman of forty-two in a quilted coat, holding a paper plate.

She knocked again.

The window came down four inches. The man inside was perhaps fifty, with short gray hair and a heavy, ordinary face that could have stood in any lobby in any city and never been noticed. He was wearing a gray overcoat. She had seen him once before, sitting on a bench by the mailboxes at 312 East Forty-Third Street, reading a newspaper.

He said nothing, and his face gave her nothing.

"I'm Kambria," she said. "But you know that." She held out the plate. "It's macaroni and cheese. My best friend's recipe. You've been sitting out here in the freezing air for a week, and it's the night before Thanksgiving, and I thought you might be hungry."

He looked at the plate. For one second his face moved, and then it did not.

"Mr. Vale," he said, and his voice was flat and polite, "would like you to stay home tomorrow."

"I'm sorry," Kambria said. "I can't do that. I'm going to my family's for Thanksgiving. I've been invited." She did not lower the plate. "There's room at the table, if you'd like to come. I mean that. There always has been."

He studied her. She was surprised to find she was not afraid of him at all. She had been afraid all week, of the car, of the dark windows, of the photograph on the train. But now, with a paper plate in her hand, the fear had gone out of her, and pity had come in its place. He had a job to do, and it was this. He had spent a week of nights in a frozen car watching a woman read her Bible at a dinner table.

"Is there somebody waiting for you at home?" she said. "Tomorrow?"

The window went up.

The engine changed pitch, and the headlights came on, and the Malibu pulled away from the curb and drove off down Twelfth Street, and turned at the corner, and was gone.

Kambria stood in the street in her slippers with the plate in her hand until she could no longer hear it. Then she bowed her head, right there, under the dead streetlight, and prayed for him. She did not know his name. It was Julian Sharpe, and he had never once been prayed for by name. She prayed for him anyway. *Lord, You know who he is. You know who's waiting for him. Don't let him be lost.*

She went back inside, and locked the door, and put the macaroni in the refrigerator. She did not eat it. It was his.

\* \* \*

The alarm was set for five, so she could warm the dish and be at the Pattersons' by eight. Before she went up to bed she did what she did every night. She filled the lamp, trimmed the wick, and set it in the window where the street could see it.

Then she sat down at the kitchen table one last time with the ninth notebook, and wrote the date. *November 25, 2026. 11:20 p.m.* She wrote for a long while. When she finished, she slid the notebook into the gap behind the loose board under the window seat, where she had once hidden Angela's letters, and pressed the board home with her palm.

On the counter, the dish waited under its foil. She tore the back off an envelope and wrote on it. *For the Pattersons. Thursday. Back by eight. K.* She set it on top of the dish.

Out on Twelfth Street the dead streetlight buzzed, flickered, and stayed dark. No gray sedan. She told herself that was a good sign, and went upstairs.

\* \* \*

The first sound was an engine, which was wrong, because nobody drove down Twelfth Street at one-forty in the morning.

Then a second engine. Then a third.

She was at the bedroom window before she knew she had moved. Three black vans were rolling up the block with their lights off, slow as hearses, and behind them a dark sedan she knew. It stopped across from her house, under the dead streetlight. The engine idled. Nobody got out of it.

The vans stopped at her curb. Doors slid open together, and men in dark tactical gear poured out, and every one of them wore a red band on his left arm. They did not run. They did not need to. They moved like men who had done this before, and had been told in advance there would be no trouble.

She did not scream. She was surprised by that, later. She stood in her nightgown at the window with her heart hammering in her ears and said, out loud, to the empty room, "All right, Lord."

The pounding on the door shook the house.

"FEDERAL CONTAINMENT DIVISION. OPEN THE DOOR."

She went down the stairs barefoot. She had her hand on the knob when they took the door off its frame.

Light hit her, white, from four directions. A gloved hand closed around her arm, hard enough to bruise. She heard boots on her floors, in her kitchen, in her spare room. She heard glass break. The wall she had built, the Ezekiel wall, the headlines and the strings and the verses, was coming down in sheets under their hands.

"Look at this." An officer, his flashlight on the pinned pages. "Bible verses. Maps. Names." He turned the beam on her face, and there was nothing in his own. "Ma'am. Are you one of them?"

She said, "If you mean do I belong to Jesus Christ, yes."

"She's one of them," he said over his shoulder. It was not an accusation. It was a box being ticked.

They did not search her. They did not read her anything. A second man had her other arm, and her feet were bare on the cold boards, and they were already turning her toward the door when she saw the officer in the kitchen, standing at the window, with his gloved hand reaching for the lamp.

"No," she said.

He did not look at her. His thumb found the wick.

"Please." It came out of her smaller than she meant. "Please, don't put it out."

The hand stopped.

For three seconds nobody in the house moved. The little flame stood in the glass chimney and shook, and she saw it in his eyes, gold, over his mask. Something went through his face. She could not have said what. Maybe it was only that it was a lamp, and a woman in a nightgown begging for it, in a room full of men with guns.

He took his hand away.

"Leave it," he said. "It's not evidence."

They walked her out barefoot over the frozen concrete. At the curb, the black van gaped. Across the street the sedan sat under the dead light, engine running. She turned her head and found the face behind the windshield. Julian Sharpe's heavy, ordinary face was lit blue from the dash. His hands were on the wheel at ten and two. He did not look away from her. He did not look at her either. His eyes held something that she had not seen there when she held out the plate.

Then the van door closed on the street, and the dark.

\* \* \*

The cell was underground. She knew that before they took the hood off, because the air had changed. Cold, dead, recycled; the smell of concrete that had never seen weather.

It was perhaps eight feet by ten. A steel bench ran along one wall. A drain sat in the middle of the floor. A single caged bulb burned overhead. Above the drain, set into the ceiling, was a steel grille with a flat nozzle behind the slats, the kind she had seen over the decontamination showers at work. Under the bulb hung a placard, riveted to the block: **FEDERAL CONTAINMENT DIVISION — SUBLEVEL 3 — HOLDING B.**

They sat her on the bench. A guard knelt and locked a steel cuff around her left ankle, then her right, and ran the chain between them under the bench to a ring bolted into the floor. Another locked her wrists, in front, with a shorter chain to the same ring. The metal was colder than anything she had ever touched. It bit the bones.

Nobody spoke. The door shut. A bolt thrown, a second bolt, and then the sound that was almost the worst, the sound of footsteps walking away.

She sat in the humming light with her wrists chained under a bench and her feet bare on the concrete, and she waited for the fear.

It came, as it had to. It came up her spine and into her throat, and she let it. *Of course I'm afraid,* she thought. *I'm forty-two years old and I'm chained to a floor.* She did not fight it. She did the only thing she knew how to do with fear. She handed it up.

She thought of Mia's call. *You're the lady with the oil.* She thought about the five wise ones, and the five foolish, and the cry at midnight, and the ones who had oil in their vessels with their lamps and went in. *And they that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut.*

She did not know what time it was. She knew only that it was still dark above her, somewhere, and that the night before Thanksgiving had a morning on the other side of it.

"Lord," she said. Her voice came out cracked and small and the concrete gave it back to her. "I don't know what You're doing." She breathed. "I don't need to. You know where I am."

The bulb hummed.

"I left a light on in my window. Let it keep burning." She closed her eyes. "Whatever they do to me. Let my light keep burning."

\* \* \*

Across the city, in a house with a broken door and a floor strewn with paper, a small brass lamp stood on the kitchen sill and threw its steady gold light out onto an empty street.

On the counter, a dish waited under its foil. On top of it lay a note.

*Back by eight.*

The lamp burned on.
