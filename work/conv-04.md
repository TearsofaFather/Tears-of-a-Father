Ezra came in from work, took a beer from the refrigerator, and stood at the window over the sink. Across the yard the cello was playing.

"He's still at that thing?"

"That thing got him a scholarship audition."

"A scholarship for what? Sitting in a chair sawing on a box?"

"Mr. Hart says he's the best cellist that orchestra has had in twenty years," Angela said. "He's been teaching him for free since he was nine, Ezra. Free."

"When I was sixteen I was framing houses."

"When you were sixteen," Angela said, "you didn't have a father who came to anything."

The kitchen went still. Even the cello stopped. Ezra's thumb found the stopped watch on his wrist.

"Don't do that," he said. "Not in front of company."

"Kambria isn't company. And I'm not doing anything. I'm telling you your son is out there playing for a seat he keeps hoping you'll sit in."

He set the bottle down and picked it up again. "I'm going to shower."

He went up the stairs. Angela closed her eyes.

"You want to pray," Kambria asked softly, "or you want to sit?"

"Sit. Some nights all I've got left is his name."

"Mommy."

Mia stood in the doorway in her pajamas, a sheet of paper pressed to her chest.

"Baby, what are you doing up? It's been an hour."

"I made a picture for Miss Kambria." She laid it on the red notebook. A house, the crooked porch light, and the whole sky colored red.

"That's beautiful, sweet pea. Why'd you make the sky red?"

"That's what the sky does when Jesus is almost here."

Angela's pen stopped. "Who told you that, baby?"

"Nobody. I just know it." She pointed to a brown shape in the front window. "That's Daddy on the couch. He doesn't see it 'cause he's sleeping."

Upstairs, the shower came on.

"Bed," Angela said, too brightly. "Right now. Up you go."
