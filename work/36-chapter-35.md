## Chapter 35: One Hour

> *For in one hour so great riches is come to nought. And every shipmaster, and all the company in ships, and sailors, and as many as trade by sea, stood afar off, and cried when they saw the smoke of her burning.* — Revelation 18:17–18

**Word study: *hour* — ὥρα** Greek *hōra* (Strong's G5610). An hour; a fixed, appointed time; the moment a thing has been waiting for. It is the word Jesus used all through John's gospel: *mine hour is not yet come*, and then, at the end, *the hour is come.* Babylon spent her whole reign sure her hour would never come. It came, and it took an hour.

*In the same hour came forth fingers of a man's hand, and wrote over against the candlestick upon the plaister of the wall of the king's palace… And this is the writing that was written, MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN… MENE; God hath numbered thy kingdom, and finished it… In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain. And Darius the Median took the kingdom… (Daniel 5:5, 25–31)*

\* \* \*

Every day at noon the face came up in the hayloft too, on Nina's laptop, through the back door, asking the world to kneel.

Most of them knelt. It cost nothing. It was a gesture.

The ones who did not kneel were noticed. Nina knew exactly how, because she had written the code. A face that did not lower at noon was flagged. A flag became a visit. A visit became a van.

"Turn it off," Michael said one noon. "You've got the door."

"Not yet," Nina said. "If I kill it now, they rebuild it in a week and they know the door was there. I get one shot." She looked at the face on the screen, asking the world to kneel. "I'll know when."

Ezra had read her to the barns for years, the one John saw riding the beast, arrayed in purple and scarlet, with a golden cup in her hand full of abominations. She was every blessing the Council had ever spoken over the peace, from its first session on the Mount of Olives to its last: the robes, the prayers to everyone and no one, the hand-holding in the kingdom's name by people who stood for nothing in particular and called it unity. She was the red strings knotted at ten thousand wrists, and every pulpit and parliament that had come together and not stood in the truth. And in the week after the image went up, the ten, who had needed her to bless them, found that they no longer did. *And the ten horns which thou sawest upon the beast, these shall hate the whore, and shall make her desolate and naked, and shall eat her flesh, and burn her with fire.* (Revelation 17:16) The Council for Universal Moral Law was dissolved by a single vote. The Olivet Crown burned that night. By Saturday the red-string circles were being turned out of their offices, their accounts closed, and the lists the kingdom had kept on them so carefully, for so long, were read down in the other direction. Rabbi Arcangeli was not seen again. *For God hath put in their hearts to fulfil his will, and to agree, and give their kingdom unto the beast, until the words of God shall be fulfilled.* (Revelation 17:17)

Nobody wept for her but the ones who had never stopped telling her the truth.

\* \* \*

The dragon's time was short, and he knew it, and in the months after the image went up his rage at the saints ran hotter than it ever had.

That was the part the Book had warned them of, and it came exactly as it was written. *Woe to the inhabiters of the earth and of the sea! for the devil is come down unto you, having great wrath, because he knoweth that he hath but a short time.* Vale had the whole world on its knees at noon, and it was not enough for him. It never would be. There was only one people in all the earth who had refused him from the beginning, who had never bowed to the image, never taken the mark, never wept when his prophets died, never cheered when his enemies fell. The hidden ones. The ones who had walked out of his cities between his feet. The ones whose voice came up out of the ground every night at nine o'clock and would not stop.

He came for them with everything he had left.

Not only with rifles. In a cold room beneath Fort Detrick, in a column Nina had once read through her back door, sat the remainder of what had been staged for the caves marked *Empty*: PALEHORSE, Lots 4 through 6, sealed and waiting for a window that would not open. Vale meant to loose it on the hidden the day he found them. He had not found them yet.

The raids in those last weeks were not like the raids before. Before, the Peacekeepers had come for food and bonuses, following tips, working lists. Now they came for blood, in numbers, in the fury of a world going under. Camps that had been hidden for years were found and burned. The front of the hill, the cave where strangers waited until they were known, the great hub of the supply lines, was betrayed at last, by a man who took the mark to save his family and told them where the door in the rock was. Not everyone made the deep door in time, and the ones who did not, Ezra did not let himself think about, except to pray for them by name, every day, on the list.

Michael told what happened under the hill once, in a truck with the engine off, and never again.

"They came at dawn," Michael said. "Sixty men, dogs, lamps. Through the door the traitor showed them. I'd known a month someone would. I gave that man his white stone myself. I wasn't angry. He had a girl of nine. I'd have thought about it too." He watched the windshield. "Three of mine had been bomb techs in the old service. Whole careers spent learning how things fail. For a month they taught the hill how to fail on purpose. In the dark, in the front passages the patrol would walk. Nothing a man would look twice at."

"We close the road, I told them. Not the men. Wait until they're standing in the dome with the sky over their heads, and the way they came is open behind them."

"I counted my people through the deep door and stood in it with the last of them. The first lamps came into the dome. A dog sat down in the middle of the floor and wouldn't go on. The captain walked to the left-hand wall and put his palm flat on the slab, the one that swings, and looked straight at it." He shook his head slowly. "And saw rock."

"I prayed what Elisha prayed," Michael said. "I'm not proud of it. I asked Him to smite them with blindness." *And he smote them with blindness according to the word of Elisha.* (2 Kings 6:18) "He did it gentle. They stood in my house and couldn't see it."

A voice behind him said, very quietly, "Now."

The hill came down behind his people. The sound went through the mountain like a door being shut in the house of God, and Michael, with a stone-white face, said the only verse that came to him. *And the LORD shut him in.* (Genesis 7:16)

The patrol stood in the dome among their lamps and cursed the dust. The way they had come stood open behind them, and they took it, shaken, with a dog that would not stop howling, and the captain wrote one word in his report. *Empty.*

Beyond the fall, the deep halls went on, and most of the people lived there still, in the warm dark, drinking water that did not fail. Michael had come out with the transmitter and with Ezra and a few more, because the voice needed a guard, and because somebody had to walk the long way round and tell the others the road behind them was gone. The deep halls had a second door, on the far side of the ring, that no patrol had ever found.

News of the others reached him in pieces, by runner and by radio, the way it always had. Leon had carried his granddaughter, born in a hidden room the winter after the sky opened, to a farm in Vermont, and she had learned to walk there. Patrice's little ones were thin but whole. Andre and Pruitt ran the northern routes between them now, and Andre had not touched a rifle since Christmas Eve. Mrs. Fairley had kept the dogwood and the stone behind it, and the Harmon girl still asked every week whether Walt had enough paper. Michael said the names of the two men lost at Havre de Grace at every supper, and no one asked him to stop. On New Jersey Avenue the wall of screens had gone dark in the hour Babylon fell, and Kessler, they said, sat in his doorway until the lights went out; of the thirty-one who had watched Trent walk out of that room, a few had come north since with their passes in their pockets, asking for the radio. And a runner from Brookland brought the oldest news last: Lionel Hart's door had been found unlocked and his house empty on the first morning, the viola in its case on the piano and the ram's horn gone from its wall.

Trent Walker was not with them. He had gone north the season before, on foot, with a copy of Nina's card sewn into his coat, from room to room, reading the Absalom papers and Kambria's Meridian filings aloud to anyone who would hear what the Chairman's son had done. Most of the rooms did not need it, he said, but it was true and it was written down, and one who had loaded a face into eleven hundred cameras could carry it. The last word Ezra had, he was in the Adirondacks, walking a company out of a town one day ahead of the vans, with a map in his head and a prayer he was still learning to say.

And through all of it, Ezra kept broadcasting.

Every broadcast was a beacon now, in a kingdom that had nothing left to do but hunt, and everyone in the room knew it. Michael moved the transmitter every night and posted Dorsey on the roof, and never once asked Ezra to stop. He only asked where they would set up next. Michael read the same Book Ezra did, and he knew the road a watchman walks, and he would not be the one to turn him around. Ezra would not stop, because the people in the dark needed it more in the last weeks than they ever had, and because he had been made a watchman, and a watchman does not come down off the wall because the enemy has finally noticed him. Every night at nine, wherever he was, he found a transmitter, and the low line of his son's song went out over the falling kingdom, and then his voice, reading the plans, all the way to the end of the plans now, to the bowls of wrath and the fall of Babylon and the marriage supper of the Lamb and the King on the white horse. He read them the end of the Book, in the end of the world, and he closed every night the way he always had.

*Grace be unto you, and peace.*

He would not have stopped if he could.

On one of the last nights he broadcast, Michael stood at his shoulder in the dark with the ledger open and chose the closing line himself. Ezra read the chapter as he always did, and when he came to the boxed line he slowed and read it twice, the psalm and then the verse with a breath between, as he had read every address since the night Isaac went down the wrong street. *From the end of the earth will I cry unto thee, when my heart is overwhelmed: lead me to the rock that is higher than I.* (Psalm 61:2) To anyone listening in the cities it was a psalm about weariness. To the ones who kept the blue notebook it was a column and a row, and a door in the mountains, and they were to go on foot, by night, to the people who would be waiting there. Michael wrote the hour in the ledger, and nothing else, and Ezra said the benediction.

\* \* \*

Michael sat on an upturned crate in the barn with his father's ledger open on his knee, and he did not write in it.

"Nineteen houses," he said. He kept his eyes on the ledger. "Eleven hundred names. Forty trucks. Every door numbered, every key by the Book." He turned a page and the page was blank. "I counted it all. Everything I counted is worth nothing, Ezra. He'll have it by morning."

"Counting kept them alive."

"Counting kept me from feeling it." He closed the ledger. "My father never lost a drum of water. And he never got to see a thing he built be needed." He shook his head. "I built a city under a city. And in one hour..."

"*In one hour,*" Ezra said.

Michael laughed, a low, wrecked sound. Then he stood up, crossed to the corner where Anton's old cassette deck sat on a feed bin, and thumbed it on, and put the mic in his hand like a man picking up a tool he had put down long ago.

He did not rap. He spoke over the hiss, low and even, so that the loft above the barn could hear.

*They counted bread. They counted breath.*
*I counted names on a wall.*
*A thousand doors, a hundred miles,*
*and Lord, I'd count them all.*
*But the Count is in Your hand,*
*and I was only the pen.*
*You numbered every hair of them,*
*and You numbered my men.*

Up in the loft, Anton's hands had gone still on the board. Months ago he had looped four bars of Jalen's cello off the broadcast under a drum pattern, and played it for no one but himself, late, with the volume low. On the floor beside the cassette deck sat a milk crate of Michael's old discs, the ones he had cut in his father's basement, and the boy was looking down at the crate and then at the man, and his face was the face of someone who has just seen a door in a wall.

"That's a good verse," Ezra said quietly.

"It's the only one I've got left."

\* \* \*

Then a runner came: the barn was known. In that last week they crossed the state line to a burned farmhouse above the Hudson. One night near the end, Ezra broke bread with the brethren.

There were forty of them, in the cellar of a burned farmhouse near the Hudson, the ones who had been with him longest and the ones who had come to him last: Michael, scarred and gray now, with the cross still outside his vest. Nina, who had come up from the coast and not gone back, because she had said she was done running from the thing she helped build and meant to stand with the people who had forgiven her for it. Dorsey. David Levin, the old Jewish neighbor from Kambria's street in Washington, who had stood at his grill the week before the sky opened and told Kambria that a law about faith gave him the creeps, and had found his Messiah at last in the back of a bakery van, reading Isaiah fifty-three by a flashlight propped on a flour sack. Kyle and Sarah, who had answered Ezra from a church basement in Brooklyn on the first night, and had a little girl now, born in a hidden room, who had never once in her life been outside in the daytime.

They had bread, that night. Real bread. Nina had traded the last of what she had for a sack of flour from a farmer who still had some and would not take the mark, and Ezra had baked it himself in the farmhouse oven, and the whole cellar smelled of it, warm, in the dark.

He broke it as the Lord broke it. He had done it a hundred times in a hundred dark rooms, but that night his hands were not quite steady.

"The Lord Jesus the same night in which he was betrayed took bread," Ezra said. "And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me."

He broke the loaf, and it went around the cellar, hand to hand, forty hands in the candlelight, and every one of them tore a piece and passed it on, and the little girl who had never been outside in the daytime tore her piece with small careful fingers and ate it, watching her mother to see how.

They were afraid, all of them. Ezra could see it on every face and did not pretend otherwise.

"I'm afraid too," he said, when the bread had gone all the way around. "I want to say that out loud, so nobody in this room thinks they're the only one, or that being afraid means something's wrong with their faith. I'm afraid. I've been afraid a long time. The morning they were taken, I slept through the trumpet." A few of them almost smiled. "The brave ones in the Book were afraid. David was afraid. Elijah ran into the wilderness and asked God to let him die. The Lord Himself, in the garden, sweat like it was blood and asked if the cup could pass. Being afraid isn't the sin. Bowing is the sin. You can be afraid and not bow. That's the whole of it. That's all He's ever asked of us."

He looked around at them, at the forty faces, at the cross on Michael's vest and the little girl in Sarah's lap.

"We missed the morning," he said. "Every one of us. We had the chance to go up with the ones we loved, without ever tasting any of this, and we missed it, because we didn't believe, or we didn't want to, or we were too busy, or too angry, or too afraid. I slept through it. I know what that is to carry." He waited until the room was his. "But listen to me. He died for us in the dark, before we ever loved Him back. While we were yet sinners, the Book says. He went first. He went to the cross first, before we deserved any of it, and He did it looking at us, as my boy looked at an empty seat and played anyway." His voice caught, and he waited, and went on. "So now we get to show Him. That's what this is. The ones who went up in the morning were spared this. We weren't. So we get to show Him here. At the appointed time, we get to look at Him and say: I love You too. I love You enough to go last. I love You enough to die, as You loved me enough to die first."

The candle burned. Far above them rolled the sound that was always in the sky now, a deep thunder that was not quite thunder.

"*Strengthen ye the weak hands,*" Ezra said quietly, "*and confirm the feeble knees.*" He had said it in the first dark room, the winter after the trumpet, and in a thousand since. "*Say to them that are of a fearful heart, Be strong, fear not: behold, your God will come.*"

"*Behold, your God will come,*" the room said back to him, forty voices, as they had learned to, and the little girl said it too, in her high small voice, the words she had known her whole short hidden life.

It was Dorsey who started it, of all of them. Dorsey, who had not smiled since the hill came down, sat on an overturned bucket with his big hands hanging between his knees, and said into the quiet, to nobody, "He's still good, though."

Nobody answered. Then Ezra said, "Yes, He is," and Dorsey looked up, startled, as if he had not known he'd spoken aloud.

"Say it again," Ezra said.

"He's still good."

"Again."

"He's still good." Dorsey's voice broke in the middle, and something let go in his face. "He's still good. Lord, You are still good."

Ezra found he was on his feet. He did not remember standing. "*Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name. Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits.* (Psalm 103:1–2) Forget not. Not one. Let's count them, right now, out loud. I'll go first." His voice cracked, and he laughed at it. "Thank You for the bread."

"Thank You for the bread," the cellar said.

"The roof," Kyle called from the corner, looking up at the black rafters and the stars between them. "Such as it is."

"For the roof, such as it is!" And the cellar laughed, all forty of them, the first real laughter that room had held, low and helpless, hands over mouths so the sound would not carry, and failing.

They could not stop then. It went around the cellar as the bread had, hand to hand, and nobody could have said where one person's gladness left off and the next began. Sarah drew her daughter close. "Thank You for her heartbeat," she said. "I can feel it against my ribs right now."

Nina stood, wet-faced. "Thank You for forgiving me," she said. "I built the thing. I didn't earn this. You gave it to me anyway." Dorsey said *amen* so hard the candle leaned.

David Levin thanked Him for the Messiah he had waited for all his life and found at last in the back of a bakery van, and the cellar howled. "Of all the places," David said, spreading his hands, delighted. "The God of Abraham, and He sends me to a bakery van. He has a sense of humor, this God, I tell you."

Michael let a mile go by, and then said quietly, "For every man who got off that mountain alive. And for the ones who didn't, that You have them."

Nobody laughed at that. Everybody said amen.

And then the little girl, who had never been outside in the daytime, who knew the world only as a hidden room and her mother's lap, stood up on her mother's knees and said in her high small voice, "Thank You for everybody."

The whole cellar broke.

David Levin began to sing. He sang it in Hebrew, softly at first, the old tune to the old words, *Bar'chi nafshi et Adonai*, bless the LORD, O my soul, as his fathers had sung it for three thousand years. The Gentiles caught the tune before they knew the words, and then the words. Dorsey was clapping softly on his knees, a muffled heartbeat. Kyle swayed. Nina had Michael by the arm, and Michael, scarred and gray Michael, was laughing out loud with his head thrown back like a young man.

Ezra stood in the middle of it and had not known he could feel this. Not here. Not at the end. He told them about Paul and Silas, because he could not help it: the inner prison at Philippi, their backs laid open with rods, their feet fast in the stocks. "*And at midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God: and the prisoners heard them.* (Acts 16:25) Nobody told the prisoners to listen. They listened because it was the last thing in that prison anyone expected to hear. So let them hear us. *The joy of the LORD is your strength.* (Nehemiah 8:10) Not our joy. His. And He has plenty to spare tonight."

They sang it through three times. In the morning every one of them knew the boots would come down the stairs. It was the brightest room Ezra had ever stood in.

When the song finally burned down, he asked them to sit, and they sat, flushed and breathing hard, some of them still laughing under their breath. The candle was low. Ezra stood with his hands hanging empty at his sides, and looked at them, and found for a moment that he could not begin.

"I want to tell you something," he said. "And then I want to sing the rest of my life."

They waited.

"I came into all of this carrying a pack. You couldn't see it. Most days I couldn't see it myself. *Let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us.* (Hebrews 12:1) Every weight. I read that verse for years the way a man reads a speed limit." He took a breath. "I was carrying stones. A little boy holding out his hand at a bus station, and a father who patted his head and turned around. A wife who reached for me every night for twelve years, and a hand I did not always take. A trumpet I slept through. A boy who played to an empty chair, and a little girl's bed with the covers thrown back. I carried every one of them so long I thought they were part of my body. I thought they were what held me up."

The candle moved in some draft none of them could feel.

"They were pulling me under. And tonight, standing here laughing like a fool in a cellar with all of you, I put my hand in my pocket to check, the way you do, and they were gone." His voice finally broke, all the way. "Every one. I went looking for that bus station, and I could not find it. He took it. He took all of it at the cross, before I was ever born, and I have been walking around for years carrying it for Him as if I'd been paid to." He was laughing and crying at once. "Jesus said it. *If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.* (John 8:36) Free indeed. Not brave. You know I'm not brave. Free."

He did not wipe his face. He lifted both his hands, open, palms up, the way a woman he loved had held hers out across a table for twelve years, and held them there in the candlelight where all forty of them could see that they were empty.

"I am going to walk out of here in the morning with nothing in my hands," Ezra said. "Wherever they take me. I have nothing left to carry. And I have never in my life been this light."

Nobody spoke. Then Michael stood, and then Nina, and then all of them, until the whole cellar was on its feet with their hands lifted and open in the light of one low candle, and the little girl on her mother's shoulder lifted both of hers too, not knowing why, with her fingers spread, laughing.

Then, when they could, they sang the last song of the night, low, as they always sang, so the sound would not carry: the forty-sixth psalm, all eleven verses, the one Ezra's mother used to hum over the dishes. They sang it in the cellar of a burned farmhouse, forty hungry people around a candle and a torn loaf, while Vale's kingdom fell to pieces over their heads, and when they came to the last verse, Ezra led it, and his voice did not shake at all.

*The LORD of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge. Selah.*

Nina was the one who found it.

She had not slept. When the singing ended and the others lay down along the cellar walls in their coats, she sat on an upturned crate at the foot of the stairs with the old laptop on her knees, wired to the last good battery, and read the logs the way she had read them every night since the sky opened. Near four she stopped scrolling. She read the same lines again. Then she closed the lid very gently, went and knelt beside Michael, and said it into his ear.

"It was never the notebooks."

Michael was awake. He was always awake. "Say it plain."

"They never broke the books. They never needed to." Her voice was flat and low. "Every night the carrier goes up from a new place for eight minutes, and every night the nearest receiver logs a signal and a clock. They stopped trying to read us. They drew a circle around each night and waited. Years of circles, Michael. Every night since we came here, every one of them crosses here."

Ezra heard it from where he sat against the wall, with the cold of the stone coming through his coat. He did not ask her to say it again.

"I knew the radio would be how they found us," he said.

Nina turned. "You never told me."

"I told Him."

Then he got up off the floor and crossed the cellar to her, and sat down on the crate beside hers, and said, very low, "Come with me a minute."

He took her to the back of the cellar, past the people lying along the walls in their coats, to the shelves where the farmhouse jars had stood. He put his hand flat on the boards beside the shelves and pushed, and a section of the wall swung in on a hinge she had never seen, onto a space the size of a closet. There was a crate in it, and a blanket, and a jug of water.

"I built it Tuesday," he said. "Nobody knows but Michael."

She understood before he said anything else, and she shook her head.

"No," she said. "Ezra. I told them I was done running. I'm standing with you."

"Standing with us is staying." He kept his voice down. "They'll count heads in the morning, and they won't count you. Stay. Do what you can, with what you've got, for whoever's left. You'll know what that is better than I will."

She stood with her hand on the hinge and did not say anything for a long time.

He went back to the shelf and took Kambria's lamp down in its towel and put it in her hands.

"Keep it lit," he said.

The cellar door overhead gave one soft knock, then two. Kyle came down the stairs with his hand flat on the wall and his boots in his other hand, and he did not whisper, because there was no longer anyone to hide the sound from. "Grace and peace," he said, and did not wait for the answer. "Lights on the river road. Both ends. No headlamps, just the running lamps, and a helicopter somewhere high up with its own lights out. I counted nineteen trucks before I quit counting."

Around the cellar people were sitting up. A child coughed and was hushed. Somebody found a shoe in the dark.

Michael stood. "The tunnel comes out in the orchard," he said. "We have six minutes before the trees are lit. Anyone who can run, runs now, in twos, and no one in this room will think less of you for it. I mean that." He said it to the room, not to Ezra.

Nobody moved.

Sarah drew her daughter back against her chest and put her chin on the top of the child's head. "I have run for years," she said. "I'd like to see the end of it with my eyes open."

David Levin laid his hand on the nearest wall as if it were a door post. Dorsey did not stand, and did not speak, and did not take his eyes off Ezra.

Ezra thought of a man standing before a governor and saying nothing at all. *He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.* (Isaiah 53:7) He had read that verse into a microphone a hundred times, to the barns, as a promise about Someone else.

"If I go out the back," he said, "the carrier goes with me, and they follow it to whoever is next. Let it stop in this cellar. Let it stop here."

Michael had not moved either. He stood at the mouth of the tunnel with the rifle on its sling and his eyes on Ezra, and Ezra could see that he meant to stand there until the boots came down.

Ezra crossed the cellar to him. "Not you."

"Don't."

"There's a hill full of people behind that fall. Andre and Pruitt are running the north with nobody over them. Seven stations on that map, and one man alive who knows what all the numbers mean." He kept his voice under the sound of the room waking. "Tomorrow night they'll be sitting in the dark with the radio off, waiting to hear who's coming. It has to be you."

"I'm not leaving you in a hole."

"You're not leaving me. You're going to them." Ezra laid his palm flat on the cross outside Michael's vest. "When David wanted to march out with his men against Absalom, the people wouldn't let him go. *…but now thou art worth ten thousand of us: therefore now it is better that thou succour us out of the city.*" (2 Samuel 18:3)

Michael's jaw worked under the beard. He looked past Ezra at the room, at Sarah with her chin on her daughter's head, at Dorsey on the floor, at the shelves where a wall swung in that only the two of them knew about. Then he unslung the rifle and stood it against the stone by the tunnel mouth, and took his hand off it.

"*He that leadeth into captivity shall go into captivity,*" he said, low, to the Book he had lived by. "*He that killeth with the sword must be killed with the sword. Here is the patience and the faith of the saints.*" (Revelation 13:10)

From the pocket where he had carried it all this time, he took out a smooth white stone and closed his fist around it.

"Grace and peace, brother."

"And unto you," Ezra said.

Michael bent into the tunnel and was gone. Ezra stood at its mouth a moment longer and listened, and heard nothing, which was what Michael had taught him to listen for.

Outside, a truck door shut, very carefully.

"*As it is written,*" Ezra said, quietly enough that the people at the back leaned forward to hear it, "*For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter.*" (Romans 8:36)

It was Dorsey who answered, in a voice that came up from the floor of him. "*Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us.*" (Romans 8:37)

Someone took Ezra's hand, and he did not see whose. The candle had burned down to a thumb of wax. Behind the shelves, behind a wall nobody else in the room knew was there, Kambria's lamp was still burning low. Thirty-eight people stood up in the dark of a burned farmhouse cellar in their coats and their stocking feet, and nobody reached for the tunnel, and nobody wept aloud. They stood with their hands joined and listened to the gravel above them take the weight of boots.

They came for them at dawn.

\* \* \*

They did not fight.

Dorsey could have. He was Michael's man to the bone, and the rifle stood against the wall by the tunnel mouth where Michael had left it, and for a moment, in the gray light, with the boots coming down the farmhouse stairs, Ezra saw him look at it.

Then Dorsey looked at Ezra, and Ezra shook his head, once.

Dorsey stepped away from the wall and put his hands where the Peacekeepers could see them, and stood beside Ezra, and Ezra gripped his shoulder. They waited together as the soldiers came down into the room with their lights and their rifles and found thirty-eight people standing around a guttered candle and a loaf of bread, singing, with their hands open and empty and raised, and not one of them afraid enough to bow. Nobody looked at the tunnel. The soldiers never found it.

They took them all. Ezra, and Dorsey, and David Levin, and Kyle and Sarah and their little girl who had never been outside in the daytime, every one of the thirty-eight, up out of the cellar into the gray dawn of the last days, into the trucks.

And when the lead officer, a young man with a tired hard face, demanded to know which one of them was the one called Ezra, the voice from the ground, the one Vale himself had put at the very top of the list, Ezra did not make them guess.

"I am," he said. "It's just Ezra. I come in peace."

Before they put him in the first truck, he asked the young officer for one minute on the cellar stairs, and the officer, who could not have said why, gave it to him. Ezra took Kambria's lamp down from the shelf, carried it up into the gray light, and set it in the kitchen window of the farmhouse where anyone coming after could see it. Then he held out his wrists. As the truck pulled away, through the slats, he could see the farmhouse behind them, and the small gold light in its window, and the sky over it, and in the sky, high in the east, where the sun was coming up, the clouds had begun to move as he had never seen clouds move, and he did not yet know what it meant, and he was not afraid.
