## Chapter 35: One Hour

> *For in one hour so great riches is come to nought. And every shipmaster, and all the company in ships, and sailors, and as many as trade by sea, stood afar off, and cried when they saw the smoke of her burning.* — Revelation 18:17–18

**Word study: *hour* — ὥρα** Greek *hōra* (Strong's G5610). An hour; a fixed, appointed time; the moment a thing has been waiting for. It is the word Jesus used all through John's gospel: *mine hour is not yet come*, and then, at the end, *the hour is come.* Babylon spent her whole reign sure her hour would never come. It came, and it took an hour.

*In the same hour came forth fingers of a man's hand, and wrote over against the candlestick upon the plaister of the wall of the king's palace… And this is the writing that was written, MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN… MENE; God hath numbered thy kingdom, and finished it… In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain. And Darius the Median took the kingdom… (Daniel 5:5, 25–31)*

\* \* \*

The end of the world came on a Tuesday, and it took an hour, and Ezra watched it on a dying phone wired to a car battery in a barn in Connecticut.

It was the season the Book had warned of. The covenant with the nations had bought the peace and built the temple and set up the image and marked the world. Ezra had Walt's math in the green ledgers, and Kambria's in her notebooks, and Daniel's seventy weeks underneath all of it: one week, split in the middle by a wound and an image, and now the Book said what came after the middle.

It had begun the week before, with the woman.

Ezra had read her to the barns for years, the one John saw riding the beast, arrayed in purple and scarlet, with a golden cup in her hand full of abominations. She was every blessing the old Council had ever spoken over the peace, and the Congress that had birthed it: the robes, the prayers to everyone and no one, the hand-holding in the kingdom's name by people who stood for nothing in particular and called it unity. She was the red strings knotted at ten thousand wrists, and every pulpit and parliament that had come together and not stood in the truth. And on a Thursday in those last days, the ten, who had needed her to bless them, found that they no longer did. *And the ten horns which thou sawest upon the beast, these shall hate the whore, and shall make her desolate and naked, and shall eat her flesh, and burn her with fire.* (Revelation 17:16) The Council for Universal Moral Law was dissolved by a single vote. The Seven Arches burned that night. By Saturday the red-string circles were being turned out of their offices, their accounts closed, and the lists the kingdom had kept on them so carefully, for so long, were read down in the other direction. Rabbi Arcangeli was not seen again. *For God hath put in their hearts to fulfil his will, and to agree, and give their kingdom unto the beast, until the words of God shall be fulfilled.* (Revelation 17:17)

Nobody wept for her but the ones who had never stopped telling her the truth.

He had not meant to be watching a screen. He had spent years teaching people to look away from screens. But Nina had come up from the coast two days before with word that something was happening in the markets, something she could not explain and could not look away from, and she had rigged the old phone to a battery and found a feed, and on the Tuesday morning, in the barn, with a dozen of them gathered around it in their coats, they watched Babylon fall.

It fell as Nina had always been afraid it would, from the inside, through the thing she had helped build. The whole world ran on one account now. One ledger, Crowe had called it, before he died; one world, one account, the mark on every hand that could buy or sell. Vale had spent years making the world's money into a single system, with the image at the center of it, the face that knew every name. And on the Tuesday morning, in one hour, the single system came apart.

No one ever agreed on how it started. Some said a city burned first, a great port city, and the smoke of it brought the markets down. Some said the markets came down first and the fire came after. Nina, watching the numbers scroll in the dark barn, said it did not matter which came first, because they were the same thing: a kingdom that had made itself into one single machine, one account, one ledger, one face, and when God reached out and touched one part of it, all of it fell at once, because they had left themselves no other way to live. They had built one door on purpose, so that no one could leave by another, and now there was no other.

In one hour, the richest kingdom in the history of the earth became worth nothing at all.

On the screen, the great city burned. The merchants of the earth stood far off and wept, because no man bought their merchandise anymore: the gold and silver and precious stones, the fine linen and purple and silk, the cinnamon and wine and oil, the cattle and the sheep, and, at the very end of John's long list, the one that Ezra read aloud to the dark barn in a voice that shook, *and slaves, and souls of men.* All of it worth nothing, in an hour. The warm bright markets dark. The screens on the corners dead. The face gone out of all of them at once.

And at the end of the cargo, where John slowed down, a line Ezra read twice before he could say it. *…for by thy sorceries were all nations deceived.* (Revelation 18:23) The Greek was *pharmakeia*, the root of the word on every pharmacy door: the drug, the potion, the poison given as a cure. He thought of a protocol that was free to the registered. He thought of a lot number on a cell wall. The merchants of Babylon had sold it as medicine.

"*Alas, alas, that great city,*" Ezra read, from Angela's Bible, over the burning on the screen, "*that was clothed in fine linen, and purple, and scarlet, and decked with gold, and precious stones, and pearls! For in one hour so great riches is come to nought.*"

Nina reached over and turned the phone off.

"Don't watch the rest," she said. Her face was wet. "I've seen what's in the rest. Don't watch it."

\* \* \*

The dragon knew his time was short, and he turned his rage on the saints.

That was the part the Book had warned them of, and it came exactly as it was written. *Woe to the inhabiters of the earth and of the sea! for the devil is come down unto you, having great wrath, because he knoweth that he hath but a short time.* Vale's kingdom was falling, and Vale knew it, and a falling kingdom with the devil in it does not go quietly into the dark. It looks for someone to take down with it. And there was only one people in all the earth who had refused him from the beginning, who had never bowed to the image, never taken the mark, never wept when his prophets died, never cheered when his enemies fell. The hidden ones. The ones who had walked out of his cities between his feet. The ones whose voice came up out of the ground every night at nine o'clock and would not stop.

He came for them with everything he had left.

Not only with rifles. In a cold room beneath Fort Detrick, in a column Nina had once read in a vault, sat the remainder of what had been staged for the caves marked *Empty*: PALEHORSE, Lots 4 through 6, sealed and waiting for a window that would not open. Vale had meant to loose it on the hidden before the end. He ran out of time, as men who build a kingdom on the clock always do.

The raids in those last weeks were not like the raids before. Before, the Peacekeepers had come for food and bonuses, following tips, working lists. Now they came for blood, in numbers, in the fury of a world going under. Camps that had been hidden for years were found and burned. The front of the hill, the cave where strangers waited until they were known, the great hub of the supply lines, was betrayed at last, by a man who took the mark to save his family and told them where the door in the rock was. Not everyone made the deep door in time, and the ones who did not, Ezra did not let himself think about, except to pray for them by name, every day, on the list.

Michael told what happened under the hill once, in a truck with the engine off, and never again.

"They came at dawn," Michael said. "Sixty men, dogs, lamps. Through the door the traitor showed them. I'd known a month someone would. I gave that man his white stone myself. I wasn't angry. He had a girl of nine. I'd have thought about it too." He watched the windshield. "Three of mine had been bomb techs in the old service. Whole careers spent learning how things fail. For a month they taught the hill how to fail on purpose. In the dark, in the front passages the patrol would walk. Nothing a man would look twice at."

"We close the road," he told them. "Not the men. Wait until they're standing in the dome with the sky over their heads, and the way they came is open behind them."

"I counted my people through the deep door and stood in it with the last of them. The first lamps came into the dome. A dog sat down in the middle of the floor and wouldn't go on. The captain walked to the left-hand wall and put his palm flat on the slab, the one that swings, and looked straight at it." He swallowed. "And saw rock."

"I prayed what Elisha prayed," Michael said. "I'm not proud of it. I asked Him to smite them with blindness." *And he smote them with blindness according to the word of Elisha.* (2 Kings 6:18) "He did it gentle. They stood in my house and couldn't see it."

A voice behind him said, very quietly, "Now."

The hill came down behind his people. The sound went through the mountain like a door being shut in the house of God, and Michael, with a stone-white face, said the only verse that came to him. *And the LORD shut him in.* (Genesis 7:16)

The patrol stood in the dome among their lamps and cursed the dust. The way they had come stood open behind them, and they took it, shaken, with a dog that would not stop howling, and the captain wrote one word in his report. *Empty.*

Beyond the fall, the deep halls went on, and most of the people lived there still, in the warm dark, drinking water that did not fail. Michael had come out with the transmitter and with Ezra and a few more, because the voice needed a guard, and because somebody had to walk the long way round and tell the others the road behind them was gone. The deep halls had a second door, on the far side of the ring, that no patrol had ever found.

News of the others reached him in pieces, by runner and by radio, the way it always had. Leon had carried his granddaughter to a farm in Vermont, and she had learned to laugh again. Patrice's little ones were thin but whole. Andre and Pruitt ran the northern routes between them now, and Andre had not touched a rifle since Christmas Eve. Mrs. Fairley had kept the dogwood and the stone behind it, and the Harmon girl still asked every week whether Walt had enough paper. Michael said the names of the two men lost at Havre de Grace at every supper, and no one asked him to stop. On New Jersey Avenue the wall of screens had gone dark in the hour Babylon fell, and Kessler, they said, sat in his doorway until the lights went out; of the thirty-one who had watched Trent walk out of that room, a few had come north since with their passes in their pockets, asking for the radio. Lionel Hart's door had been found unlocked and his house empty on the first morning, the viola in its case on the piano and the ram's horn gone from its wall. So the old man had gone up with the rest, Ezra thought, and whatever Angela had asked of him at her bed, he was keeping it where she was.

Trent Walker was not with them. He had gone north the season before, on foot, with a copy of Nina's card sewn into his coat, from room to room, reading the Absalom papers and Kambria's Meridian filings aloud to anyone who would hear what the Chairman's son had done. Most of the rooms did not need it, he said, but it was true and it was written down, and one who had loaded a face into eleven hundred cameras could carry it. The last word Ezra had, he was in the Adirondacks, walking a company out of a town one day ahead of the vans, with a map in his head and a prayer he was still learning to say.

And through all of it, Ezra kept broadcasting.

Every broadcast was a beacon now, in a kingdom that had nothing left to do but hunt, and everyone in the room knew it. Michael moved the transmitter every night and posted Dorsey on the roof, and never once asked Ezra to stop. He only asked where they would set up next. Michael read the same Book Ezra did, and he knew the road a watchman walks, and he would not be the one to turn him around. Ezra would not stop, because the people in the dark needed it more in the last weeks than they ever had, and because he had been made a watchman, and a watchman does not come down off the wall because the enemy has finally noticed him. Every night at nine, wherever he was, he found a transmitter, and the low line of his son's song went out over the falling kingdom, and then his voice, reading the plans, all the way to the end of the plans now, to the bowls of wrath and the fall of Babylon and the marriage supper of the Lamb and the King on the white horse. He read them the end of the Book, in the end of the world, and he closed every night the way he always had.

*Grace and peace be unto you.*

He would not have stopped if he could. He had spent too much of his life being silent while his wife held out her hand.

On one of the last nights he broadcast, Michael stood at his shoulder in the dark with the ledger open and chose the closing line himself. Ezra read the chapter as he always did, and when he came to the boxed line he slowed and read it twice, the psalm and then the verse with a breath between, as he had read every address since the night Isaac went down the wrong street. *From the end of the earth will I cry unto thee, when my heart is overwhelmed: lead me to the rock that is higher than I.* (Psalm 61:2) To anyone listening in the cities it was a psalm about weariness. To the ones who kept the blue notebook it was a column and a row, and a door in the mountains, and they were to go on foot, by night, to the people who would be waiting there. Michael wrote the hour in the ledger, and nothing else, and Ezra said the benediction.

\* \* \*

Michael sat on an upturned crate in the barn with his father's ledger open on his knee, and he did not write in it.

"Nineteen houses," he said. He did not look up. "Eleven hundred names. Forty trucks. Every door numbered, every key by the Book." He turned a page and the page was blank. "I counted it all. Everything I counted is worth nothing, Ezra. He'll have it by morning."

"Counting kept them alive."

"Counting kept me from feeling it." He closed the ledger. "My father never lost a drum of water. And he never got to see a thing he built be needed." He shook his head. "I built a city under a city. And in one hour..."

"*In one hour,*" Ezra said.

Michael laughed, a low, wrecked sound. Then he stood up, crossed to the corner where Anton's old cassette deck sat on a feed bin, and thumbed it on, and put the mic in his hand like a man picking up a tool he had put down long ago.

He spoke over the hiss, not rapped. Spoken, low and even, so that the loft above the barn could hear.

*They counted bread. They counted breath.*
*I counted names on a wall.*
*A thousand doors, a hundred miles,*
*and Lord, I'd count them all.*
*But the Count is in Your hand,*
*and I was only the pen.*
*You numbered every hair of them,*
*and You numbered my men.*

Anton, in the loft, had stopped breathing. Months ago he had looped four bars of Jalen's cello off the broadcast under a drum pattern, and played it for no one but himself, late, with the volume low. On the floor beside the cassette deck sat a milk crate of Michael's old discs, the ones he had cut in his father's basement, and the boy was looking down at the crate and then at the man, and his face was the face of someone who has just seen a door in a wall.

"That's a good verse," Ezra said quietly.

"It's the only one I've got left."

\* \* \*

A runner found them the barn, and in that last week they crossed the state line to a burned farmhouse above the Hudson. One night near the end, Ezra broke bread with the brethren.

There were forty of them, in the cellar of a burned farmhouse near the Hudson, the ones who had been with him longest and the ones who had come to him last: Michael, scarred and gray now, with the cross still outside his vest. Nina, who had come up from the coast and not gone back, because she had said she was done running from the thing she helped build and meant to stand with the people who had forgiven her for it. Dorsey. David Levin, the old Jewish neighbor from Kambria's street in Washington, who had read Zechariah eight at his own fence the summer before the sky opened, *we have heard that God is with you*, and had found his Messiah at last in the back of a bakery van, and had become the one who taught them all the Law and the Prophets, so that the Gentiles among them finally understood the roots they had been grafted into. Kyle and Sarah, who had answered Ezra from a church basement in Brooklyn on the first night, and had a little girl now, born in a hidden room, who had never once in her life been outside in the daytime.

They had bread, that night. Real bread. Nina had traded the last of what she had for a sack of flour from a farmer who still had some and would not take the mark, and Ezra had baked it himself in the farmhouse oven, and the whole cellar smelled of it, warm, in the dark.

He broke it as the Lord broke it. He had done it a hundred times in a hundred dark rooms, but that night his hands were not quite steady.

"The Lord Jesus the same night in which he was betrayed took bread," Ezra said. "And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me."

He broke the loaf, and it went around the cellar, hand to hand, forty hands in the candlelight, and every one of them tore a piece and passed it on, and the little girl who had never been outside in the daytime tore her piece with small careful fingers and ate it, watching her mother to see how.

They were afraid, all of them. Ezra could see it on every face and did not pretend otherwise.

"I'm afraid too," he said, when the bread had gone all the way around. "I want to say that out loud, so nobody in this room thinks they're the only one, or that being afraid means something's wrong with their faith. I'm afraid. I've been afraid a long time. The night they took the children, I was so afraid I slept through the trumpet rather than face it." A few of them almost smiled. "The brave ones in the Book were afraid. David was afraid. Elijah ran into the wilderness and asked God to let him die. The Lord Himself, in the garden, sweat like it was blood and asked if the cup could pass. Being afraid isn't the sin. Bowing is the sin. You can be afraid and not bow. That's the whole of it. That's all He's ever asked of us."

He looked around at them, at the forty faces, at the cross on Michael's vest and the little girl in Sarah's lap.

"We missed the morning," he said. "Every one of us. We had the chance to go up with the ones we loved, without ever tasting any of this, and we missed it, because we didn't believe, or we didn't want to, or we were too busy, or too angry, or too afraid. I slept through it. I know what that is to carry." He swallowed. "But listen to me. He died for us in the dark, before we ever loved Him back. While we were yet sinners, the Book says. He went first. He went to the cross first, before we deserved any of it, and He did it looking at us, as my boy looked at an empty seat and played anyway." His voice caught, and he waited, and went on. "So now we get to show Him. That's what this is. That's the one thing the ones who went up in the morning never got to do. They never got to prove it. We do. At the appointed time, we get to look at Him and say: I love You too. I love You enough to go last. I love You enough to die, as You loved me enough to die first."

The candle burned. Far above them rolled the sound that was always in the sky now, a deep thunder that was not quite thunder.

"*Strengthen ye the weak hands,*" Ezra said quietly, "*and confirm the feeble knees.*" He had said it in the first dark room, the winter after the trumpet, and in a thousand since. "*Say to them that are of a fearful heart, Be strong, fear not: behold, your God will come.*"

"*Behold, your God will come,*" the room said back to him, forty voices, as they had learned to, and the little girl said it too, in her high small voice, the words she had known her whole short hidden life.

It was Dorsey who started it, of all of them. Dorsey, who had not smiled since the hill came down, sat on an overturned bucket with his big hands hanging between his knees, and said into the quiet, to nobody, "He's still good, though."

Nobody answered. Then Ezra said, "Yes, He is," and Dorsey looked up, startled, as if he had not known he'd spoken aloud.

"Say it again," Ezra said.

"He's still good."

"Again."

"He's still good." Dorsey's voice broke in the middle, and something let go in his face. "He's still good. Lord, You are still good."

Ezra found he was on his feet. He did not remember standing. "*Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name. Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits.* (Psalm 103:1–2) Forget not. Not one. Let's count them, right now, out loud. I'll go first." His voice cracked, and he laughed at it. "Thank You for the bread."

"Thank You for the bread," the cellar said.

"The roof," Kyle called from the corner, looking up at the black rafters and the stars between them. "Such as it is."

"For the roof, such as it is!" And the cellar laughed, all forty of them, the first real laughter that room had held, low and helpless, hands over mouths so the sound would not carry, and failing.

They could not stop then. It went around the cellar as the bread had, hand to hand, and nobody could have said where one person's gladness left off and the next began. Sarah drew her daughter close. "Thank You for her heartbeat," she said. "I can feel it against my ribs right now."

Nina stood, wet-faced. "Thank You for forgiving me," she said. "I built the thing. I didn't earn this. You gave it to me anyway." Dorsey said *amen* so hard the candle leaned.

David Levin thanked Him for the Messiah he had waited for all his life and found at last in the back of a bakery van, and the cellar howled. "Of all the places," David said, spreading his hands, delighted. "The God of Abraham, and He sends me to a bakery van. He has a sense of humor, this God, I tell you."

Michael said nothing for a long time, and then said quietly, "For every man who got out of Connecticut alive. And for the ones who didn't, that You have them."

Nobody laughed at that. Everybody said amen.

And then the little girl, who had never been outside in the daytime, who knew the world only as a hidden room and her mother's lap, stood up on her mother's knees and said in her high small voice, "Thank You for everybody."

The whole cellar broke. People who had not wept in years wept, and people who had been weeping for years laughed.

David Levin began to sing. He sang it in Hebrew, softly at first, the old tune to the old words, *Bar'chi nafshi et Adonai*, bless the LORD, O my soul, as his fathers had sung it for three thousand years. The Gentiles caught the tune before they knew the words, and then the words. Dorsey was clapping softly on his knees, a muffled heartbeat. Kyle swayed. Nina had Michael by the arm, and Michael, scarred and gray Michael, was laughing out loud with his head thrown back like a young man.

Ezra stood in the middle of it and had not known he could feel this. Not here. Not at the end. He told them about Paul and Silas, because he could not help it: the inner prison at Philippi, their backs laid open with rods, their feet fast in the stocks. "*And at midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God: and the prisoners heard them.* (Acts 16:25) Nobody told the prisoners to listen. They listened because it was the last thing in that prison anyone expected to hear. So let them hear us. *The joy of the LORD is your strength.* (Nehemiah 8:10) Not our strength. His joy. And He has plenty to spare tonight."

They sang it through three times, and the third time there was no one in that cellar for whom it was anything but true. It should have been the saddest room in the country. The raids were closing in, the kingdom of the beast was breaking up over their heads, and in the morning every one of them knew the boots would come down the stairs. It was the brightest room Ezra had ever stood in. The darkness had not gone anywhere. It was simply, for the first time, outnumbered.

When the song finally burned down, he asked them to sit, and they sat, flushed and breathing hard, some of them still laughing under their breath. The candle was low. Ezra stood with his hands hanging empty at his sides, and looked at them, and found for a moment that he could not begin.

"I want to tell you something," he said. "And then I want to sing the rest of my life."

They waited.

"I came into all of this carrying a pack. You couldn't see it. Most days I couldn't see it myself. *Let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us.* (Hebrews 12:1) Every weight. I read that verse for years the way a man reads a speed limit." He took a breath. "I was carrying stones. A little boy holding out his hand at a bus station, and a father who patted his head and turned around. A wife who reached for me every night for twenty years, and a hand I did not always take. A trumpet I slept through. A boy who played to an empty chair, and a little girl's bed with the covers thrown back. I carried every one of them so long I thought they were part of my body. I thought they were what held me up."

The candle moved in some draft none of them could feel.

"They were pulling me under. And tonight, standing here laughing like a fool in a cellar with all of you, I put my hand in my pocket to check, the way you do, and they were gone." His voice finally broke, all the way. "Every one. I went looking for that bus station, and I could not find it. He took it. He took all of it at the cross, before I was ever born, and I have been walking around for years carrying it for Him as if I'd been paid to." He was laughing and crying at once. "*If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed.* (John 8:36) Free indeed. Not brave. You know I'm not brave. Free."

He did not wipe his face. He lifted both his hands, open, palms up, the way a woman he loved had held hers out across a table for twenty years, and held them there in the candlelight where all forty of them could see that they were empty.

"I am going to walk out of here in the morning with nothing in my hands," Ezra said. "Wherever they take me. I have nothing left to carry. And I have never in my life been this light."

Nobody spoke. Then Michael stood, and then Nina, and then all of them, until the whole cellar was on its feet with their hands lifted and open in the light of one low candle, and the little girl on her mother's shoulder lifted both of hers too, not knowing why, with her fingers spread, laughing.

Then, when they could, they sang the last song of the night, low, as they always sang, so the sound would not carry: the forty-sixth psalm, all eleven verses, the one Ezra's mother used to hum over the dishes. They sang it in the cellar of a burned farmhouse, forty hungry people around a candle and a torn loaf, while the kingdom of the beast fell to pieces over their heads, and when they came to the last verse, Ezra led it, and his voice did not shake at all.

*The LORD of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge. Selah.*

Nina was the one who found it.

She had not slept. When the singing ended and the others lay down along the cellar walls in their coats, she sat on an upturned crate at the foot of the stairs with the old laptop on her knees, wired to the last good battery, and read the logs the way she had read them every night since the sky opened. At four in the morning she stopped scrolling. She read the same lines again. Then she closed the lid very gently, went and knelt beside Michael, and said it into his ear.

"It was never the notebooks."

Michael was awake. He was always awake. "Say it plain."

"They never broke the books. They never needed to." Her voice was flat and low. "Every night the carrier goes up from a new place for eleven minutes, and every night the nearest receiver logs a signal and a clock. They stopped trying to read us. They drew a circle around each night and waited. Years of circles, Michael. The last forty nights, every one of them crosses here."

Ezra heard it from where he sat against the wall, with the cold of the stone coming through his coat. He did not ask her to say it again.

"I knew it would," he said.

Nina turned. "You never told me."

"I told Him."

The cellar door overhead gave one soft knock, then two. Kyle came down the stairs with his hand flat on the wall and his boots in his other hand, and he did not whisper, because there was no longer anyone to hide the sound from. "Lights on the river road. Both ends. No headlamps, just the running lamps, and a helicopter somewhere high up with its own lights out. I counted eleven trucks before I quit counting."

Around the cellar people were sitting up. A child coughed and was hushed. Somebody found a shoe in the dark.

Michael stood. "The tunnel comes out in the orchard," he said. "We have six minutes before the trees are lit. Anyone who can run, runs now, in twos, and no one in this room will think less of you for it. I mean that." He did not look at Ezra when he said it.

Nobody moved.

Sarah drew her daughter back against her chest and put her chin on the top of the child's head. "I have run for years," she said. "I'd like to see the end of it with my eyes open."

David Levin laid his hand on the nearest wall as if it were a door post. Dorsey did not stand, and did not speak, and did not take his eyes off Ezra.

Ezra thought of a man standing before a governor and saying nothing at all. *He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.* (Isaiah 53:7) He had read that verse into a microphone a hundred times, to the barns, as a promise about Someone else.

"If I go out the back," he said, "the carrier goes with me, and they follow it to whoever is next. Let it stop in this cellar. Let it stop here."

Outside, a truck door shut, very carefully.

"*As it is written,*" Ezra said, quietly enough that the people at the back leaned forward to hear it, "*For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter.*" (Romans 8:36)

It was Dorsey who answered, in a voice that came up from the floor of him. "*Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us.*" (Romans 8:37)

Someone took Ezra's hand, and he did not see whose. The candle had burned down to a thumb of wax. On the shelf beside it, in its towel, Kambria's lamp was still burning low. Forty people stood up in the dark of a burned farmhouse cellar in their coats and their stocking feet, and nobody reached for the tunnel, and nobody sang, and nobody wept aloud. They stood with their hands joined and listened to the gravel above them take the weight of boots.

They came for them at dawn.

\* \* \*

They did not fight.

Michael could have. He had men, and weapons, and a tunnel out the back of the cellar that the Peacekeepers had not found, and for a moment, in the gray light, with the boots coming down the farmhouse stairs, Ezra saw him look at the rifle leaning against the wall by the tunnel mouth, and saw every year of the soldier in him rise up at once.

Then Michael looked at Ezra, and Ezra shook his head, once.

And Michael, who had kept so many people alive for so long, who had carried the supply lines through the worst of it, who had never stopped standing for the children of his people, bent down in the candlelight, and laid his rifle on the stone floor of the cellar, and stepped away from the tunnel. He bent once more and, from the pocket where he had carried it all this time, took out a smooth white stone, and closed his hand around it, and kept it there.

"*He that leadeth into captivity shall go into captivity,*" he said, to no one, to himself, to the Book he had lived by. "*He that killeth with the sword must be killed with the sword. Here is the patience and the faith of the saints.*" He straightened up, and put his hands where the Peacekeepers could see them, and stood beside Ezra, and Ezra put a hand on his shoulder, and they waited together as the soldiers came down into the room with their lights and their rifles and found forty people standing around a guttered candle and a loaf of bread, singing, with their hands open and empty and raised, and not one of them afraid enough to bow.

They took them all. Ezra, and Michael, and Nina, and Dorsey, and David Levin, and Kyle and Sarah and their little girl who had never been outside in the daytime, and all forty, up out of the cellar into the gray dawn of the last days, into the trucks.

And when the lead officer, a young man with a tired hard face, demanded to know which one of them was the one called Ezra, the voice from the ground, the one Vale himself had put at the very top of the list, Ezra did not make them guess.

"I am," he said. "It's just Ezra. I come in peace."

Before they put him in the first truck, he asked the young officer for one minute on the cellar stairs, and the officer, who could not have said why, gave it to him. Ezra took Kambria's lamp down from the shelf, carried it up into the gray light, and set it in the kitchen window of the farmhouse where anyone coming after could see it. Then he held out his wrists. As the truck pulled away, through the slats, he could see the farmhouse behind them, and the small gold light in its window, and the sky over it, and in the sky, high in the east, where the sun was coming up, the clouds had begun to move as he had never seen clouds move, and he did not yet know what it meant, and he was not afraid.
