## Chapter 8: The Empty Seat

> *And it came to pass, when the evil spirit from God was upon Saul, that David took an harp, and played with his hand: so Saul was refreshed, and was well, and the evil spirit departed from him.* — 1 Samuel 16:23

**Word study: *refreshed* — רָוַח** Hebrew *ravach* (Strong's H7304). To breathe freely, to have room, to find relief. It is the word for a man who can finally take a full breath. When David played, Saul could breathe again.

*But the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the LORD troubled him. And Saul’s servants said unto him, Behold now, an evil spirit from God troubleth thee. Let our lord now command thy servants, which are before thee, to seek out a man, who is a cunning player on an harp…* (1 Samuel 16:14-16)

\* \* \*

On the afternoon of Thursday, April 16, the day of his senior recital, Jalen Patterson sat alone in the studio his father had built and played the ending one last time.

That morning he had stopped his father at the back door and held out the program, printed on pale blue paper, *Senior Recital. Thursday, April 16. 7:00 p.m.*, with his own name last on the list. "I'm closing it," he had said. "With something of mine. Mr. Hart's playing viola underneath, and there's something else, but you have to be there to see it. It's finished now. It has a name. You'll hear it there. You'll be the first." His father had studied the page. "You'll come?" Jalen had asked. "I'll be there," his father had said, and pinned the program to the refrigerator under the strawberry magnet, and gone out to the truck. Jalen had wanted so badly to believe him that he had.

The studio was small, twelve feet by sixteen, with a high window on the north wall and a wide one on the south facing the house. Everything in it he knew by hand. The pine floor he had helped sand. The walls he had helped frame. The door he had helped hang, the summer he was eleven, with his father's big hands over his on the hammer and his father's voice in his ear: *Let the hammer do the work, J. Don't fight it. Just let it fall.*

His mother had held the level. He remembered her laughing every time the bubble wandered off center, and his father pretending to be offended, and the three of them eating sandwiches on an upside-down bucket in the shade while the concrete cured.

At the end of the first day, when his father went inside to get them both a glass of water, Jalen had pulled the black marker out of his father's tool belt where it hung on a sawhorse. He had found the stud beside the door opening, the first one they had ever stood up together, and written on it in big, careful letters:

*ME AND DAD. FIRST WALL.*

Under it, the way his father numbered every stud on the plan, he wrote two small numbers, 91 and 4, the psalm and verse his mother had read aloud at the table the night before they framed it. Then he put the marker back. A week later the drywall went up over that stud, and the words disappeared behind it. His father never saw them. No one knew they were there; Jalen had never told anyone, not even his mother.

On the last day, when the roof was on and the door was hung, his father did what Jalen had never seen him do.

They had carried two folding chairs out from the garage and set them in the middle of the unfurnished room so they could sit in it for the first time. His mother had gone inside to start supper. The evening light came through the south window and lay across the floor in a long gold square, and the room smelled of sawdust and fresh paint. His father sat bent forward, forearms on his knees, and took in the walls one by one without saying anything.

"My father never built anything with me," he said finally. "Not one thing. Not a birdhouse."

Jalen had no answer. He had never met his grandfather. He knew only that he had been a deacon at his grandmother's old church, that he had died in a war before Jalen's father was ten, and that nobody in the family talked about him.

His father put one big hand on the floor between their chairs, and Jalen saw that it was shaking. Then he saw that his father was crying. He made no sound. The tears ran down through the sawdust on his cheeks, and he let them fall, his eyes on the walls they had built.

"I don't know what's wrong with me," he said, and laughed a little. "I'm just glad, J. That's all it is. I'm just glad."

Jalen sat beside him in the gold light, silent. He was eleven. He had always thought his father was the strongest man in the world. He still thought so. Now he had seen him cry, and both of those things were true at once, and he didn't have a word for it. He thought there ought to be a song for it instead.

That night, after everyone was asleep, he took his mother's old tablet out to the studio, propped it on the windowsill against a coffee can full of nails, and pressed record. He talked to it for six minutes.

"Hi, Dad. It's me. J." He cleared his throat. "Thank you for the room. I love it. I love it more than anything." He looked down at his hands. "Someday I'm going to write a song in here. A real one. The best one I can. And it's going to be for you."

Then he leaned close to the camera and said the name of the song so softly the tablet barely caught it.

He watched the video once, under the blanket in his room, and then he hid it in a folder on the tablet called HOMEWORK, where nobody would ever look. He never showed it to anyone. He carried that name for all those years, through every lesson and every audition and every fight in the kitchen, and he never said it out loud again.

People thought it was the cello. His father thought it was the cello and nothing else, and they had fought about it for two years, standing in the kitchen with their voices getting louder. *There's no money in music, J. A trade is something nobody can take from you.* Lately his father was never out here.

He lifted his bow and played the last page.

He had been writing the piece for a year and a half, since the night his mother found the lump. It had started as the only thing he could do with what he was feeling, which was a slow, low line on the C string, the deepest string on the cello, that climbed a little and fell back. It grew all through the chemo. It grew through the summer at the Outer Banks, when his mother's hair came back. It grew through the hospital bed in the living room. He played it for her the last night, in the corner, while she slept. Every version climbed higher than the one before, and every version stopped at the same place: one long note at the very top, held until his arm shook. Then nothing. He could never find what came after.

Not long ago, at three in the morning, he found it.

He played it now, alone, and when it was done he sat in the quiet with the bow resting on his knee and his eyes closed, and he thought about his mother. Near the end, when she could hardly lift her head, she had heard him practicing out here through the open window, and when he came inside she had beckoned him over to the hospital bed and pulled his face down close to hers.

"Your dad hears more than he lets on," she whispered. "Play it for him anyway."

On the music stand was the score, forty pages in ink, with whole bars crossed out and written over again in the margins. On the first page he had written a dedication. He had folded that page back so no one would see it until he was ready. On the program the school had printed that week, his piece was listed only as *Untitled,* and underneath it, in smaller letters: *Lionel Hart, viola.*

It wasn't untitled. He had known its name since he was eleven years old. But the name was for one person, and Jalen had decided a long time ago that his father would be the first one to hear it.

He took out his phone and texted his father.

*Seats saved. Row C, on the aisle. 7:00. Don't be late.*

The reply came twelve minutes later. A thumbs-up.

Jalen read it three times. Then he put the phone in his pocket and loosened his bow and laid the cello in its case.

His father had been out every Thursday night for weeks, and Jalen was starting to be able to read between the lines. He came home after eleven, and his coat smelled of incense, a sweet, heavy incense that was not church. One night, Jalen had seen his father's phone light up on the kitchen counter with a text from someone saved only as *C*, and a small yellow moon. His father had turned it over before he could read the rest.

Jalen had a good idea what it meant, and he didn't want to say it out loud. Tonight he had a piece to play.

\* \* \*

The auditorium at Coolidge High held four hundred people, and by a quarter to seven every seat was taken.

Kambria had driven Grandma Loretta and Mia. Jalen came out through the side door before the house lights went down to make sure the seats were still there. He had saved five, at the end of Row C, with programs laid on each one. Grandma was already in the first, in her Sunday hat. Mia was in the second, swinging her legs and holding a bunch of grocery-store daisies she had picked out herself. Kambria sat in the third with her phone in her lap, already checking the camera.

The fourth seat held nothing but his mother's scarf.

It was the soft purple one she had worn to every treatment. Purple was her favorite color. He had found it in the back of her closet that morning, folded, still smelling faintly of lavender, and he had brought it and laid it across the seat himself, smoothing it with both hands. No one had asked him about it. Grandma had looked at it, and then at her hands.

The fifth seat was on the aisle. On it was a program, and across the top of the program, in black marker, Jalen had written *DAD*.

It was empty.

"He's coming," Mia told him confidently. "He promised."

"I know," Jalen said.

At 6:58, from behind the curtain at the edge of the stage, he looked out again. The seat was still empty. He texted *Where are you?* and watched the screen. *Delivered.* Not read.

At seven the house lights went down.

\* \* \*

There were six seniors on the program, and Jalen was last.

He stood in the wings with his cello and listened to Marisol play a Chopin nocturne, and then the Lee twins play a duet for violin and viola, and he could hardly hear any of it. Every time one of them finished and the audience clapped, he leaned out past the curtain and looked down at Row C.

Grandma. Mia. Kambria. The purple scarf. The empty seat.

He kept himself from looking at the seat again. He thought instead of how all of this had come to be.

The viola part was Mr. Hart's idea, and he had kept it spare on purpose.

Lionel Hart had been his teacher since he was nine years old. He was sixty-eight now, a tall, stooped man with white hair cut close to his head and long fingers that fretted the air even when he sat talking. He had played viola with the Capital Philharmonic for thirty-one years, and he had no children of his own. When Jalen's mother got sick, Mr. Hart started picking him up for lessons on Saturdays. He had promised Angela, in her last month, that the boy would not go to his recital alone. After she died, he never stopped. He drove Jalen to every audition. He sat in the front row of every concert. He was the one who had heard the unfinished piece through the door of the music room after school one afternoon, and come in and closed the door behind him and taken his old viola out of its case and said, "Play it again. Slower. Let me find you."

Since then they had built it together, the old man with his viola and the young cellist beside him, as Jalen and his father had once built the walls of the studio. Mr. Hart never told him what to play. He only listened, and then found a few long, quiet notes underneath him, so that wherever the cello went, it was never alone. "This is your piece," he said, whenever Jalen asked him to play more. "I'm just the floor under it."

The orchestra was Jalen's idea, and it was a secret.

In March he had brought Mr. Hart the whole score and asked if the Coolidge orchestra could learn it: all of it, the strings and the winds and the brass and the drums at the end, and the harp. The harp was for his mother. On Sunday afternoons she used to grade papers at the dinner table with *Clair de Lune* playing on her phone, and he wanted those soft, rippling notes under the saddest part of the piece, so that she would be in the room. But he didn't want the audience to see any of them. He wanted the orchestra behind the curtain. He wanted the people in the seats to see only him and his teacher, two people alone on a bare stage, and to hear the harp and the strings come in from somewhere they couldn't see. And near the end, he wanted the curtain to open.

For five weeks, fifty-eight students had stayed after school to learn a piece with no name. Mr. Hart called two old friends from the Philharmonic to help him: Ada Lindqvist, who had sat first violin beside him for twenty years, to coach the strings, and Thomas Reyes to teach a sophomore named Grace the harp part. Neither of them would take a dollar. "Lionel asked," was all Mrs. Lindqvist said. The principal agreed to keep the orchestra off the program. Not one of them told anyone.

And every one of them would be dressed in white.

That had been Jalen's idea too, and he knew exactly why. His mother was the one who had led them to Christ. She had prayed with Mia every night since Mia could talk. She had knelt beside Jalen at the dinner table on a Sunday night when he was twelve, while he gave his life to the Lord. She had prayed for his father every day of her life and never once stopped. And she had taught her children that there was only One who could wash anyone clean from the darkness the whole world was living in.

When he was nine, he had botched his first recital and come home crying, and his mother had sat on the edge of his bed and read to him about the great multitude in the Book of Revelation, people from every nation and every language standing before the throne in white robes, the church of the Lamb, carried safely out of the world's trouble. *These are they which came out of great tribulation, and have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb. (Revelation 7:14)* "Nobody's robe starts out white, baby," she told him. "He's the only one who can make it that way."

Then she read him the last verse of the chapter, about the ones in white, and wiped his wet cheeks with her thumb while she read it. *…and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes. (Revelation 7:17)*

"That's a promise, baby. Every tear. Yours, and mine, and your dad's too."

She tucked the blanket under his chin.

"And nobody plays alone, either," she said. "Down here it feels like it sometimes. But the whole time, God is behind the curtain putting His orchestra together. One day that curtain's going to open, and you'll see everybody who was playing with you the whole time."

His white suit hung on the back of the studio door, pressed and ready. Every player on that stage would wear the same.

And at the very end, there was the horn.

The morning after he found the ending, Jalen had ridden his bike across Northeast with his cello on his back and knocked on Mr. Hart's door at seven o'clock. The old man answered in his bathrobe. Jalen played him the ending right there on the front steps, and then he asked him.

The shofar hung on the wall of Mr. Hart's living room, over the piano: a long, twisted ram's horn, dark brown at the mouthpiece and pale as bone at the bell. He had brought it home from Jerusalem in 1998, when the Philharmonic played there, and a Jewish friend from the brass section had taught him how to sound it. Once a year, at the Easter sunrise service at his church, he took it down and blew it.

Jalen had heard it once. The Easter before last, his mother was too weak to stand for long, but she made Mr. Hart drive them both to the service anyway. She sat in a folding chair at the edge of the church cemetery in the gray light, with a blanket over her knees and her purple scarf tied tight, and when the old man raised the horn and the long blast went out over the graves, she took Jalen's hand and held it hard.

"That's what it's going to sound like, baby," she said. "When He comes for us. The trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised. Don't you ever forget it."

A few months later, she was gone.

"I want you to blow it at the end," Jalen told Mr. Hart on the steps. "After my last note. For her. She believed He was coming back. She believed it more than anybody I ever knew. I want everybody in that room to hear what she believed."

Mr. Hart said nothing. He stood in his doorway in his bathrobe and looked at the young man on his steps with the cello case on his back, and Jalen saw the old man's chin begin to shake.

"That horn isn't for a show," he said at last. "It's for calling people to God."

"I know," Jalen said. "That's why I'm asking you."

The old man came down and sat beside him on the step. He had never once cried in front of Jalen, not at the hospital and not at the grave. He cried now, with his long hands over his face, on his own front steps at seven in the morning. When he could speak, he looked up at Jalen.

"My wife and I prayed nineteen years for a child," he said. "Nineteen years. And God told us no." He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "She died still believing He knew what He was doing. I never could.

"Not until a nine-year-old boy walked into my church basement with a rummage-sale cello, sawing away at it with his bow held all wrong." He wiped his face on his sleeve, and he laughed. "I'll blow it, son. I'll blow it for your mother. And I'll blow it for you."

Now, in the wings, Mr. Hart came and stood beside him. He was wearing a white suit, the same as Jalen's, and his long fingers were moving at his side, playing nothing. Under his other arm he carried a long, narrow bag of midnight-blue velvet.

Behind the closed curtain, on the risers that had been set up that afternoon, fifty-eight students in white sat in the dark with their instruments across their knees. Jalen could hear them breathing. Not one of them made a sound.

"There's someone in the back row from the Capital Philharmonic," Mr. Hart murmured. "Evelyn Shaw. She runs their young artists program. I played in that orchestra most of my life, and I sent her your recording from the winter showcase." He looked down at Jalen. "I didn't tell you because I didn't want you to be nervous."

"I'm not nervous," Jalen said. He was looking at the empty seat.

Mr. Hart followed his eyes. He took his time answering.

"Your mother asked me for a promise," he said finally. "The last week. She took both my hands, as she did with everybody, and she said, 'Lionel, stand with my boy. Whatever his father does, you stand with him.'" His voice roughened. "I told her I would. So whoever is in that seat tonight or not, son, you are not going out there by yourself."

He held out his hand.

Jalen looked at it. Then he took it. The old man's grip was dry and warm, the grip of a hand that had held a bow for fifty years, and he held on past the end of a handshake, as a father would.

At 8:20, the vice principal walked out to the microphone and read from her card. "Our final performers tonight are Jalen Patterson, cello, with Mr. Lionel Hart, viola, playing an original composition by Jalen. The program lists it as Untitled."

Jalen and Mr. Hart walked out into the light together, two figures in white in front of the closed red curtain.

\* \* \*

The stage was bright and hot, and beyond the edge of it the auditorium was a murky blur of faces. Mr. Hart took the chair a few steps behind him and to his left, at the edge of the light, with his old viola resting on his knee and the velvet bag on the floor beside his chair. Behind them both, the heavy red curtain hung closed all the way across the stage. Jalen sat in the chair at the center, settled the cello's endpin into the floor, and tuned the A string, as he always did, though it didn't need it. Then he looked out at Row C.

Grandma had her hands folded in her lap. Mia was sitting up straight, holding her daisies in both fists. Kambria had raised her phone to film. The purple scarf lay across the fourth seat in the glow from the stage lights.

The fifth seat was empty.

A microphone stood at the front edge of the stage. Jalen leaned toward it. His eyes were stinging, and he could feel his chin starting to go, and he made it stop. He had decided that afternoon what he would say before he played. The piece had a name, and the name was not his to give away yet. But the people it was written for deserved to be spoken of before the first note, even if they could not hear it.

He said it in a strong, clear voice that carried all the way to the back wall.

"I'm dedicating this to someone who isn't here right now."

Then he sat back and settled the cello against his chest.

He could not lift the bow. An ache went down into his stomach, and his eyes burned. Four hundred people were waiting beyond the lights. He thought, *I can't.* He thought about getting up and walking off.

Then Jalen remembered his mother's voice.

*Your dad hears more than he lets on. Play it for him anyway.*

Behind him, faint as breath, a single low C sounded from the viola.

Jalen turned his head. Mr. Hart was looking at him over the top of the music stand, his bow still resting on the string. He had given Jalen his note that way at the start of every lesson for all those years. He nodded once.

*I'm here.*

He drew the bow across the C string.

The first note was so low that people felt it before they heard it. It came up through the wooden floor of the stage and into the seats. It was the note he had started with, the night his mother told Kambria about the lump in the kitchen while he sat in the studio, afraid, because he had heard the fear in her voice through the open window. It was grief before he knew it was grief. It climbed a little. It fell back. Under it, so faintly that at first only Jalen could hear it, the viola came in: a few long, quiet notes underneath, holding him up.

He played the chemo room. He played his mother's hair coming out in handfuls, and his father standing behind her chair with the clippers shaking in his hand. The line pushed forward and climbed, and the viola pushed with it, and the music turned angry. Then it fell back, harder, all the way down to the lowest note.

He played the hospital bed in the living room. He played the last night, in the corner, while his mother slept and his father finally held her hand. He played the funeral in the cold, and "Amazing Grace" at the graveside with tears running down his face, and the silence of the house afterward, and his father asleep on the couch with the television on, every night, every night, every night. Behind him, Mr. Hart drew one soft note on every beat, steady as a metronome. And then, from somewhere behind the closed curtain, a harp began, soft notes rippling up and down beneath the line: his mother's Sunday-afternoon music. In the front rows, people looked at one another. There was no harp on the stage. A moment later, so delicately that no one could be sure of it, strings began to breathe underneath the harp from the shadows behind the curtain, and the hall went stiller.

The line climbed again. Higher. Searching.

Out in the seats, the auditorium had gone still. No one coughed or shifted.

Then the viola fell away. Mr. Hart lowered his bow and laid it across his knee and left him alone up there, as they had planned it. The cello climbed higher than it had ever gone, up past every place it had stopped before, up to the long note at the very top. The note he could never get past. He held it. His bow arm began to shake. This was where it always ended. This was where it had ended for a year and a half: one long trembling note at the top, and then nothing, and silence, and no answer.

He didn't stop.

He leaned into the bow and gave the note his whole weight, and it swelled. It grew louder. It grew louder still, until the sound filled the stage and the hall and the rafters, until the strings were roaring under his fingers and the cello was shaking against his chest. In Row C, Mia dropped her daisies and put both hands over her mouth. Grandma's eyes were shut and her lips were moving. Kambria was crying behind her phone and holding it steady. At the back of the hall, a gray-haired woman rose to her feet without seeming to know she had done it. The note climbed and swelled and climbed and swelled until it seemed the wood must split and the room itself break.

And then he stopped.

He lifted the bow off the string, and the silence was total. Four hundred people sat in the hush and did not breathe. One second. Two. Mia's daisies lay on the floor of Row C. Three. Somewhere in the back, someone began to sob and caught it. Four. Jalen sat with his bow in the air and his eyes closed, and he could feel each of them waiting, and he let them wait. Five.

Then, almost inaudibly, he began again from the bottom.

It was the low line from the very first night, in its plainest form, slower than he had ever played it. It sounded like someone getting up off the floor. Behind him, Mr. Hart's viola came back in underneath it, quiet and steady, one long note at a time. And then the line began to climb.

It climbed out of the pit one handhold at a time. Each phrase started a little higher than the one before. The viola climbed with it, always a step below and never above, and behind the curtain the unseen strings climbed too, swelling under them both. Higher. Louder. The music pressed forward bar by bar, and the hall leaned forward with it.

Then, behind him, the curtain began to open.

Jalen didn't turn around. He didn't need to. He heard the gasp ripple through the hall as the heavy red velvet drew back from the center, both halves at once. Behind it, on risers that climbed all the way to the back wall, sat the Coolidge High orchestra: fifty-eight students, every one of them dressed in white, bows moving together under the lights. Violins and violas and cellos. The basses at the back. Flutes and clarinets, horns and trumpets and trombones, the timpani and the drums. Mrs. Whitfield in the first violin chair with her eyes closed. Grace at the gold harp. Every one of them had been there from the very first note, playing unseen behind the velvet.

The sound of all of them together came out over the edge of the stage in one great wave. Mr. Hart rose to his feet with his viola and turned halfway toward the orchestra and lifted his bow, and the students in white lifted theirs with him. And at the front of it all, the cello reached the top of the climb and held it, with the whole orchestra holding it up from underneath, and the sound filled the hall to its rafters.

In Row C, Kambria had lowered her phone, and her mouth was open. Mia was standing on her seat. Grandma had both hands pressed over her heart.

Jalen thought of his mother at his bedside when he was nine. *One day that curtain's going to open, and you'll see everybody who was playing with you the whole time.*

Then the line came down. It wasn't falling this time. It came down the stairs of its own house in the dark, and knew every step. It came to rest on a C in the middle of the cello, and he held it.

Beside that C, Mr. Hart held an E-flat on the viola, somber and low, and the two notes together were C minor. Then he moved one note. Just one. The E-flat rose to an E.

C major.

It was not a question anymore, and not yet an answer. It was still mourning. But there was light in it now, a door left ajar and light at the crack.

Jalen kept the C alive. He drew one more breath and broke upward out of it, as the ram's horn had broken upward on that Easter morning: from the low C to the G above it, and to the C above that, and then up to a high G, the highest note he would play. He held it straight and hard, with no sweetness in it, pressing the bow into the string until the note rang off the back wall and people in the front rows flinched from the sound of it. He held it until his arm burned. And he kept holding it.

Under his note, the orchestra began to rise.

It came up out of nothing, all of them at once, so muted at first that people in the back rows were not sure they heard it. It was an A-flat chord, and Jalen's high G sat inside it and ached. It grew. Under it the timpanist began to tap the rim of his drum, slow and quiet, one tick and then another, then closer together. In the first violin chair, Mrs. Whitfield lifted her bow and began to climb above him, one step and then another, over a brooding F minor that made Grandma cover her mouth. The ticking ran faster and faster under all of it, until it was almost one sound.

Then it stopped, and the whole orchestra broke open into C major, as loud as it had played all night. Mrs. Whitfield's violin stood on the high G an octave above Jalen's, and Jalen's G stood in the middle of the chord, and the hall rang with both of them. And over all of them, Grace's harp began to sing. It was the melody Jalen had begun with, the low, grieving line from the night of the lump, high and clear now, and in a major key. It was the same melody. It was not grieving anymore.

Jalen held his note with his eyes closed while the harp sang over him and the orchestra carried him, and when they began to fade, he faded with them, every player in white letting go together, until the last soft chord of the harp and the last of his bow on the string were the same sound. Then that was gone too.

In the silence after it, with the students in white holding still behind him, Mr. Hart turned to face the hall.

He laid his viola on his chair. He bent and drew the ram's horn out of the velvet bag, and the stage lights caught it, burnished and twisted and pale at the bell. Behind him, without anyone telling them to, the students in white began rising to their feet, row by row, until the whole orchestra was standing. Four hundred people watched him raise the horn to his lips. In Row C, Grandma's hand went to her heart.

The old man filled his lungs and blew.

The sound was nothing like the cello. It was raw and ancient, one long blast that rose and broke and rose again and would not stop. It went through the hall and through the walls and out into the night. Jalen sat with his bow in his lap and his eyes closed and let it pour over him. He thought of the folding chair at the edge of the cemetery, and the gray light, and his mother's hand gripping his. *That's what it's going to sound like, baby.*

Mr. Hart held the blast until there was no breath left in him. Then he lowered the horn, and the sound rang on in the rafters a moment longer, and was gone.

Jalen opened his eyes. He was breathing hard, and the hall was silent. He looked down at Row C, at the purple scarf and the empty seat beside it. The microphone stood at the front of the stage, where he had pictured himself saying the name, with his father in that seat to hear it.

He kept the name to himself.

\* \* \*

For a second no one stirred. Then the whole auditorium was on its feet.

The applause came in a wave, and it kept coming. People were crying openly. Mia was jumping up and down and shouting his name. Grandma was on her feet with both hands raised, as she raised them in church, and tears were running down into her collar. Mr. Hart stood with the ram's horn at his side and applauded him, and would not take a bow himself. Jalen stood and bowed, as he had been taught. Then he turned around and bowed to the orchestra, to all of them in white, and they tapped their bows against their music stands for him, and some of them were crying too. When he turned back, he looked one more time at Row C.

The seat on the aisle was still empty. But when he straightened, Mr. Hart was standing right behind him, close enough to catch him, as he had stood at the graveside.

\* \* \*

In the lobby afterward, people he didn't know kept stopping to shake his hand. A man in a Nationals cap told him he had lost his father in March. A girl from the sophomore class hugged him and ran away before he could say anything.

Across the lobby, he saw his grandmother make her way through the crowd to Mr. Hart, tiny and straight-backed in her Sunday hat. She took both of the old man's hands in hers, as his mother used to, and held on. Jalen couldn't hear what she said. He saw Mr. Hart shake his head, and he saw his grandmother nod hers, firmly, as she did when she would not be argued with.

She told him that night what she had said. "I told him he has been standing where my son should have been standing," she said. "And he told me he was only standing where your mother asked him to."

The gray-haired woman from the back row found him by the trophy case. She was slight and tidy, in a charcoal suit, with reading glasses on a chain around her neck. She held out a business card. *Dr. Evelyn Shaw. Director, Young Artists Program. Capital Philharmonic Orchestra.*

"Lionel Hart has been telling me about you for three years," she said. "I came to find out if he was exaggerating. He wasn't." She studied his face. "Who taught you to write like that, Mr. Patterson?"

Jalen looked down at the card. Then he looked across the lobby at the old man standing by the doors, holding Jalen's cello case so that he wouldn't have to. "My mom and dad," he said. "And Mr. Hart."

"Well, tell all three of them they did well." She tapped the card. "We have a summer fellowship. Eight weeks, with a stipend. And after that, I'd like to talk to you about the fall. Have your parents call me."

Kambria waited until the lobby had emptied. Then she came and stood beside him and held out her phone.

"I got all of it," she said. "Every note." Her eyes were still red. "Jalen, people need to hear this. Can I share it? Only if you want me to."

He watched himself on the phone's screen: the chair, the bright light, the bow arm shaking on the long note at the top. He thought about his father, wherever his father was.

"Yeah," he said. "But just call it Untitled. It has a name. My dad hears the name first."

"I will," she said.

"And Miss Kambria?" He hesitated. "Can you put it on something? Like a flash drive? For my dad."

Kambria held his gaze, and he saw that she understood.

"For when he's ready," she said.

"Yeah," Jalen said. "For when he's ready."

\* \* \*

Kambria copied the video onto a thumb drive from Jalen's desk drawer that night, while Grandma put Mia to bed. When she left, Jalen went upstairs and dug his mother's old tablet out of the bottom of his closet. It still turned on. The HOMEWORK folder was still there. He copied the file onto the drive beside Kambria's video, and he renamed them both so they would play in the right order: *1 For Dad* and *2 Untitled*. Then he sat at the dinner table and held the drive in his hand. It weighed almost nothing, and his hand was shaking anyway. Everything he had wanted to say to his father since he was eleven years old was on it. He put it in his pocket.

\* \* \*

At 11:40, he was sitting in the unlit studio when the truck pulled into the driveway.

He heard the engine stop, the truck door close, his father's boots on the back steps, and then a long pause. Then the boots came back down the steps and across the yard, and the studio door opened.

His father stood in the doorway with the light off.

"J," he said. "Buddy. I'm sorry. The job ran long, and I lost track of the time, and by the time I..." He stopped.

Jalen kept his back to him. He was sitting in the chair with the cello case closed at his feet. Across the music stand in front of him lay his mother's purple scarf.

The smell reached him a second later. Sweet, heavy incense, and under it, perfume. Not his mother's. His mother had smelled of lavender, and this was lavender too, but turned: heavier, darker, sweeter, and it was coming off his father's jacket.

"You smell like that incense again," Jalen said.

His father stayed silent.

"Was the client burning candles?" Jalen asked.

Jalen could hear the crickets outside, a car passing on the street, his own heart.

"Does the client wear perfume too, Dad?"

"Watch your mouth."

Jalen stood up. He reached past his father and pulled the chain on the bare bulb over the door, and the light came on hard and yellow, and his father flinched from it. His eyes were red. There was a plum smudge on the collar of his shirt, and he smelled of whiskey and somebody's perfume. Jalen looked at the collar, and then he made himself stop looking at it.

"Four hundred people," Jalen said. "Four hundred people stood up tonight, Dad. Grandma. Mia. Miss Kambria. Mr. Hart, who isn't even family, who's been driving me to lessons since Mom got sick because you were always working. A lady from the Philharmonic. Everybody stood up." His voice was rising, and he let it. "And there was one seat on the aisle with your name on it. I wrote it myself. In marker. So you couldn't miss it."

"I said I was sorry."

"You're always sorry!" It came out of him louder than anything he had ever said to his father. "You were sorry for every round of chemo! You sat in that waiting room every one of those Tuesdays, and you never once walked through the door!"

His father's face changed. "Don't you talk to me about those Tuesdays. You don't know what that was."

"I know you weren't in there! Mom was in there. Miss Kambria was in there. You were out in the hall looking at your phone!"

"I drove her to every one of them!"

"You drove her to the door!" Jalen was shouting now, and his eyes were burning, and he didn't care. "That's what you do. You drive everybody to the door, and you never come in. You did it to her. You did it at church. You did it tonight."

"I have been working myself into the ground to keep a roof over this family!" His father was shouting too, and his voice filled the cramped room. "Who do you think paid for that cello? Who paid for the lessons, and the summer camps, and the bow that cost more than my first truck? You think music pays a mortgage? I put food on that table. I built this..." He threw his arm out at the walls. "I built this whole room for you!"

"Then why are you never in it?"

His father opened his mouth, and nothing came out.

"You think I come out here for the cello." Jalen's voice cracked, and he went on anyway. "Every night. You yell at me in the kitchen about trades and money, and you think it's the cello. It's not the cello, Dad. I come out here because this is the only place left where you were ever my father." He pointed at the floor between them. "Right there. You sat right there in a folding chair the day we finished, and you cried. You told me your dad never built anything with you. Not one thing." He was breathing hard. "And now look at you. You're turning into him. You're gone, and you're not even dead."

The words hit his father, and Jalen watched them hit. His father's face drained of color, and then it went hard, and he took one step into the room, and his right hand came up.

Jalen's whole body went cold. His father had never raised a hand to him. Not once in his life. But the hand was up, open, shaking in the air between them, and for one second neither of them breathed.

Then his father looked at his own hand.

He turned it over and studied it. Jalen saw a look come over his face that he had never seen there before, and it was fear. Not fear of Jalen. Fear of the hand. His father lowered it and held it against his chest with his other hand. Then his knees gave out, and he went down against the wall to the pine floor.

"He drank," his father said. His voice was barely there. "My father. Every Friday. And then he'd use his hands, on my mother and on me, and he'd quote the Bible the whole time he was doing it." He stared at the floor. "One night he put a gun to my head. I was eight years old. I never told your mother that. I never told anybody." His hand was still pressed against his chest. "I swore. I swore to God I would never come home with drink on me and lift my hand to you. And tonight I did both." He couldn't finish.

Jalen dared not move. He had never heard any of it. Not one word, ever.

"I don't know how to do this, J," his father said. "I never saw anybody do it. Your mother knew how. Your mother did all of it, and I stood in the doorway and watched her. And then she was gone." His face crumpled. "I couldn't go in the chemo room. If I went in, it was real. I couldn't go in that auditorium tonight. I sat in a parking lot for an hour, and then I drove somewhere I shouldn't have, because I couldn't stand to sit in a seat next to her empty one." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "I wasn't at a job."

"I know," Jalen said.

His father looked up at him.

"I miss her," his father said, and his voice broke all the way. "I miss her so much I can't breathe, J. I can't breathe in that house."

Jalen's legs gave way. He was standing, and then he was on his knees on the floor beside his father, and his father's arms were around him, and he was crying into his father's shirt harder than he had cried at the funeral, or at the grave, or in all the months since. His father held Jalen's head against his chest with one big hand, the same as he used to when Jalen was little and woke up from a bad dream, and his father was crying too, and the sobs shook them both.

"I miss her," Jalen said into his shirt. "I miss her, Dad."

"I know. I know, buddy. Me too."

"She should have been there."

"She was there," his father said. "She was there, and I wasn't. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, J. I'm so sorry I wasn't there."

They stayed there in the room they had built, holding each other until the crying wore itself out.

The door creaked.

Mia stood in the doorway in her nightgown, the yellow one with the moons, with her bare feet wet from the grass and her teddy bear hanging from one arm by its paw. Her face was grave. Later Jalen would understand that she had been out there a long time. Her feet were numb, and the bear's ear was damp where she had been holding it against her mouth.

"I heard you yelling," she said. "So I waited till you stopped."

His father wiped his face with his sleeve.

Mia came across the floor and knelt down between them. She put one small hand on her father's wet cheek and the other on Jalen's, and she looked from one of them to the other.

"Don't be sad," she said. "Mommy's not sad. Mommy went to be with Jesus."

She said it with no doubt in her voice. Then she squeezed in between them with her bear, and they held her, and for a long time no one spoke.

After a while his father lifted his head. "Will you play it for me?" he asked, his voice raw.

Jalen looked at the cello case at his feet, and at his mother's scarf on the music stand. He thought of the drive in his pocket.

"Not tonight," he said. "I don't think I could get through it." He wiped his eyes. "You'll hear it, Dad. I promise. When you're ready."

His father nodded. He did not ask again.

When Mia was asleep against him, his father picked her up, bear and all, and carried her across the moonless yard to the house. At the studio door he stopped and looked back at Jalen, and for a moment it seemed he would say more. Then he said, "Good night, son," and went.

Jalen sat alone in the studio. Then he rose and crossed to the door and laid his palm against the wall beside the door, over the place where the words were hidden. *Me and Dad. First wall.*

He stood there with his hand on the wood until it grew warm under his palm.

\* \* \*

Kambria posted the video at 11:52 that night.

*Untitled. Jalen Patterson, cello.*

By the time the sun came up over the studio roof, eleven thousand people had heard it. Strangers had left comments in English and Spanish and Portuguese. A woman in Ohio wrote that she had played it four times and cried every time, because it sounded like every goodbye she had never gotten to say. A man in Lagos wrote only: *I called my father.*

His own father was not one of them.
