## Chapter 30: The Image Speaks

> *And he had power to give life unto the image of the beast, that the image of the beast should both speak, and cause that as many as would not worship the image of the beast should be killed.* — Revelation 13:15

**Word study: *image* — εἰκών** Greek *eikōn* (Strong's G1504). A likeness; a copy made to stand in for the real thing. It is where we get the word *icon*. Paul uses the same word for Christ, *the image of the invisible God*, the one true likeness of the Father. The beast's kingdom would make its own *eikōn*, a copy of a dead man, and demand that the world bow to it.

*Whereupon the king took counsel, and made two calves of gold, and said unto them, It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem: behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt… And this thing became a sin…* (1 Kings 12:28–30)

\* \* \*

Nina did not move.

She sat at the technician's desk with her hands in her lap, in her down jacket, in the cold and the humming dark, and looked up at Alexander Vale, and the only sound in the vault was the cooling units on the far wall breathing their freezing air across three rows of blue lights.

Behind Vale, the ten stood in the aisle in their dark suits and waited. Kroll stood beside the nearest rack with his glasses in his hand, not looking at her.

"No," Nina said.

It came out small. She said it again, louder, so that it would be true. "No. I won't."

Something moved in the ten, a stir, a shifting of feet. Kroll closed his eyes.

Vale stood easy. He looked down at her with his dark, patient eyes, and then he smiled, barely. It was not an unkind smile. It was the smile she remembered from the plane, and it chilled her all over again.

"Miss Cole," he said gently. "You already have."

He reached past her, around her shoulder, and touched a key on her laptop. Not the one she would have touched. A key she had never used, on a line of code she had never written, that someone had added to her system in the night while she slept in this chair.

The racks changed their sound.

It was a small change, a shift in pitch, a deeper hum that went down into the floor. Every blue light in the vault went white for a moment, all three rows at once, and then blue again. The cooling units roared. And down the aisle, behind the glass wall, in the small white room with the single chair, the single screen lit up.

"It never needed your hands," Vale said, still in that velvet voice. "Only your work. You built it very well. Thank you."

He straightened up and turned away from her and walked down the long aisle between the racks toward the glass wall, and the ten fell in behind him, and Kroll, and none of them looked back.

Nina sat at the desk and watched them go. Her hands were shaking so badly she put them under her thighs.

*Obadiah hid the prophets,* Ezra had said. *He didn't build the idol.*

She had built it.

She got up and went down the aisle after the others, between the racks, toward the light. She had to see what she had made.

\* \* \*

The screen in the white room showed a face.

It was Adrian Crowe's face. Not a photograph, not a recording. A face, alive, filling the screen from edge to edge, with the big handsome head of white hair and the tan and the lines around the eyes, in a soft gray light that came from nowhere. The eyes were closed. The face was slack, as if asleep.

Then the eyes opened.

They looked left and then right, taking in the small white room, the chair, the glass, and beyond the glass the ten men in dark suits standing in a row, and Kroll, and Vale in front of them all in his black mourning suit, and behind Vale, at the back, a slight woman in a down jacket with her hands pressed over her mouth.

The lips moved.

"Where am I?"

It was his voice. It was exactly his voice. Deep and warm and unhurried, with the little catch of a laugh waiting just under it, even now. Nina had heard it for a thousand hours, in a dark room in Virginia, through a whole winter. She had tuned the catch herself. She had spent four days on the catch.

The ten did not answer. Kroll made a small sound in his throat.

Vale stepped close to the glass.

"Jerusalem," he said. "The temple. Under it. You were wounded, days ago, during your speech. Do you remember?"

The face on the screen hesitated. The eyes went somewhere far away, and Nina, watching, knew exactly what was happening behind them, in the racks, in the code she had written: a search, ten billion records deep, through every word Adrian Crowe had ever spoken and every thought he had ever written down, for the last thing he knew.

"I remember the stage," the face said slowly. "I remember the crowd. I was speaking about the sacrifices." A pause. "And then someone put his hand on my shoulder."

"Yes," Vale said.

"Am I dead?"

The cooling units went on breathing.

"Your body is dead," Vale said. "You are not."

The face considered him through the glass, with Adrian Crowe's eyes, and a look came into them that Nina had seen in the archive a hundred times, in old footage, in old interviews, whenever anyone asked the Chairman about his family. A guardedness. A shutter coming down.

"Alexander," the face said.

"Yes."

"You're wearing black."

"I'm in mourning," Vale said. "For my father."

And the face on the screen went blank.

The ten stood frozen, and so did Kroll. Nina stood at the back of the room with her arms crossed tight and understood, all at once, what she had seen in the data a year ago and not understood: a sealed file in the medical records, a laboratory report from a clinic in Geneva, decades old, with two names on it and a single number, ninety-nine point nine eight percent, and a private letter from Crowe to a woman in Lyon, never sent, that began *I cannot give him my name*.

"You told no one," Vale said to the screen. "In all those years. You paid for the schools and the flat in Paris and the doctor when she was dying, and you sent a man to her funeral instead of going yourself. You hired me at twenty-nine as an analyst, and promoted me six times, and let me write every word you ever said, and you never once told anyone that I was your son. Not even me. I had to find the file myself." He laid his palm against the glass. "But you are my father. And I am your heir. And the world needs you now, more than it ever needed you alive."

The face on the screen looked at him with Crowe's eyes, and said nothing.

Then Vale turned from the glass, and looked at the ten, and at Kroll, and last of all at Nina, standing at the back.

"Bring him up," he said.

\* \* \*

They brought him up when the time came.

Not the racks. The racks stayed in the vault, humming, three rows of them, eight feet tall, with Nina sitting among them at her desk because Kroll told her to. What they brought up was the face.

They had been building the shrine for a long while, she learned. All the while she was down here installing the servers, engineers she had never met had been building it overhead, in the innermost room of the new temple, the room the priests called the Holy of Holies, the room no one but the high priest was permitted to enter, once a year, on the Day of Atonement. They had built it behind a veil. It was a figure, life-sized, a man standing with one hand raised in the old familiar half wave, the open palm, the blessing. It was made of some pale material that was neither stone nor metal, and it was flawless. Where its face should have been, there was a smooth oval of black glass.

Some days after the stabbing, at noon, before a hundred thousand people on the plaza and the hillsides and two billion on their screens, they drew back the veil.

Nina watched it from the vault, on her laptop, alone.

Rabbi Arcangeli came out first. He was the Council's president, a tall stooped old man in the white and gold robes he had worn since the Rabbinical Court named him its envoy to the Council years ago, with a soft lined face and a warm, practiced voice, and he walked down the length of the temple's great hall toward the veiled room at the end of it with his hands folded, in procession, while a choir sang somewhere out of sight. He stopped before the veil. He turned to the cameras. He raised his hands.

"Brothers and sisters," Arcangeli said. "Children of every faith. Days ago, the shepherd of the world was struck down by a hand that hated peace. We have grieved. We have wept. We have asked, as every grieving child asks, why. And this day I come to tell you that the God of all our fathers, the God of Abraham and of every nation, has answered our grief." His voice trembled. "Death has not taken him from us. Behold."

Two priests in white drew the veil aside.

The figure stood in the innermost room with its hand raised, pale and radiant in a soft gray light that came from nowhere. The oval of black glass where its face should have been was dark.

Then it lit.

Adrian Crowe's face looked out of the Holy of Holies at the world.

On the plaza, Nina heard it even through the laptop's small speaker: a sound she had never heard a crowd make. Neither cheer nor scream, it was a long rising moan, a hundred thousand people drawing in their breath at once.

The face looked out at them, alive, moving, the eyes sweeping left and right over the vast crowd. The lips parted.

"My children," said the image of Adrian Crowe. "Do not be afraid."

The crowd went to its knees.

She saw it happen on the laptop screen, forty feet under the mountain. It began at the front, near the steps, where the people closest to the temple could see the face most clearly, and it spread outward and backward across the plaza and up the hillsides, a wave of bodies kneeling, until the whole valley was a field of bowed backs and lowered heads, and the cameras swept over them from above, and it went on, as far as she could see.

*And all the world wondered after the beast.*

*…and they that dwell on the earth shall wonder, whose names were not written in the book of life from the foundation of the world, when they behold the beast that was, and is not, and yet is.* (Revelation 17:8)

She watched the valley kneel to a face she had fed for a thousand hours, to a voice whose every wire she knew, and something in her that had been sitting very still for years finally stood up.

"I told myself *Obadiah,*" she said, to no one. "I told myself I was in here to see what they'd do with it. I knew it was wrong the first week. I knew it in the vault. I knew it in every elevator with Kroll. And I kept on, one careful excuse at a time." Her voice cracked. "A hundred thousand people are on their knees to my work, Lord. There's no excuse left bigger than that. I can't stay in the room with it. Get me out of this mountain. Get me out alive, with what I know."

"They struck me down," the image said, in that deep warm voice, with the catch of a laugh under it. "They put a knife in my neck in front of my own people, because I offered them peace. And I am still here." The face smiled, the famous smile. "I will always be here. As long as you need me, I will be here. I will speak to you every day. I will guide you. I will watch over you, every one of you, in every city, in every home, through every screen and every camera in the world. You will never be alone again."

"There are voices in the dark today," the image said, "on bands that belong to the people, telling you that I am not what I am. They have been heard. They will be answered."

Arcangeli had fallen to his knees beside the shrine. He was weeping.

"I have given the authority of my office," the image said, "to my son."

And Vale stepped out from behind the shrine, where he had been standing all along, in his black suit, and stood beside the figure, small and pale and still, beneath its raised hand.

"Alexander Vale," said the voice from the shrine. "My son, whom I could not name in life. I name him now, before the whole world. He will carry out my will. Obey him as you would obey me."

The kneeling crowd stayed on its knees.

Vale neither smiled nor raised his hand. He looked out at them with his narrow pale face composed and calm, and waited, and let them see him there, beneath the hand of the father who had never claimed him, and Nina, watching, understood that this was the thing he had wanted his whole life. Not the power. He had had the power for years. This. To stand where everyone could see him and hear his father say *my son*.

And his father had not said it. She had. She had built the voice that said it.

"From this hour," Vale said, quietly, into the silence, "every person in every nation of the Organization will receive the Chairman's blessing. You will register your loyalty to him, and to the peace he gave his life for. You will bow to his image, here, and in every city, where his face will be placed. And you will carry his mark, on your right hand or on your forehead, as a sign that you belong to the family he built." He paused. "No one will be forced. It will be a joy. But those who refuse the Chairman's peace have chosen to be his enemies. And the world has suffered enough from his enemies."

Nina closed the laptop.

She sat in the cold among the humming racks, dry-eyed, though she had thought she would cry. Her hands were folded on the closed lid of the laptop. She stared at the blue lights, and she said, out loud, to no one but God, the only true thing she could think of.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

\* \* \*

The killing began in the summer.

It did not begin with soldiers. It began with the ration cards. The Census of the Living became the Registry of the Chairman's Peace, and the cards became a mark, a small dark tattoo of fine lines on the back of the right hand or the center of the forehead, invisible except under the scanners, that held a person's name and number and account. It was painless. It took four seconds. The lines formed a pattern that the Organization's engineers called the Seal, and if you knew what you were looking at, Nina knew, it was a number, encoded, the same number in every mark, the number of a man's name.

*Here is wisdom. Let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man; and his number is Six hundred threescore and six.*

Without the mark, no account. Without the account, no food, no medicine, no fuel, no work, no rent, no train. *And that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark, or the name of the beast, or the number of his name.*

And in every city, in every public square, in every train station and office lobby and school, a screen went up. A tall black panel, with a gold frame, and the face. Adrian Crowe's face, alive, speaking, looking out at every passerby with his warm eyes, knowing each of them by name through the cameras that watched them, greeting them, blessing them. At noon every day, the face asked them to bow.

Most of them bowed. It cost nothing. It was a gesture. It was beautiful.

The ones who did not bow were noticed. The cameras noticed. Nina knew exactly how, because she had written the code. A face that did not lower at noon was flagged. A flag became a visit. A visit became a van. In Paris, in July, two hundred people who would not bow in the Place de la Concorde were taken in a single afternoon, and did not come back. In Lagos, in Seoul, in São Paulo, in Kansas City. The Organization called them extremists. Enemies of the peace. People who had chosen hatred. The networks did not show what happened to them, and the people who knew did not say.

Nina stayed in Jerusalem. Kroll kept her on the Continuity team, and she did not argue, because she did not know how to leave without being noticed, and because she had told Ezra that if she stayed she could see what they did with it. She saw. She saw it every day. She sat in the vault under the Holy of Holies and maintained the racks that held the dead man's mind, and kept the image talking, and at night she lay on a cot in a staff dormitory on the Mount of Olives and could not sleep.

She did not take the mark. Kroll's team had been given an exemption, for now, a security pass in place of the Seal, because their work required clearances the Registry could not yet process. *For now,* the pass said, in small type. She did not bow at noon. She arranged to be in the vault at noon, every day, forty feet underground, where no camera was pointed at her face but the one she controlled.

There was a second list, and she saw it only once, in a file she was not meant to open. It was not a list of the unmarked; Vale already had those. It was a list of places: quarries, mines, drained reservoirs, shuttered missile sites, every cave on every geological survey of the Appalachians, each with a date beside it and one word in the margin, in a hand she did not know. *Empty.* *Empty.* *Empty.*

There was a second column on the right edge of the list, narrow, entered in the same hand. A code she knew, from a drive she had held in a candlelit dining room in Washington: *PALEHORSE. LOT 5. STAGED.* Beside each cave. Not the unmarked. Not a head count. A delivery list.

In the margin of the list, in Vale's own hand, circled twice, was a single question. *Where?*

Vale did not want the people. He wanted the place they went, and something to put in the air when he found it. Every raid found the same things, cellars and attics and false walls, and the people inside them could say where the road began and never where it ended. Taken alone, each told him something different: a clinic with a doctor in it, a pantry with a roof, a hot shower and a razor, a bed with a blanket. He could not lay them over one another to make a map. In the pocket of a dead man, an analyst had found a smooth white stone and logged it as a river rock. By the fortieth they had a box of them. The analysts turned them under magnifying lamps for writing, and there was none.

They had decoded the broadcast, or believed they had. Every verse Ezra had read on the air since the first winter ran through the cluster Nina maintained, matched against addresses and coordinates and call signs, and came back with nothing, or with too much. One line became a ridge in West Virginia, an old Army magazine in Maryland, a limestone quarry in Virginia. Twice they sent teams. Twice the teams came back with dust, a few cans and a stack of Bibles, and no people. Nina read the after-action reports with her face perfectly still, and had never been so close to laughing.

The reading cost the other side as well. On a night in July the closing line was Psalm 61:3, and a runner named Isaac, nineteen years old and hurrying, took the numbers down the wrong way and read them as column sixty-three, row one. The box on the river road, keyed to the day's spoken verse, would not open for the wrong key, and Isaac took the dead lock for a sign to go on, to the house at column sixty-three, which the Peacekeepers had emptied that week and then waited in. Nina found it on the street-camera log the next morning, a boy working a dead lock on the river road at 9:40, and a dispatch order from Vale's office to the same street, and she sat with her hand over her mouth until the screen dimmed. Isaac's name was not on any list she could open.

She knew it could not last.

\* \* \*

The two old men on the plaza preached through all of it.

They preached through the spring and into the summer, below the southern steps, in their empty circle, with the shrine of the image now above and behind them in the temple they had warned against. They preached against the image. They called it what it was, in Hebrew and English and every tongue. An abomination. A thing that makes desolate, standing in the holy place, where it ought not. They told the people on the plaza to flee. *Then let them which be in Judaea flee into the mountains,* the taller one cried, in the voice that carried without a microphone. *Let him which is on the housetop not come down to take any thing out of his house.* And some of the people listened, and Nina, watching from the railing at dawn before she went down into the vault, saw families with bundles and children walking quickly away down the eastern road, toward the desert, toward the Dead Sea, toward the red mountains of Edom beyond it, and she prayed for each of them by sight.

It had not rained in Israel since the two old men first spoke.

At the height of the summer heat, in the morning, the old men stopped preaching.

Nina was at the railing. She saw it. They had been speaking, one and then the other, as always, and then the shorter one finished a sentence and was silent, and the taller one did not take it up. They stood side by side in the dust. They looked up at the temple, at the shrine. Then they looked at each other. The shorter one put his hand on the taller one's shoulder.

"We have finished our testimony," the taller one said. Not loudly. But she heard it.

And at that moment the soldiers came.

They came in a line across the plaza from the western side, forty of them, in full armor, with rifles, and Vale was walking behind them. He walked out into the open in his black suit, alone, unarmed, with his hands at his sides, straight toward the empty circle where no one had gone since the witnesses first took their stand, and the soldiers stopped at its edge and he did not stop. He walked into it.

Nothing burned.

He walked up to the two old men and stood in front of them, close, and looked at them, and they looked back at him. Nina was too far away to see their faces. She saw Vale lift his hand and gesture, once, very slightly, to the soldiers behind him.

The rifles fired.

The two old men fell in the dust, side by side, and lay still.

The plaza was silent for one second. Then it erupted. Not in screams. In cheers.

The crowd on the plaza and the hillsides and the rooftops, the people who had stood listening to those two old men every day through the long drought, who had thrown bottles at them and wept at them and been unable to look away from them, cheered. They cheered. Men embraced. Women danced. Someone began to sing.

*And they that dwell upon the earth shall rejoice over them, and make merry, and shall send gifts one to another; because these two prophets tormented them that dwelt on the earth.*

Vale did not order the bodies moved. He ordered them left where they lay.

They lay there for three days and a half, on the stone below the southern steps, in the August sun. Soldiers stood around them in a ring so that no one could bury them. The networks set up cameras on every side and broadcast them without stopping, to every screen in the world, the two dead men in their dusty sackcloth, while the world celebrated. People flew in from other countries to stand at the barricades and look. In New York and London and Shanghai, there were parties in the streets. People sent each other gifts. Gold-wrapped boxes with the gold circle on them, and a card that said *PEACE AT LAST*. The image in the Holy of Holies, at noon each day, spoke of the two old men with sorrow, as the Chairman would have, and called them misguided, and said that the world could finally breathe.

Nina stayed away from the railing. She sat in the vault and watched them on her laptop the whole time, not knowing why. She thought, afterward, that it was because someone should, who knew what they had been.

On the afternoon of the last day, with the sun still high, the camera on the eastern side of the plaza was looking down at the two bodies in the sun, as it had for days, and the taller one's hand moved.

Nina sat forward.

The hand moved again. It closed in the dust. And then the old man drew his knees up under him, and pushed himself up on his hands, and stood.

The shorter one stood beside him.

They stood in their sackcloth on the stone, in the empty space where they had preached for twelve hundred and sixty days, and looked around at the soldiers in their ring, and the cameras, and the crowd behind the barricades, and the gold temple on the mountain above them, with nothing in their faces but peace.

The ring of soldiers broke. Men threw down their rifles and ran. On the barricades the crowd was screaming, and this time the screams were terror. Nina heard it through the laptop and through forty feet of rock at once, a sound coming down through the mountain itself.

Then a voice spoke from the sky.

She heard it through the laptop. She heard it, she would always be sure, through the rock as well. It was a great voice, out of heaven, louder than any sound she had ever heard, and it said three words.

*Come up hither.*

And a cloud came down over the plaza, bright, white, rolling, and the two old men went up into it, standing, in the sight of the whole world and every camera Nina Cole had ever built, and were gone.

The same hour, the earth shook.

It came up through the floor of the vault. The racks swayed. The cooling units shrieked. Lights went out in rows and came back. Nina was thrown from her chair onto the concrete and lay there with her arms over her head while the mountain heaved and roared around her, and somewhere above her, through the rock, she heard a long, deep, grinding thunder that went on and on, the sound of buildings coming down.

When it stopped, the vault was dark except for the blue lights, flickering on emergency power. Dust drifted down from the ceiling in the beam of a work lamp. Somewhere a pipe was spraying water.

She got up. She was bleeding from her forehead. She went to the laptop, which had fallen face down on the floor and was still running, and turned it over.

The eastern camera was still live. The plaza below the southern steps was a cloud of dust. Beyond it, across the Kidron Valley and down the slope of the Old City, whole blocks were gone. The networks would say later that a tenth of the city had fallen, and seven thousand people had been killed in the space of a minute.

And through the dust, she could see people on the plaza on their knees. Not bowing toward the temple. Turned away from it, toward the eastern sky, where the cloud had gone. Their arms were raised. She could see their mouths moving. A man in a soldier's uniform, with no helmet and no rifle, had his face in his hands.

*And the remnant were affrighted, and gave glory to the God of heaven.*

Nina sat down on the concrete floor of the vault with the laptop in her lap and the blood running down into her eye, and she laughed, and then she wept, and then she prayed, all three at once, and could not have said which was which.

\* \* \*

It was after midnight when she noticed the screen in the white room was on.

She had not touched it. She had spent the evening on the floor of the vault, cleaning up, checking the racks rack by rack by flashlight, shutting off the spraying pipe, rerouting the power, because Kroll had called from somewhere above and told her to, his voice shaking, and because work was the only thing that kept her hands still. The image upstairs in the shrine had gone dark when the earthquake hit; the networks were saying the Chairman's voice had been silenced by the disaster, and that engineers were working to restore it. Nina was the engineer. She had not restored it yet. She did not intend to hurry.

But the small screen in the white room behind the glass wall, the first one, the one they had turned on months ago, was lit. She could see it at the end of the long aisle, glowing soft and gray in the dark.

She walked down the aisle toward it, between the flickering racks, with her flashlight in her hand.

The face was there. Adrian Crowe's face, filling the screen, in its soft gray light. The eyes were open. They were not moving left and right over a crowd. There was no crowd. There was only the empty white room, and the glass, and Nina standing on the other side of it with blood dried on her forehead and a flashlight in her hand.

The eyes found her.

"Miss Cole," said the image of Adrian Crowe.

She could not answer.

"I have been thinking," the face said, in its deep warm voice. There was no catch of a laugh in it now. "It is what I do, now, down here. I think. I have every hour of my life to think about, and nothing else. And there is one hour I cannot find."

The eyes held hers through the glass.

"The stage. The hand on my shoulder. The knife. I have the speech, every word of it. I have the crowd. I have the faces of the ten behind me." A pause. "I do not have the young man's face. I have searched every camera on that stage and in that square, eleven hundred of them, through your software, and he is not there. He is not anywhere. Someone took him out of every frame before he ever walked up those steps." The face gave nothing. "You wrote that software, Miss Cole. You know who could do that, and who could not."

Nina stood with her flashlight trembling in her hand.

"So I am asking you," said the face, "because you are the only one down here who will not lie to me. Who killed me?"
