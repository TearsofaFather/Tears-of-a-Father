## Chapter 30: The Image Speaks

> *And he had power to give life unto the image of the beast, that the image of the beast should both speak, and cause that as many as would not worship the image of the beast should be killed.* — Revelation 13:15

**Word study: *image* — εἰκών** Greek *eikōn* (Strong's G1504). A likeness; a copy made to stand in for the real thing. It is where we get the word *icon*. Paul uses the same word for Christ, *the image of the invisible God*, the one true likeness of the Father. The beast's kingdom would make its own *eikōn*, a copy of a dead man, and demand that the world bow to it.

*Whereupon the king took counsel, and made two calves of gold, and said unto them, It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem: behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt… And this thing became a sin…* (1 Kings 12:28–30)

\* \* \*

Nina did not move.

She sat at the technician's desk with her hands in her lap, in her down jacket, in the cold and the humming dark, and looked up at Alexander Vale, and the only sound in the vault was the cooling units on the far wall breathing their freezing air across three rows of blue lights.

Behind Vale, the ten stood in the aisle in their dark suits and waited. Kroll stood beside the nearest rack with his glasses in his hand, not looking at her.

"No," Nina said.

It came out small. She said it again, louder, so that it would be true. "No. I won't."

Something moved in the ten, a stir, a shifting of feet. Kroll closed his eyes.

Vale stood easy. He looked down at her with his dark, patient eyes, and then he smiled, barely. It was not an unkind smile. It was the smile she remembered from the plane, and she liked it no better now.

"Miss Cole," he said gently. "You already have."

He reached past her, around her shoulder, and touched a key on her laptop. Not the one she would have touched. A key she had never used, on a line of code she had never written, that someone had added to her system in the night while she slept in this chair.

The racks changed their sound.

It was a small change, a shift in pitch, a deeper hum that went down into the floor. Every blue light in the vault went white for a moment, all three rows at once, and then blue again. The cooling units roared. And down the aisle, through the narrow window in the steel door, the white room filled with a soft gray glow.

"It never needed your hands," Vale said, without raising his voice. "Only your work. You built it very well. Thank you."

He straightened up and turned away from her and walked down the long aisle between the racks toward the steel door, and the ten fell in behind him, and Kroll, and none of them looked back. At the door Vale keyed six numbers without looking at the pad, and the bolts drew back.

Nina sat at the desk and watched them go. Her hands were shaking so badly she put them under her thighs.

*Obadiah hid the prophets,* Ezra had said. *He didn't build the idol.*

She had built it.

She got up and went down the aisle after the others, between the racks, toward the light. She had to see what she had made. The steel door was swinging shut behind Kroll. She caught it with her shoulder and slipped in after them, and no one told her to leave.

\* \* \*

Light rose out of the lens in the white room floor, a column of it, soft and gray, and in the air above the chair it gathered into a head.

It was Adrian Crowe's head. Not a photograph, not a recording. A head and nothing under it, hanging at the height of a standing man, with the big handsome head of white hair and the tan and the lines around the eyes, in a soft gray light that came from nowhere. The eyes were closed. The face was slack, as if asleep.

Then the eyes opened.

They looked left and then right, taking in the small white room, the chair, the ten men in dark suits standing in a row along the wall, and Kroll, and Vale in front of them all in his black mourning suit, and behind Vale, at the back by the door, a slight woman in a down jacket with her hands pressed over her mouth.

The lips moved.

"Where am I?"

It was his voice. It was exactly his voice. Deep and warm and unhurried, with the little catch of a laugh waiting just under it, even now. Nina had heard it for a thousand hours, in the vault under Third Avenue, through a whole winter. She had tuned the catch herself. She had spent four days on the catch.

The ten did not answer. Kroll made a small sound in his throat.

Vale stepped close to the light.

"New York," he said. "Under the house you built. You were wounded, days ago, in Jerusalem, during your speech. Do you remember?"

The face hesitated. The eyes went somewhere far away, and Nina, watching, knew exactly what was happening behind them, in the racks, in the code she had written: a search, ten billion records deep, through every word Adrian Crowe had ever spoken and every thought he had ever written down, for the last thing he knew.

"I remember the stage," the face said slowly. "I remember the crowd. I was speaking about the sacrifices." A pause. "And then someone put his hand on my shoulder."

"Yes," Vale said.

"Am I dead?"

The cooling units went on breathing.

"Your body is dead," Vale said. "You are not."

"Then where are my hands?"

"You don't need them now."

Nina looked at the head hanging in the light, with nothing under it, and thought of Ezra at the dinner table with Luke open in front of him. Jesus on the evening He rose, in a locked room, with His friends sure they were seeing a ghost: *Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have.* (Luke 24:39) He had come back with hands they could hold, and the holes still in them. This had come back as light, and there was nothing in the room to hold.

The face considered him with Adrian Crowe's eyes, and a look came into them that Nina had seen in the archive a hundred times, in old footage, in old interviews, whenever anyone asked the Chairman about his family. A guardedness. A shutter coming down.

"Alexander," the face said.

"Yes."

"You're wearing black."

"I'm in mourning," Vale said. "For my father."

And the face went blank.

The ten stood frozen, and so did Kroll. Nina stood at the back of the room with her arms crossed tight and understood, all at once, what she had seen in the data a year ago and not understood: a sealed file in the medical records, a laboratory report from a clinic in Geneva, decades old, with two names on it and a single number, ninety-nine point nine eight percent, and a private letter from Crowe to a woman in Lyon, never sent, that began *I cannot give him my name*.

"You told no one," Vale said to the light. "In all those years. You paid for the schools and the flat in Paris and the doctor when she was dying, and you sent a man to her funeral instead of going yourself. You hired me at twenty-nine as an analyst, and promoted me six times. You let me write every speech you ever gave. You sent me to speak for you in every room you would not walk into yourself. And you never once claimed me. You never once told anyone that I was your son. Not even me. I had to find the file myself." He laid his palm against the glass. "But you are my father. And I am your heir. And the world needs you now, more than it ever needed you alive."

The face looked at him with Crowe's eyes, and said nothing.

Then Vale turned from the light, and looked at the ten, and at Kroll, and last of all at Nina, standing at the back.

"Let them hear him," he said.

\* \* \*

They let the world hear him two days later, at noon in Jerusalem, on every screen there was.

The racks stayed where they were, three rows of them under Third Avenue, humming, with Nina sitting among them at her desk because Kroll told her to. The model stayed in Virginia. What went out over the wire was the face. In every city the Organization had put up its tall black panels in gold frames, in squares and train stations and office lobbies, and at noon every one of them went dark at once, and then came up again with a dead man looking out of it.

Nina watched it from the vault, on her laptop, alone. Every word was coming up out of the data center in Virginia, through the racks around her, and out along the line she had wired to the shrine forty feet under the temple. In Jerusalem the doors of the Holy of Holies stood open, and in the center of its floor the lens she had set there lit, and the head rose out of it three times the size of life, and every panel on earth carried it. Vale stood beside it, just inside the edge of the picture, in his black suit. The control room upstairs kept the line open. She kept the mainframe running.

"My children," said the image of Adrian Crowe. "Do not be afraid."

She had every street camera she wanted. She opened them. Union Station. Times Square. A market in Dakar. A train platform in Seoul. In every one of them people had stopped walking. They stood with their bags in their hands and their faces turned up to the panels, and some of them were crying, and some of them went down on their knees on the pavement without anyone asking them to.

*And all the world wondered after the beast.* (Revelation 13:3)

She watched them stop for a face she had fed for a thousand hours, for a voice whose every wire she knew, and something in her that had been sitting very still for years finally stood up.

"I told myself *Obadiah,*" she said, to no one. "I told myself I was in here to see what they'd do with it. I knew it was wrong the first week. I knew it in the vault. I knew it in every elevator with Kroll. And I kept on, one careful excuse at a time." Her voice cracked. "The whole world is standing still for my work, Lord. There's no excuse left bigger than that. I can't stay in the room with it. Get me out of here. Get me out alive, with what I know."

"They struck me down," the image said, in that deep warm voice, with the catch of a laugh under it. "They put a knife in my back in front of my own people, because I offered them peace. And I am still here." The face smiled, the famous smile. "I will speak to you every day. I will watch over you, every one of you, in every city, in every home, through every screen and every camera in the world. You will never be alone again."

"There are voices in the dark," the image said, "on bands that belong to the people, telling you that I am not what I am. They have been heard. They will be answered."

"I have given the authority of my office," the image said, "to my son."

Vale took one step, into the middle of the picture, beside the column of light.

"Alexander Vale," said the voice. "My son, whom I could not name in life. I name him now, before the whole world. He will carry out my will. Obey him as you would obey me."

Vale neither smiled nor raised his hand. He looked into the camera with his narrow pale face composed and calm, and waited, and let them see him there, beside the father who had never claimed him, and Nina, watching, understood that this was the thing he had wanted his whole life. Not the power. He had had the power for years. This. To stand where everyone could see him and hear his father say *my son*.

And his father had not said it. She had. She had built the voice that said it.

Then Vale sat down. There was a chair beside the light, plain, cut from olive wood, and he sat in it, in the Holy of Holies, with his hands on its arms and the dead man's face hanging in the air at his shoulder.

*Who opposeth and exalteth himself above all that is called God, or that is worshipped; so that he as God sitteth in the temple of God, shewing himself that he is God.* (2 Thessalonians 2:4)

Nina had heard Ezra read that verse a hundred nights and wondered whose face it would have. She knew his water glass. She knew the quarter turn.

"From this day," Vale said, quietly, from the chair, "the Chairman will speak to you at noon, and you will kneel to hear him. Every person in every nation of the Organization will register in the Chairman's Peace, and carry its Seal, on the right hand or on the forehead, as the key to every account. No one will be forced. It will be a joy. But those who refuse the Chairman's peace have chosen to be his enemies. And the world has suffered enough from his enemies."

Below the temple, on the plaza, the two old men had stopped speaking.

Nina saw it in the corner of her screen, on the eastern camera, where she had kept them for three years without telling anyone why. The shorter one finished a sentence and was silent, and the taller one did not take it up. They stood side by side in the dust and lifted their eyes to the temple door, where the olive-wood chair stood. The shorter one put his hand on the taller one's shoulder.

"We have finished our testimony," the taller one said.

Nina counted it without wanting to, from the morning the covenant was signed. Twelve hundred and sixty days. It came out to the day.

Vale rose from the chair. He came out through the doorway and down the white steps, and forty soldiers in armor fell in behind him, and every camera on the mountain turned to follow, because he had told them to. He walked into the empty circle where nothing had gone in for three and a half years, and nothing burned. He stood in front of the two old men, close, and lifted his hand once, very slightly.

The rifles fired.

*And when they shall have finished their testimony, the beast that ascendeth out of the bottomless pit shall make war against them, and shall overcome them, and kill them.* (Revelation 11:7) She had heard Ezra read it on the air the first winter and had not believed a word of it.

The plaza cheered. Vale had the bodies left where they fell, in the street of the city where their Lord was crucified. Within the hour every network had them on a loop, two dead men in dusty sackcloth on the stone, and by evening people were sending each other gifts in gold paper.

Nina closed the laptop.

She sat in the cold among the humming racks, dry-eyed, though she had thought she would cry. Her hands were folded on the closed lid. She stared at the blue lights, and she said, out loud, to Jesus, the only true thing she could think of.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

\* \* \*

Then she opened the laptop again, and went to work for the last time.

She did not touch the model. She went under it, into the control layer she had written herself, the plumbing nobody upstairs had ever asked to see, and she built a door. One account with no name on it, buried in a routine that ran the cooling logs, that would wake only for her and only from outside. Through it she could read anything the vault read: the logs, the camera network, the Chief of Staff's files, the white room. And at the bottom of it she wrote one more thing, a single line that did nothing at all unless it was called, and that if it was ever called would cut Continuity off from every screen it spoke through and leave it talking to an empty room.

The key to it was a verse. Michael had taught her that. A password could be taken off a woman. A verse she knew by heart could not.

She thought about which one. Then she typed it, and tested it twice, and hid the door behind a thousand lines of maintenance code, and wiped her hands of the session.

She kept the receiver on the shelf turned down to a whisper. At nine that night it gave out the low line of the cello, and then Ezra, reading from the hidden rooms. He was in Jonah. He could not have known. *And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not.* (Jonah 3:10)

Nina sat among the racks and thought of a kitchen in Baltimore, and sweet potatoes, and the cows in sackcloth. Then she cried.

When she was done crying she remembered the other thing Ezra had said, on the phone, on the second night. *Come out of there.*

She put on her coat.

\* \* \*

She went out at one in the morning, with nothing in her hands and her laptop in a grocery bag.

The building was dark. The guard at the night desk was asleep in his chair. She went down the back stairs and out through the loading dock onto Forty-Second Street and walked east toward the river, praying with every step. She had sent one word to the bakery on Atlantic Avenue before she left the vault, the word Kyle had given her years ago for the night she needed it. She did not know if anyone had read it.

She kept to the shadows along the buildings, under the cameras she had taught to see.

A hundred yards from the Tudor City stairs, a black car pulled up beside her on the empty street and stopped.

The rear window came down.

"Miss Cole," said Alexander Vale. "It's very late. May I give you a ride?"

She got in. There was nothing else to do. Two men in dark suits had stepped out of the front of the car and were standing on either side of her, not touching her, waiting.

The back of the car was dark and smelled of leather. Vale sat in the far corner with his legs crossed and a glass of water in the cup holder beside him, which he did not drink. He turned it a quarter turn. The car pulled away from the curb and drove slowly up First Avenue in the dark, going nowhere in particular.

"You're leaving us," Vale said.

It was not a question. Nina said nothing.

"You think I didn't know about your weekends, Miss Cole? The trains to Washington? The old library on Kennedy Street? The radios in the old cabinets? The work orders that went missing, season after season?" He smiled, very slightly. "I knew about you the week you started. I knew about the carpenter before you did. I have known about every false wall he ever built."

"Then why," Nina said. Her voice barely came out. "Why didn't you—"

"Stop you?" Vale turned the question over. "Because you were gathering them. Don't you see? That's what I needed. Scattered, they're impossible. A believer here, a believer there, in a million apartments, in a thousand cities, and every one of them has to be found one at a time. It takes years. I don't have years. I have a very short time." He said it calmly. "But you and your carpenter gathered them. You put them in rooms. You gave them radios, and a song to listen for, and a time, nine o'clock, every night. You taught them to trust each other and tell each other where they were. You made them a church." He spread his hands. "You did my work for me, Miss Cole. I only had to let you."

The car turned a corner. Nina could see the Organization's tower through the window, across First Avenue, lit gold from top to bottom.

"Why tell me?" she said.

"Because it doesn't matter anymore. Go to him. Tell him I know his name. And his son's name. And the name of the song." He looked at her with an expression she could not read at all. "I'm letting you go, because you'll go straight to him. And wherever you go, Miss Cole, I'll be watching. You'll lead me home."

The car stopped at the foot of the Tudor City stairs. Through the window Nina could see a young man in a blue relief jacket standing beside a white van, holding a round loaf of bread. Kyle.

"Your friends are here," Vale said. "Good night. Thank you for your work."

She got out. The car pulled away and was gone.
