## Chapter 27: Head Knowledge

> *Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble.* — James 2:19

**Word study: *believest* — πιστεύεις** Greek *pisteueis*, from *pisteuō* (Strong's G4100). To be persuaded; to put your weight on something. It is the same word John uses in *whosoever believeth in him should not perish*. James is not mocking belief. He is saying that agreeing a bridge exists is not the same as walking across it, and that the devils agree with every word of the Creed, and are terrified.

*I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes.* (Job 42:5–6)

\* \* \*

Walt Simmons had been a typesetter most of his working life, and he still woke every morning at four-fifteen, the hour he used to catch the bus to the *Star*.

There was no bus now. There was no paper. He woke anyway, in the dark, on his own side of the double bed, and lay listening to the house, and then got up and put on his cardigan and his slippers and went down the stairs one at a time with his hand on the rail and his cane hooked over his wrist, and made coffee in the percolator as his wife had made it for all those years, two scoops and a pinch of salt. The gas still worked, most mornings. When it did not, he made it on the camp stove Ezra had brought over in December.

Then he sat down at the round table under the kitchen window and opened the ledger.

It was a bound accounting book, green cloth, with red and blue ruled columns, the kind the *Post* had used for payroll before computers. He had found a box of them in his basement in December. On the first page of the first one, in his careful square capitals, he had written THE RECORD, and under it the verse he had set for himself.

*Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it.* Habakkuk 2:2.

Every morning since December, he had written down what had happened in the world the day before, as exactly as he could find it out, with the date in the left margin and the verse it belonged to in the right. He got the news from the radio, from the Organization's broadcasts in the morning and the BBC on shortwave at night, from what Nina brought down from New York in her head and would not write anywhere else, from Elijah's runs through the city, from the line outside the relief center on Bladensburg Road. He wrote it all in red pen. Facts on the left. Scripture on the right. Nothing in between.

*WEEK 6, FROST. Organization announces Census of the Living extended 30 days. 61% of District registered. Unregistered to be "visited."* Revelation 13:16.

*WEEK 7, SLEET. Rice $41 for 10 lb. at Bladensburg relief market. Wheat futures suspended.* Revelation 6:6.

*WEEK 7, SLEET. Earthquake, Peru, 7.9. Second in 5 weeks.* Matthew 24:7.

*WEEK 8, HARD COLD. Crowe addresses Collective. Calls for "one ledger for one world." Applause 4 min.* Daniel 8:25. *By peace shall destroy many.*

*WEEK 9, HARD COLD. Fever in the nursing homes of Prince George's and Baltimore counties. "Isolated clusters." Health Directorate offers the "Verity Prevention Protocol," free, to the registered.* Revelation 6:8.

*WEEK 10, THAW. Forty-one dead in one week at a care home in Baltimore. The unregistered, turned away at the door of the clinic, are healthy. A reporter asks why. He is no longer on the air.* Revelation 6:8. *Behold a pale horse.*

It was the most useful thing he had ever done, Ezra said. Walt read from it on the air every other night, before Ezra read from the notebooks, so that the people listening could see the prophecy and the newspaper side by side, as Walt had always seen them. *This is the evening paper,* Walt would say into the cheap microphone in the coal bin, *and this is the Book that printed it first.* People wrote to them about it, in the strange ways people wrote now: notes left under the brick at the relief center, messages passed hand to hand. *Tell the old man to keep the record. It helps.*

Walt kept the record. He was good at it. He was the best in the city at it, probably. He knew where every verse was. He knew what every sign meant. On the radio, when a caller asked a question, he could answer it before Ezra had finished repeating it, chapter and verse, with the Greek if they wanted it.

And he did not pray.

He had never prayed. Not once, not in seventy-four years. Not as a boy, at his mother's knee in the row house in Shaw, where he had bowed his head and moved his lips and thought about baseball. Not at his wedding. Not in the hospital, years ago, when his wife lay in the bed with the machines breathing for her and her hand in his, and she had said, very faintly, *Walter, will you pray with me?*, and he had said, *You pray, honey. I'll listen.* And she had prayed, in a whisper, for him, and he had listened, and she had died two days later with her eyes on his face.

He knew the Book better than any preacher in Washington. He had read it cover to cover forty-one times. He had a red pen mark beside nearly every prophecy in it, a date, a clipping. He had known the Rapture was coming, he had known the season, he had read the signs on the front page of the *Post* every morning with his red pen.

And on the morning of the twenty-sixth of November he had been sitting at this table with his coffee when the horn sounded, and he was still here.

\* \* \*

They read at Ezra's dinner table every night at seven, and broadcast from the coal bin at nine.

By the end of January there were nine of them at the table, not counting the ones on the radio. Elijah, who had stopped drinking and started cooking, badly and with enthusiasm, for everybody. Nina, when she could get down from New York, which was every week or two, always by a different train and a different route, always with her phone left in a drawer at her apartment, running on Juno, so that the cameras would think she was home. Mrs. Okafor from next door, who had lost her husband and her twin daughters and had banged on Ezra's door on Thursday night and been turned away, and who came back in January and was let in. A young couple from the next block who had heard the broadcast on a car radio. A bus driver named Marcus. An old woman named Dolores who had been a member at Greater New Hope for forty years and had never once believed a word of it, and cried every night at the table because she did now and it was too late for her husband. Ezra did not let anyone tell her it was too late for her.

Walt took the end seat by the window, where he could see his own house across the street. He answered the questions. He kept the record. He brought the coffee in his thermos.

He noticed that when they prayed at the end of the night, he folded his hands and bowed his head and moved his lips and thought about the record.

No one else noticed, or if they did, they kept it to themselves. They thought of him as the old watchman, the one who had known before anyone. They brought their questions to him. When Dolores asked him, she took his hand. "Walter. Was he saved? Sixty years married. A deacon thirty of them. Tell me he went."

"The book says the dead in Christ rise first, Dolores," Walt said. "It doesn't say who was in Christ at the last. That was between him and God, in the last hour. I won't tell you what I don't know."

She held his hand across the table and thanked him. He went home that night and sat at his own table in the dark and could not get warm.

It was Ezra who said it to him, finally. Not unkindly. Ezra was not unkind anymore. It was the strangest thing about him, the thing Walt had watched happen across the street over two months and still did not entirely believe: the man who used to stand in his own front door with his arms folded and a smirk on his face now sat on the floor so Dolores could have his chair, and waited out every question to the end.

It was the second week of February. Everyone else had gone home. Walt was gathering up his ledger and his thermos, and Ezra was sitting at the table with the candle, and he said, without looking up from the notebook in front of him:

"Walt. When was the first time you prayed?"

Walt stood with his thermos in one hand and his cane in the other.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not trying to—"

"I know what you're trying to do. I know exactly what you're trying to do. I've watched you do it to nine people." His voice came out harder than he meant. "You don't have to do it to me. I know the book. I know it better than you do."

"I know you do," Ezra said.

"I could quote you the whole road. Romans three twenty-three, Romans six twenty-three, Romans ten nine. I could quote you every verse on the Roman road and tell you who carved it. I know it, Ezra."

"I know you do." Ezra looked up at him then, in the candlelight, with eyes that were not judging him at all, which was worse. "Walt. You read me James two last week. On the air. You read it better than I could have. *The devils also believe, and tremble.*" He was quiet for a moment. "Do you tremble?"

Walt said nothing. He set the thermos down on the table so that it would not make a sound. Then he picked up his ledger and his cane and walked out the back door and across the dark street to his own house, and he did not sleep at all that night.

\* \* \*

He trembled. That was the truth of it. He had trembled since the morning of the twenty-sixth.

He trembled as the demons in James trembled, he thought, lying on his side of the bed in the dark. Because they knew. They knew exactly who God was, better than any theologian. They had seen Him. They knew every promise was true and every warning was true, and they knew precisely what was coming, and the knowing did them no good at all. It only frightened them. He knew what was in the second half of the book. He knew about the seals and the trumpets and the bowls. He knew about the mark and the image and the lake of fire. He knew, to the chapter, what happened to a man who knew all of this and did not repent. And the knowing lay on his chest at night, a locked chase of cold lead, and did nothing for him but make it hard to breathe.

He had thought, all his life, that knowing was the hard part. That the people who went to church and clapped and cried and did not know Daniel from Deuteronomy were fools, and that he, who knew, was the serious one. He had sat at his dinner table every morning for decades with the Bible beside the newspaper and his red pen uncapped, checking the world against the Word, and he had been proud of it. He had thought that was faith.

It was proofreading. That was all it was. He had proofread God for decades, and found Him accurate, and never once trusted Him with anything.

Somewhere past three he got up and went downstairs without turning on a light, and sat at the round table in the dark, and opened the drawer under it, the one he had not opened in years.

Her things were in it. He had put them there after the funeral, everything from her side of the bed and her purse and her pockets, all in one drawer, and closed it, and not opened it since. A pair of reading glasses with a cracked lens. A tube of hand cream. A rosary that had been her mother's, though she had not been Catholic. A church bulletin from Greater New Hope, folded small. A pen. And a little spiral notebook with a blue cardboard cover, the kind they sold at the drugstore for a dollar, that he had never opened, because he had known what it was and had not wanted to know what was in it.

He opened it now, in the dark, and tilted it toward the faint light from the street.

It was her prayer list. He had known that. He had seen her writing in it every night of their married life, sitting up on her side of the bed with her knees drawn up and her reading glasses on the end of her nose. Pages and pages of it, in her loose, looping handwriting, names and dates. The children down the street. Her sister. The pastor. People from the hospital. A boy from the neighborhood who had gone to prison. And on every page, on every single page, at the top, before anything else, the same name.

*Walter.*

It was on the first page, dated the year they married. It was on the last page, dated the week she went into the hospital, the handwriting shaky. All those years. Every night. At the top of every page.

Beside his name, on some pages, she had written verses. Not prophecies. She had never cared much about the prophecies; she had teased him about his newspapers and his red pen. Short verses, simple ones, the kind they taught children, most of them in Jesus' own words. *For God so loved the world.* *Come unto me.* *I stand at the door, and knock.* And on the last page, in the shaky hand, beside his name, there was a single line.

*Lord, he knows all about You. Let him know You.*

Walt Simmons sat at his dinner table at three in the morning, with his dead wife's prayer list open in his hands, and after seventy-four years of checking, he had nothing left to check.

He did not know how to pray. That was the plain fact. He knew every prayer in the Bible. He could have recited Solomon's dedication of the temple, or Daniel's confession, or the Lord's own prayer in Matthew and Luke, and noted the differences. He did not know how to say a single word to God himself, in his own voice, about his own self. He had never done it. He was afraid to.

He was afraid, he understood, sitting there, because he had always believed that if he ever really spoke to God, God would answer, and he would have to do whatever God said. That was why he had kept to the newspaper and the red pen. A man could check prophecy all his life and never once have to obey anything.

*I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear.*

Job had known everything too. Job had been right about God for thirty chapters, and his friends had been wrong, and it had not been enough. It took the whirlwind. It took God Himself, speaking, asking him questions he could not answer. *Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.* And Job, who had understanding, who had the best arguments in the book, put his hand over his mouth.

*But now mine eye seeth thee.*

Walt got up from the table. He left his cane. He went up the stairs in the dark with both hands on the rail, one step at a time, and down the hall into the bedroom, and around the foot of the bed to her side, the side nobody had slept on for years, and he got down on his knees beside it.

Getting down took some doing. His knees were seventy-four years old, and they had never done this, and they cracked, and he had to hold on to the mattress. When he was down, he had no words. He knelt there with his forehead against the edge of her side of the bed, where her pillow still was, and the smell of her hand cream was still faintly in the pillowcase after all those years because he had never washed it, and he could not think of a single word.

So he said her words.

"Lord," he said. His voice was a cracked whisper. "I know all about You. Let me know You."

Nothing happened. No light came, no voice, and Walt had expected none; he knew from the book that it did not usually go that way.

But he kept kneeling. And after a while, he found that he could say something else, something of his own. It came out of him slow and halting.

"I proofread You," he said. "For decades. I checked You. I never trusted You. I was too proud to need You, and too scared to obey You, and I hid behind knowing. You were right about everything and I knew it and it didn't matter to me." He stopped, and started again. "She asked me to pray with her. At the end. And I wouldn't. I said, You pray, honey. I'll listen." He could not go on for a minute. "I don't want to listen anymore. I want to come. If You'll still have an old fool who knew the whole road and never walked a step of it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm Yours. Jesus. I'm Yours."

He said the Name. He had set it in type ten thousand times. He had never once said it to anyone.

And something answered.

It was not a voice. It was not a light. Later, when he tried to tell Ezra, he would only be able to say that it was like the moment in the composing room, all those years ago, when he finished setting a long column of type and locked it into the chase and pulled the first proof, and held the damp sheet up to the light, and every letter was where it belonged. Not like that. It was that. Everything in him that had been set backward and upside down for seventy-four years, every letter of him, turned around at once and settled into its place, and he was being read. He was being read and understood, all the way through, and loved anyway. He knelt on the floor of the bedroom beside his wife's side of the bed, and he wept, for the first time since the funeral, and whether it was grief or joy he could not tell, and he did not care.

*Now mine eye seeth thee.*

He knelt there until the window turned gray.

He put his palm flat on his breastbone where the surgeon's long scar ran, and felt the new valve beat against it, steady and ordinary and not his own work. The surgeon at Brandick Memorial had rebuilt his valve. A new heart he had never asked for. *A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.* (Ezekiel 36:26)

\* \* \*

Then he went downstairs and made the coffee, two scoops and a pinch of salt, and sat down at the round table, and opened the ledger.

He left the news for later. For the first time in the record, he wrote something else.

He wrote the date in the left margin in red. *WEEK 12, SNOW.* And then, in the space where the facts went, in his careful square capitals:

*WALTER J. SIMMONS. BELIEVED.*

And in the right margin, the verse.

*Job 42:5.*

He sat looking at it. It was the second thing he had written in his life that was not a verse or a headline. The first had been a license plate, in October, in the margin of the morning paper, and he had hoped that night that he was a foolish old man. He had not been. He was not a fool this morning either.

Then he turned the page, because there was still news to write, and the record still had to be kept.

\* \* \*

He told Ezra that evening, before anyone else came. He made no speech of it. He set the ledger down on the table and opened it to the morning's page and turned it around and pushed it across.

Ezra read it once and then again. He got up, came around the table, and put his arms around the old man, in his chair, and held on, and Walt let him. Neither spoke. Elijah came in the back door with a pot of something that smelled like burned onions and stopped in the doorway and looked at them and said, "Well, all right," then set the pot on the stove and went back out to stand on the porch in the cold for five minutes to give them the room.

That night, when they prayed at the end of the reading, Walt prayed out loud. It was not very good. He stumbled, and repeated himself, and called God *sir* once by accident, the way he had called the managing editor *sir* for decades. Dolores laughed through her tears and squeezed his hand. Mrs. Okafor said *amen* after every sentence, to help him along. And when he finished, Ezra said *amen* too, and looked at him across the table with an expression Walt had seen once before, on the face of his own father, the day he got hired at the *Star*.

On the air, at nine, Ezra handed him the microphone.

Walt had read the record on the radio every other night for two months. His voice was known now in a few thousand kitchens in Northeast Washington, the old man with the newspaper, who knew where everything was. Not a word he had put on the air was anything but a fact or a verse.

He held the microphone in both hands, as Ezra did.

"This is Walt," he said. "The old man with the record. I want to tell you something that isn't in it." He cleared his throat. "Some of you are like me. You know the book. You've known it for years. You knew all this was coming. You can tell the people around you exactly what every sign means. And you're still here, and you're wondering why, and you're scared."

He stopped. Ezra was watching him. Nina, in the corner by the transmitter, had stopped typing.

"Knowing about Him won't save you," Walt said. "I'm seventy-four years old and I just found that out last night, on my knees, on the floor by my dead wife's side of the bed. The devils know everything I know. They believe every word, and they tremble. Trembling won't save you either. You have to come to Him. Not to the book about Him. To Him. Tonight. On your knees, if your knees still bend. Mine barely do." He tried to laugh and could not quite. "That's all. That's the whole record tonight. Back to Ezra."

He handed the microphone back. His hands were shaking.

Ezra took the microphone. Nina's hand was already hovering over the keys, waiting for the reading that always came next, and Ezra set the red notebook down on his knee, closed.

"No sermon tonight," he said. He looked at the old man, who was staring at his own hands. "My son wrote something. Some of you know it already. Tonight we'll just listen."

Nina pressed the key. The low line of the cello rose out of the steel box and out along the wire and into the snow, and in the coal bin nobody said another word.

That week there were more notes under the brick at the relief center than there had been in all of January. Elijah brought them back in his coat pockets, a stack of them, folded small, and laid them on the dinner table in front of Walt. Most of them said only a few words. *I knew it too. Last night I prayed.* *My father was a pastor. I never believed him. I do now.* *Tell the old man thank you.* One of them was written on the back of an Organization ration form, in a child's careful printing. *Mr. Walt, my grandma says you are a watchman too now. Is that true?*

Walt read that one several times. Then he took out his red pen and wrote in the record, under the day's news, in the right margin, where the verses went:

*Ezekiel 33:7.* And beside it, small, the only joke he had ever written in it: *Late hire.*

\* \* \*

The news came on the fourth of March, at six-thirty in the morning, on the BBC, through the static of the shortwave set on the kitchen counter.

Walt was sitting at the round table with his coffee and the ledger, and he almost missed it, because the announcer read it at the end of the bulletin, as a curiosity, in the bemused tone the British kept for things they could not place.

"There are two men in Jerusalem," the announcer said, "whom the Organization's channel has not once mentioned."

"They came from nowhere anyone can determine, on the morning after the covenant was signed, and took up a position on the broad stone plaza below the Temple Mount, near the new offices of the Council for Universal Moral Law, and they have preached there every day since. They are old, and bearded, and barefoot, and dressed in rough sackcloth the color of dust. They speak in Hebrew, and in Arabic, and in English, and some say in every language at once, so that every listener hears them in his own tongue. They speak against the Council and its covenant. They have called its president a false shepherd. They call on the city to look on Him whom they have pierced.

"On the second day, the Organization's security services attempted to remove them from the plaza. What happened next is disputed. Witnesses say the first two officers to lay hands on the old men were burned, though no one can say by what. One witness, a German tourist, says that fire came out of their mouths. The officers have been taken to hospital. The two men have not moved from the plaza since.

"And on the third day, a curious meteorological footnote. The men declared, before thousands of witnesses and every camera in the city, that no rain would fall on the land of Israel until they had finished their testimony. None has. The rainy season in Jerusalem runs from November through March. This year it never came."

The bulletin moved on to the weather in London.

Walt sat with his pen in his hand. He did not need to look it up. He had set it in type himself, in nineteen seventy-nine, for a full-page advertisement a church in Anacostia had bought in the Sunday paper, in thirty-point Caslon, centered, with a border of thin rules.

*And I will give power unto my two witnesses, and they shall prophesy a thousand two hundred and threescore days, clothed in sackcloth… And if any man will hurt them, fire proceedeth out of their mouth, and devoureth their enemies… These have power to shut heaven, that it rain not in the days of their prophecy.*

He wrote it down. Facts on the left. Scripture on the right.

*WEEK 15, THAW. Two men in sackcloth, Jerusalem, below the Temple Mount, since the morning after the covenant. Fire. No rain all winter.*

*Revelation 11:3–6.*

And then, because it was the first thing that occurred to him to do, he set down his pen, and folded his hands on the open ledger, and bowed his head, and prayed for two old men he had never met, half a world away, who were standing on a stone plaza in the sun with every gun in the beast's kingdom pointed at them, and telling the truth.

When he looked up, there was a car parked across the street, in front of Ezra's house, that had not been there when he sat down.

It was white, with the gold circle of the Organization painted on the driver's door. Two people were sitting in it. One of them held a tablet up to the windshield, aimed at the Pattersons' front window, where Kambria's lamp was still burning.
