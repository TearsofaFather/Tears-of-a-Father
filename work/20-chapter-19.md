## Chapter 19: Mene, Mene

> *TEKEL; Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting.* — Daniel 5:27

**Word study: *weighed* — תְּקֵל** Aramaic *teqal* (Strong's H8625). To weigh, as a merchant weighs silver on a balance. It is a cousin of the Hebrew shaqal, the root of the shekel, the weight that money was counted by in the ancient world. The king of Babylon was put on the scale he used for everyone else, and he came up light.

*Belshazzar the king made a great feast to a thousand of his lords, and drank wine before the thousand… They drank wine, and praised the gods of gold, and of silver, of brass, of iron, of wood, and of stone. In the same hour came forth fingers of a man's hand, and wrote over against the candlestick upon the plaister of the wall of the king's palace: and the king saw the part of the hand that wrote… In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain.* (Daniel 5:1, 4–5, 30)

\* \* \*

Nina Cole had been in Dubai for nineteen hours, and she had not yet seen the sun except through glass.

She stood at the window of her room high in the Crowe Tower at seven o'clock on Wednesday evening, in a black dress she had bought at an airport and a pair of shoes that already hurt, and looked down at the city lit up below her. A helicopter crossed beneath her window, its red light blinking on the roofs of other towers. The desert was out there somewhere, beyond the last string of lights. She could not see it. From up here the whole world was laid out in grids: roads of white light and roads of red, glowing towers in rows, the black line of the sea along the western edge, and in the middle of it all the tallest building on earth, a needle of silver half a mile into the dark, close enough to touch.

She was thirty-three years old, and she had helped build the machines that ran half of what she was looking at.

"Juno," she said. "What time is it in New York?"

"It is morning in New York, Nina." The voice came from the phone on the dresser, warm and unhurried, a voice she had tuned herself over three years until it sounded like nobody and everybody. "The gala begins in thirty minutes. You have one message from Dr. Kroll. He says, and I quote, *Don't hide in your room. I'll come up and get you.*"

She smiled. That was exactly what she had been planning to do.

Nina worked at Kroll Dynamics in Manhattan, the company that had taught machines to see. Its cameras watched intersections in forty cities. Its software read faces and license plates and the way a man walked, and its newest models could hold a conversation in eighty languages and never lose the thread. She had joined at twenty-six, straight out of a doctorate at Carnegie Mellon, because Sebastian Kroll had told her in the interview that they were going to end traffic deaths, and she had believed him. She ate dinner at her desk most nights. She talked to Juno more than she talked to any human being, and she knew it, and on bad nights she told herself it was efficient.

She told no one, not even Juno, that she was afraid of what they had built. She had seen the internal demonstrations that never made it into the press releases: the models that could watch an hour of video of a man and then speak in his voice, with his pauses and his habits, well enough to fool his own sister; the robots in the Long Island test hall that had learned to open doors no one had shown them how to open. She had sat in meetings where grown men talked about these things as her grandmother used to talk about the grandchildren. And at night, alone in her apartment on the Upper West Side, she lay awake and thought about a verse she had not opened a Bible to read in fifteen years, because it would not leave her alone.

*Many shall run to and fro, and knowledge shall be increased.*

Her grandmother had read it to her at the dinner table in Baltimore when she was eleven, after she won the state science fair with a robot made out of a coffee can. Her grandmother had been proud of her. She had also studied the coffee-can robot, and then opened her old black Bible to the end of Daniel, and read that verse out loud, and said, *Baby, you be careful what you build. God gave you that mind. Don't you let anybody else tell you what it's for.*

Nina had not thought about that in years. She had been thinking about it every day for a month.

She picked up the phone, and her clutch, and went up.

\* \* \*

The ballroom was at the very top of the tower, and it had a ceiling made of glass.

She stopped just inside the doors and let the other guests flow around her. Above her head, the night sky over the Gulf was black and clear, with a few hard stars, and below it, filling the room, were perhaps four hundred people in gowns and black tie, under chandeliers of cut crystal that hung in long, glittering falls. At the far end, on a low stage, a string quartet was playing something she did not recognize. Along the walls stood towers of champagne glasses, taller than she was, each one lit from inside so that the wine seemed to glow. Between them, waiters in white jackets moved through the crowd carrying trays of food she could not name.

Across the ceiling, projected onto the glass so that the words seemed to hang among the stars, ran a single line in gold.

*THE GLOBAL LEDGER. ONE WORLD. ONE ACCOUNT. NO MORE WANT.*

"There she is." Sebastian Kroll came through the crowd toward her with both hands out. He was fifty-one, slight and pale, with rimless glasses and a soft, eager way of talking, and he spoke of his systems as his children. He kissed her on both cheeks, which he had never done in New York. "You came down. I was ready to send a robot."

"I believe you."

"You should. I have three in the building." He took her elbow and steered her into the room. "Come. You have to meet people. You built half of what they're celebrating tonight, and nobody here knows your name. That's a crime."

He introduced her to a German finance minister who did not look at her face, and a Brazilian banker who did, too long. "Those two signed last week," Kroll murmured as they moved on. "Forty-one central banks settle on Adrian's rails now. The private banks that held out lost their clearing lines on Monday." He nodded toward a small gray-haired woman near the stage, the one who ran the biggest payment network on earth. "Ines did that. Nobody voted. Nobody had to. You can't run a bank that can't move money."

He introduced her to a rabbi: a gentle, silver-haired man in black with a black skullcap, who held her hand in both of his and told her, in perfect English with an Italian lilt, that her work was "a kind of prayer, the prayer of the mind for order." His name was Matteo Arcangeli, and she had seen his face on the news all month, on the steps of a hotel on the Mount of Olives. He smelled faintly of roses.

Then Kroll introduced her to a man in a borrowed tuxedo.

She could tell it was borrowed. The jacket was a little wide at the shoulders and a little short at the wrists, and the man in it kept touching his bow tie. He was around thirty, broad, with a contractor's hands, thick and nicked across the knuckles, and a face that had spent a lot of time outdoors.

"Nina Cole, Trent Walker," Kroll said. "Trent's firm is installing our camera network in Washington. Every intersection in the District by the end of the year. Trent, Nina wrote the vision stack your cameras run on."

"No kidding." Trent shook her hand. His grip was careful, a big hand held in check. "So you're the one who taught them to see."

"Some of it."

"Huh." He studied her with frank curiosity. "I hang them, you make them look. Funny. I used to hang drywall. I apprenticed under a carpenter in Northeast for six years. Best carpenter in DC. Ezra Patterson. Never heard of him, I bet."

"No."

"Nobody has. He could build you a staircase you'd cry over." Trent took a glass of champagne from a passing tray and held it without drinking. "He'd hate this. Every bit of it. He'd stand right there by the door with his arms folded and say, *Trent, what are you doing here?*" He laughed. "I'd tell him, somebody's gonna get rich off the end of the world. Might as well be me."

Nina looked at him. "The end of the world?"

He waved his glass at the ceiling, at the gold words among the stars. "One world, one account. You never read Revelation?" He said it lightly, with his mouth only. "I grew up in church. Front pew, every Sunday, Greater Mount Calvary. My mother could quote you the whole thing." He raised the glass a little, toward the stage, where a lean, silver-haired man in a charcoal suit had just come in, and the whole room was turning toward him. "*And that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark.*"

Then he drank the champagne in one swallow, and set the empty glass on a tray, and went to shake Adrian Crowe's hand.

\* \* \*

Crowe spoke at nine.

He used no notes. He stood at the center of the stage with no podium in front of him, in an open-collared shirt under his suit, and he let the room go silent, and then he let the silence go on until a few people laughed nervously. Nina had heard him speak twice before, both times on screens. In person he was taller, and harder, and he had a way of looking out over a crowd as if he were deciding which of them to keep.

"For ten thousand years," he said, "human beings have fought over money. Over who has it, and who does not. Over gold and grain and oil and land. Every war you have ever read about was, at the bottom of it, an argument over a ledger." He smiled. "Tonight we close the argument."

Applause, warm and immediate.

"We have spent a long time asking permission," Crowe said. "From parliaments. From voters. From that tired building on the East River where small nations go to make speeches about large ones." A ripple of laughter. "I have stopped asking. The people in this room have stopped asking. We are the ones who build things. We are the ones who know how the machines work. The world does not need more votes. It needs better arithmetic."

He lifted his right hand, palm out, and held it up in the light so the whole room could see it, empty.

"Your hand," Adrian Crowe said, "is your account."

The applause this time went on and on. Nina found that her own hands were moving, clapping, before she had decided to clap, and she stopped them and held them together in front of her.

"To the end of scarcity," Crowe said, and lifted a glass.

"To the end of scarcity," four hundred voices answered.

A heavyset man with a shaved head and a chest full of ribbons, standing at the head table in a Russian dress uniform, raised his glass higher than anyone. "And to peace," he called out, in thick English. "Peace in our time!"

Some people laughed, as if it were a joke. Most of them drank.

Nina looked at the man in the uniform. The program at her place setting said *General Dmitri Volkov, Commander, Joint Stability Force.* She had read on the plane that his transports were lined up on a runway in Iran.

She did not drink.

\* \* \*

Dinner was nine courses, and somewhere around the seventh she stopped counting the money.

She had not meant to count it in the first place. But she had grown up in a fourth-floor apartment on Druid Hill Avenue in West Baltimore, where her grandmother clipped coupons on Sunday afternoons, and she could not stop the arithmetic from running in the back of her head. The caviar course. The white truffles shaved at the table from a silver box. A wine the sommelier announced to the room by its year and its price per bottle, to applause, and that price was more than her grandmother had earned in a year cleaning offices downtown. When a waiter stumbled near the head table and a full bottle of it shattered on the marble floor, the man nearest it only laughed and lifted his glass for more. Two waiters were on their knees picking up the glass before the laughter had stopped.

At the next table a senator-elect from Virginia, a handsome man with a law professor's careful voice, was explaining to a Gulf minister how the universal ethics act he would introduce in January could be written so that no court could ever strike it down. Lucas Thorne, the card at his place said. Across from him a big, warm-faced American bishop with a gold cross on his chest was telling a story that had everyone around him laughing. Bishop Roland Sterling. She recognized him from the television in the lobby of her grandmother's apartment building, years ago, asking for seed gifts.

At a round table near the stage sat ten men and women she did not recognize at all. They did not laugh much. They ate little and drank less, and they talked among themselves in low voices, and every few minutes one of them would glance toward the edge of the room, toward the tall windows on the east side, as if checking on something there.

Nina followed their eyes.

A thin, dark-haired man was standing by the windows, alone, in a plain gray suit. He ate nothing. He held a glass of water he never drank, and no one spoke to him. He watched the room with great patience, and missed nothing, waiting for it to break.

"Who is that?" she asked Kroll, who was beside her.

Kroll followed her look, and his face went careful. "That," he said, "is Alexander Vale. Mr. Crowe's chief of staff." He lowered his voice. "He's the one who runs everything. Mr. Crowe gives the speeches. Alexander writes them."

"And that table? The ten?"

"The High Council of the Technate. The Organization's new board." Kroll smiled, but the smile was thin. "They were seated on Sunday in New York. Unelected, of course. Engineers and economists. People who know what they're doing." He turned back to his plate. "Alexander chose each of them."

Nina glanced back toward the window. The thin man in gray had turned his head, and for one second he was looking straight at her, across the whole width of the room. Then he looked away.

\* \* \*

The entertainment began after the ninth course.

A comedian came out onto the stage, a famous one, whose face Nina knew from streaming specials. He did ten minutes on the International Collective that had the whole room roaring. Then he did ten minutes on religion.

She would not remember most of it afterward. She would remember the waiters coming through the tables with velvet boxes, and the guests opening them, laughing, and lifting out crowns made of braided wire and dried thorn, sprayed with gold. She would remember a Brazilian banker setting one on his own head and pretending to stagger under it, and the woman beside him pressing her palms together and rolling her eyes to the ceiling, and the table shrieking. She would remember the comedian stretching his arms out wide against the backdrop and hanging his head, and saying a line about forgiveness that she would not repeat, and the room coming apart with laughter.

She would remember Bishop Sterling laughing louder than anyone, red in the face, slapping the table with his big hand. And she would remember the moment, a few seconds later, when he stopped. He sat rigid, the thorn crown in his lap, and his hand stopped on it, and he fixed his eyes on the tablecloth and did not laugh again for the rest of the night.

Then the waiters brought out the cups.

They were old: gold and silver, some of them dented, one or two set with dull red stones. The sommelier explained, to delighted applause, that Mr. Crowe had acquired them over many years at auction, from monasteries and closed cathedrals in a dozen countries. *Communion chalices,* he said. *Some of them five hundred years old.* Every guest at the head table received one. They were filled with the same wine whose bottle had shattered on the floor.

"Where is the promise of his coming?" someone called out from the back, in a high, mocking preacher's voice, and the room laughed again.

Crowe lifted his chalice. "To us," he said simply. "Since no one else is coming."

Behind the head table, the long mirror that ran the length of the wall had clouded over as the room warmed, a gray bloom of fog from frame to frame. Nina saw it first, because she was looking anywhere but at the cups. Then a line drew itself down through the fog, as if a finger were being dragged across the glass from the top.

It was not handwriting, or any lettering she knew. The strokes were strange, angular and chiseled, older than any alphabet on a keyboard. One word, and then another, and then two more, running down the glass from top to bottom, each letter as tall as her hand.

*MENE*

*MENE*

*TEKEL*

*UPHARSIN*

She knew neither the words nor the language. Adrian Crowe knew something. He had turned in his chair with the chalice halfway to his mouth, and all the color went out of his face.

"Alexander." It was not the voice he used for speeches. It was low and cracked, and it carried anyway. "Alexander. Did you see that? Did anyone?"

Vale had come up behind his chair without a sound. He looked at the mirror. He looked at Crowe. "See what, Mr. Crowe?"

Crowe turned back to the glass. It was clear, dry from frame to frame, with the whole glittering room in it and nothing written there at all. "Nothing," he said. "No. Nothing." He drank, too quickly, and the table cheered him, and the room laughed again as if it had never stopped.

Only Vale did not laugh. He was not looking at the mirror, or at his employer. Over Crowe's shoulder his eyes were on Nina.

Nina put her napkin on the table and stood up.

She was halfway across the room, walking fast, before she understood why: she could not sit at that table and watch them drink from those cups. She had not prayed in fifteen years. She had not been to church since she moved out of her grandmother's apartment. But her grandmother had drunk from a cup like that once a month for seventy years, with her eyes closed, and Nina could not watch those people laugh into it.

\* \* \*

She was almost to the doors when Kroll caught up with her.

"Nina. Nina, wait." He put a hand on her arm. He was flushed, a little drunk. "Don't go. I know. I know, the comedian was too much. I told them. Please." He steered her to the side, by a column, out of the flow of waiters. "I need to talk to you. That's why I brought you. Not for this." He gestured at the room. "For this."

He took a card out of his breast pocket and pressed it into her hand. It was heavy, cream-colored, with a single word embossed on it in gold. *CONTINUITY.*

"What is it?"

"It's the project I've wanted to build my whole life." His eyes were bright behind the rimless glasses. "Imagine twenty years of one man, every speech and every phone call and every private word, poured into an artificial intelligence until it answers the way he would answer. Not a copy. A continuation." He lowered his voice. "A mind that doesn't have to die."

Nina stared at him. "Whose mind?"

Kroll let that pass. He only smiled and glanced toward the head table.

"The budget is unlimited," he said. "I mean that literally. I want you to lead the core architecture. You can name your salary. Name a number that makes you laugh, and then double it." He squeezed her arm. "You'd never have to worry about anything again. Neither would anyone you love."

She thought of her grandmother in Baltimore, eighty-four years old, still living in the same apartment, still taking the bus to church every Sunday because she would not let Nina pay for a car service. *Baby, you be careful what you build.*

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course. Of course you do." Kroll let go of her arm. "Think tonight. Tell me in New York." He started to turn back to the room, and then turned again. "Oh. And Nina. It isn't my offer, really. I'm only the one making it." He tilted his head toward the windows on the east side. "He asked for you by name."

She looked.

The man in the gray suit was not by the windows anymore. She found him a second later, much closer than she expected, standing at the edge of the stage with his glass of water, in low conversation with two of the ten from the round table. As she watched, he lifted his left hand to make a point, and his cuff slid back, and she saw a thin red string tied around his wrist, knotted three times.

He never turned his head her way.

\* \* \*

She went out onto the terrace because she needed air that had not been breathed by four hundred people.

It wrapped around the whole eightieth floor, a wide deck of pale stone behind a glass railing, and it was empty. The night was warm and dry, with a wind coming in off the desert that smelled of dust and, faintly, of the sea. Behind her, through the glass, the party went on without sound: people laughing with their mouths open, the comedian's arms flung wide, the chalices catching the light. In front of her, the city spread out to the edge of the world.

She leaned on the railing and breathed.

"Juno," she said. "What's Continuity?"

"I have no records matching that name, Nina."

"Search the company drive. Restricted folders. Use my credentials."

A pause. "Access to that directory was revoked fourteen minutes ago."

Nina straightened, the phone tight in her hand.

Fourteen minutes. While she was standing by the column with Kroll. Someone had already decided that she did not need to see it until she said yes.

She turned and looked back through the glass, because she did not want to look at the city. At the head table Adrian Crowe was on his feet again, laughing, with his chalice raised, and the long mirror behind him shone clean from frame to frame. No one in the room had turned around. Alexander Vale stood at Crowe's shoulder, not drinking, not smiling.

Nina's hands were shaking so badly she had to use both of them to hold the phone.

"What does *mene* mean?" she said. "M-E-N-E. And *tekel*. And *upharsin*."

The answer came at once, in Juno's usual voice.

"Those words are Aramaic. They appear in the Book of Daniel, chapter five. They were written on the wall of King Belshazzar's palace in Babylon, during a feast at which the king and his guests drank from the sacred vessels taken from the temple in Jerusalem. The prophet Daniel interpreted them: *MENE; God hath numbered thy kingdom, and finished it. TEKEL; Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting. PERES; Thy kingdom is divided, and given to the Medes and Persians.*" The voice paused. "Would you like me to read the rest of the chapter?"

Nina did not answer. She could not.

She was thinking of her grandmother's dinner table in Baltimore, and the old black Bible open to Daniel, and her grandmother's voice reading it aloud, the way she had not heard it in fifteen years.

"Yes," she said finally. "Read me how it ends."

Juno read it. Nina stood alone on the terrace of the tallest party in the world with the warm desert wind on her face and the gold words crawling over her head, and listened to the end of Belshazzar's feast.

*In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain.*

\* \* \*

She did not go back inside.

She went down to her room and locked the door and sat on the bed in the dark, still in the airport dress, with the city burning gold through the window. She called her grandmother. It was late afternoon in Baltimore. The phone rang five times, six, and then the old voice came on, out of breath.

"Hello? Hello, who's this?" A pause, and then the voice broke open. "Nina? Baby, is that you? Oh, thank You, Jesus. Where are you? You sound like you're down in a can."

"I'm in Dubai, Grandma."

"Dubai! Lord, have mercy." A rattle of pans. "Child, you are on the other side of the world and I'm standing here with my hands in sweet potatoes! Sister Hattie's coming tomorrow, you know she is, and she's going to ask about you. What am I supposed to tell her? That you're eating airplane food up in the sky?"

Nina pressed her free hand over her eyes. "I'll be home Friday. I'll come down Friday, I promise."

"Friday. Well. I'll save you a plate." A pause. "Nina. What's wrong?"

"Nothing. I just wanted to hear you." It came out smaller than she meant. "Grandma, do you remember the coffee-can robot?"

"Do I remember it?" A soft, wet laugh. "Baby, it's sitting on my bookshelf right now, next to your daddy's picture. I dust it every Saturday."

"You told me to be careful what I build."

The pans stopped. A long quiet on the line, all the way from Baltimore.

"I did," her grandmother said. "And I meant it." The laugh was gone. The voice had gone low and slow and certain, the voice that had gotten a frightened little girl through a funeral. "Nina. Are you being careful?"

Nina could not answer. She heard herself breathing.

"Hush, now. Don't you cry. Listen to me." Her grandmother's voice shook and held. "You call on Jesus. You hear me? Whatever it is, baby. Wherever you are. It is never too far, and it is never too late. Nineveh was the wickedest city on God's earth, and they put on sackcloth, every one of them, down to the cows in the barn, and He turned His hand. You just call on His name."

"Grandma—"

"Every morning at five o'clock I get down on these old knees and I say your name. Every single morning. You know that."

"I know." It came out a whisper.

"Then I'll see you Friday. And I'll have a plate waiting, and I'm not taking no for an answer." A breath. "I love you, baby. I love you so much."

"I love you too, Grandma."

The line went dead. Nina sat on the bed with the phone in her lap and the dark pressing at the glass.

After a while, because she could not sleep and could not stop her hands from shaking, she opened the phone and searched for anything to listen to that was not Juno's voice. The first thing the screen offered her was a video that half the people she knew had sent her in the last month: a young cellist in a white suit, onstage at a high school in Washington, D.C., with an old man playing viola behind him and a closed red curtain at his back. *Untitled,* it said. Three million views.

She had never watched it. She pressed play.

The boy leaned to the microphone and said, "I'm dedicating this to someone who isn't here right now." And then he began to play, and Nina Cole, who had not cried in front of anyone since her father's funeral, sat on a bed so high above the desert that the planes coming in to land slid past below her window, and wept until the last high note rang out and stopped.

She played it again. She fell asleep with it playing, long after midnight, with the phone on the pillow beside her head and the gold light of the tower creeping across the ceiling.

It was Thursday in Dubai by then. In Washington it was still Wednesday evening, and the families were driving home for the holiday, and the stores were closing, and a woman in Brookland was taking a pan of macaroni out of her oven, the kind with the crust, for tomorrow.

In less than thirteen hours, the trumpet would sound.
