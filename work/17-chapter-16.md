## Chapter 16: Divisive

> *Then shall they deliver you up to be afflicted, and shall kill you: and ye shall be hated of all nations for my name's sake.* — Matthew 24:9

**Word study: *hated* — μισέω** Greek *miseō* (Strong's G3404). To hate, to detest, to persecute. The same word Jesus used when He said, "If the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you." The hatred in this verse is not for anything the believers have done. It is *for my name's sake*: for His name alone.

Daniel was preferred above the other presidents and princes because an excellent spirit was in him (Daniel 6:3). His enemies could find no occasion against him, *except we find it against him concerning the law of his God* (Daniel 6:5). So they had the king sign a decree, and Daniel, when he knew the writing was signed, went into his house, and his windows being open toward Jerusalem, kneeled three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, *as he did aforetime* (Daniel 6:10).

\* \* \*

The email had come on Sunday night, while her train from New York was somewhere south of Philadelphia, and she had gone over it again and again by the light of her phone.

*Please report to the hospital's administrative offices, fourth floor, at 8:00 a.m. Monday. This meeting is mandatory.*

At five minutes to eight on Monday morning she parked on the third level of the hospital's staff garage, in the corner by the stairwell where she had parked every day since the flu response began, and rode the elevator up to the fourth floor.

The office was at the end of a carpeted hallway that smelled of new paint. Linda Marsh, the director of Kambria's lab at the Centers for Disease Control, was sitting behind the desk. She had driven in from Hyattsville before dawn. A man Kambria did not know sat in the chair beside her, with a laptop open on his knees and a hospital lanyard that said *Human Resources*.

"Have a seat, Kambria," Linda said. She did not smile; she seldom did, but she had always been fair, and she looked at Kambria now with what might have been sorrow.

Kambria sat.

"This is Greg Tallis, from the hospital's HR office," Linda said. "I'm going to let him explain why we're here."

Greg Tallis was young, with careful hair and a careful voice. He turned his laptop a little toward her so she could read along.

"As you know," he said, "the hospital system adopted new Inclusive Spiritual Care Standards earlier this month. They're aligned with the interim principles the Council adopted at its opening session in Jerusalem, which our board formally endorsed. They apply to everyone who works in this building, including outside partners. Your team signed the agreement when the agency set up here."

"And our department adopted the same guidelines soon after," Linda said. "Every federal employee got the training module."

Kambria remembered the module. It had been forty minutes long, with soft piano music and a woman's soothing voice, and she had clicked through it on her phone in the lab at three in the morning.

"A complaint was filed on Saturday, and it came up through the Director of Special Operations and Infectious Disease Control," Greg said. "Because you're a federal employee, it landed with your agency as well as with us. Actually, it's a pattern of concerns. That you've been engaging in religious proselytizing with patients and their families. And that you've made statements to colleagues, in shared spaces, to the effect that only one religion is true, and that people of other faiths, or no faith, are..." He glanced at the screen. "Going to be punished. Several staff members have said they found this hostile. Divisive was the word used."

Kambria looked at him. Then she looked at Linda.

"A dying woman asked me to pray," she said. "On Saturday morning. Her whole family was in the room. Her son took off his cap."

"We're aware of the incident," Greg said, choosing his words. "And we understand the impulse. Nobody is questioning your compassion. But the standards are clear. Chaplaincy requests go to the chaplaincy office, and staff are asked to offer support in a way that's inclusive of all traditions. Closing a prayer in the name of a specific deity, in a way that implies exclusivity..." He spread his hands. "Some people experience that as harm."

"She asked me," Kambria said. "She had a cross around her neck."

"I understand."

"And my login stopped working on Friday," Kambria said. "At six o'clock in the evening. I tried to pull up the Petworth files from my phone and it told me my account had been suspended." She looked at Greg, and then at Linda. "Mrs. Bell didn't come in until Friday night. I didn't pray with her until Saturday morning. Who suspended my account before anybody had anything to complain about?"

Greg looked at Linda. Linda looked at the desk.

"I don't know anything about that," Linda said, lowering her eyes. Kambria believed her. Linda did not know, and she was the director of the lab.

"In the staff lounge," Kambria said slowly, "a friend asked me what I thought about the ceremony at the House of the Gate, and I told her. That's the statement? That I believe Jesus is the only way to the Father? Because He said it. That's not my idea. That's in the Bible."

Greg Tallis studied his screen.

Linda leaned forward and folded her hands on the desk. "Kambria," she said gently. "You're the best scientist I have. You know that. You've been the best in my lab for all these years. This isn't about your work. Nobody has ever said one word about your work." She paused. "But Atlanta is watching every complaint under the new guidelines. And you've been telling half this hospital the world is ending. Dana called me about it on her own, last week, before any of this. People are frightened."

"They should be," Kambria said. "I'm not trying to scare them. I'm trying to warn them. Those are different things."

"To them, right now, they're the same thing."

Greg cleared his throat. "The hospital is withdrawing your access to the building, effective today."

"And the agency is placing you on paid administrative leave pending a review," Linda said. "It's not a disciplinary finding. It's a pause. Usually two to four weeks." She held out her hand. "I'll need your badge."

"And the Petworth samples?" Kambria said. "The bacterium. Did anyone read my report?"

Linda's eyes went to the window and came back. "That's been reassigned," she said. "It's not your concern now."

For the first time, someone else spoke.

"Dr. Whitfield. How was New York?"

Kambria had not noticed him. He sat by the window in a chair pulled back from the desk, a pale man in a navy suit with a manila folder shut on his knee, and nobody had introduced him. Linda did not look at him. Greg Tallis had gone very still.

"I'm sorry?" Kambria said.

"Your train ticket. Union Station, Saturday, eleven-oh-five. Penn Station, Sunday, five-oh-five." His voice was pleasant. "Did you see someone? Anyone you'd like to tell us about?"

The room waited.

"I took a weekend in the city," Kambria said. "I saw a parade of flags. I prayed in a hotel room."

"Of course." He opened the folder an inch, and closed it. "Thank you, Doctor."

That was all. But Linda was staring at her desk now like a woman who has been told to stop looking.

Kambria stayed where she was.

She thought of the old men in Babylon going through Daniel's records, page by page, looking for anything they could use. *We shall not find any occasion against this Daniel, except we find it against him concerning the law of his God. (Daniel 6:5)* In her Sunday school Bible there had been a picture of those men, in turbans, with scrolls; she had colored their robes purple.

She unclipped her badge from the lanyard around her neck. Her photo on it was years old. She had shorter hair in it, and she was smiling. She laid it on the desk in front of Linda.

"Can I ask who made the complaint?"

"Complaints under the standards are confidential," Greg said. "To protect the reporting party."

Kambria nodded. She stood up. At the door she turned around.

"Linda," she said. "Can I tell you something? Not as your scientist. Just as me."

Linda looked at her, and nodded.

"He's coming back," Kambria said. "Soon. I'm not saying it to be divisive. I'm saying it because I love you, and I've worked beside you all these years, and I'd never forgive myself if I hadn't said it once."

Linda didn't answer. But she didn't look away either, and her eyes were wet when Kambria closed the door.

\* \* \*

Celeste was waiting by her car.

The staff garage was raw and concrete-gray and smelled of exhaust, and Kambria's car was on the third level, in the corner by the stairwell where she always parked. Celeste was leaning against the driver's door in her coat, with her red hair down and her arms folded against the cold. When she saw Kambria coming, she straightened up, and her face filled with sorrow. Her coat had fallen open, and clipped inside it, where Kambria had never seen it on the sixth floor, hung a blue lanyard and a federal badge: CELESTE MOORE. DIRECTOR, SPECIAL OPERATIONS AND INFECTIOUS DISEASE CONTROL. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Kambria read it twice.

"Oh, honey," she said. "I'm so sorry."

Kambria stopped a few feet away from her.

"You heard already," she said.

"I didn't hear." Celeste uncrossed her arms. "I knew." She took a breath. "I'm the one who said something, Kambria. I'm not going to hide it from you. You deserve better than that. And Friday night, your login. That was me too. You were pulling files that were not yours to pull. I couldn't let you keep going."

"You told them I prayed with Mrs. Bell." Kambria did not raise her voice. "You shut me out on Friday, before Mrs. Bell was even admitted. Before there was anything to complain about. You had the complaint ready, and you were only waiting for something to hang it on."

Celeste did not deny it.

"I told them you've been frightening people. For weeks." Celeste's voice was soft and earnest. "Tomás came to me, after you talked to him. He's nineteen, Kambria. He couldn't sleep. You told Dana and Paul and half the lab that they were going to be left behind. And then I walked into that room and saw you praying over that poor woman's family, telling them only Jesus..." She shook her head. "I love you. You know I do. I did this because I love you. You're exhausted. You haven't been yourself since Angela. You need rest. Nobody is punishing you. This is a chance to rest."

"Angela prayed for you," Kambria said. "Every Tuesday. She told you she would, and she did. She was still praying for you the week she died."

"I know."

"You sat with her every Tuesday. You sat by her bed. You knew her husband was sitting out in that waiting room alone." Her voice broke. "You were never just a volunteer."

"I did volunteer," Celeste said. "Every word of that was true. It just wasn't all of it. Atlanta asked me to keep an eye on the sixth floor long before Petworth, and I chose the one woman there I knew I could love." She said it gently, as if it were a gift. "I trusted *you*, Kambria."

Celeste's smile held a second too long.

"You can still trust me," she said. Then, lower, so that it barely carried across the concrete: "Let Petworth stay reassigned. Whatever Francesca left in your hands, put it down. I know what she left you."

Kambria went cold from her scalp to her heels.

"I never said her name," she said.

"No," Celeste agreed softly. "You didn't."

Behind them, an engine echoed up the ramp. Headlights swept across the concrete pillars, and a pickup truck came around the corner from the second level and slowed to a stop in the lane behind Celeste. It had a ladder rack on the back and a dent in the tailgate, and Kambria knew it. She had parked behind it in a driveway on a quiet street every Sunday for a year, with a casserole dish on the seat beside her.

Celeste glanced over her shoulder, and her face changed. It went warm and young.

"That's my ride," she said.

Ezra was behind the wheel.

He saw Kambria. She watched him see her. His hands tightened on the wheel, and for one second their eyes met through the windshield in the flat fluorescent light of the garage. Then he looked away, straight ahead, at nothing.

Celeste touched Kambria's arm lightly. "Get some rest, okay? I'll call you." She walked around to the passenger side and got in. Kambria watched her lean across the seat and kiss him, quickly, on the mouth. Ezra neither kissed her back nor pushed her away. He sat rigid, eyes straight ahead, and when she had settled back into her seat he took his foot off the brake and drove down the ramp, and the sound of the engine went around and around the concrete levels until it was gone.

Kambria stood alone in the garage.

The small silver car at the curb outside his house in October, with the red string hanging from its mirror. The new shirt on Thursday night, and the cologne. Elijah, turning his glass on the bar: *He won't tell me her name. You know what that means.* Celeste, glowing for weeks. Celeste, turning her phone face down.

She had watched it begin. A hospital waiting room, two cups of coffee, a hand laid lightly on his arm. She had seen the hunger in Celeste's face that day and told herself it was kindness, because that was easier, and she had said nothing. Not to Ezra. Not to Angela. Not to Celeste. And now Revelation 12:10 came back to her whole: *the accuser of our brethren… which accused them before our God day and night.* Celeste had not shouted. She had wept, and called it love, and filed it with a signature. Kambria thought, too, of Romans 14:13: *that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother's way.* Celeste had not blocked Ezra's road. She had only laid herself across it, soft and warm, where a grieving man would not see her until he had already fallen.

Kambria got into her car and closed the door and sat behind the wheel. She left the engine off. She knew what Angela would have said, had she been there in that garage. Angela would have said, *Pray for her, Kam.* And Kambria could not. Not yet. She sat in the unheated car, and for many minutes she could not pray at all.

\* \* \*

On the way home she stopped at the Safeway on Rhode Island Avenue, because she was out of milk.

The shelves were half bare. Handwritten signs hung in the dairy aisle: *LIMIT 1 PER CUSTOMER*. In the baby aisle, where the formula used to be, there was a row of naked metal shelves and a laminated recall notice. A young mother stood in front of the gap with a baby on her hip, reading it twice.

"Try the CVS on Twelfth," Kambria said. "They had some on Friday."

She stood and looked at the stripped shelves. *A measure of wheat for a penny.* Walt had written it in red in the margin of the business page some days back, and she had read it upside down at his table and thought it was a long way off.

\* \* \*

At home, in the daylight, she saw what she had been too tired to see on Sunday night. The deadbolt had been locked, as she left it. Nothing was broken. Nothing was missing. But in the spare room the stack of notebooks on her desk had been squared up, edges even, and Kambria never squared anything. The printed filing from Dubai was still taped to the wall, but the tape had been peeled back at one corner and pressed down crooked.

On her pillow, on the bed in her bedroom, lay a glossy color photograph. It was her spare-room wall, shot from above, every string and headline and verse sharp as a surgical field. Written on the white border in a neat hand was a time stamp. *Monday, 8:14 a.m.* She had been sitting in Linda Marsh's office at eight-fourteen.

She stood in the doorway without turning on the light.

Then she went to the shelf by the kitchen table and took down the blue concordance. The packing tape on the spine had not been touched. She ran her thumb down the spine and felt the hard, flat shape of the thumb drive still inside it, and her shoulders loosened. She put the book back exactly where it had been. She checked every window. She put a kitchen chair under the knob of the back door, as her grandmother used to, and felt foolish, and left it there.

That evening her phone would not stop buzzing. It was the Mount Zion prayer chain, forty women who for years had texted each other about casseroles and hospital visits, and tonight every message carried the same link to the same story from Channel 4. Kambria pressed play.

The story was about the standards. Everyone's, now: a school district in Oregon had suspended a teacher for leading a prayer at a football game, a video platform had removed several thousand sermons for "hateful conduct." The picture changed to a church sanctuary, and Kambria sat forward. She knew the pulpit. She knew the wooden cross on the wall behind it, and the stained-glass window with the lamb.

It was Mount Zion. It was Pastor Grant.

A few seconds of his sermon played with the sound low, and she knew which one it was. She had been in the fourth pew on the left. *Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me. (John 14:6)* Pastor Grant leaned on the pulpit with both hands, as he always did, and spoke evenly, without anger.

The clip ended. The picture cut to a man in a beautiful gray suit on a stage in front of thousands, hands lifted. *Bishop Roland Sterling, Kingdom Harvest Church.* "The only thing God cannot bless," he said, warmly, to great applause, "is division."

Kambria closed the video. The phone went on buzzing in her lap, one message after another, and she turned it face down on the cushion.

She got up and went to the spare room and came back with the ninth notebook, and she sat at the table and wrote, in pen, one verse under another, without stopping.

*…ye shall be hated of all nations for my name's sake. (Matthew 24:9)*

*…yea, the time cometh, that whosoever killeth you will think that he doeth God service. (John 16:2)*

*And whoso falleth not down and worshippeth shall the same hour be cast into the midst of a burning fiery furnace. (Daniel 3:6)*

*If the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you. (John 15:18)*

She looked at what she had written. It was only beginning. She knew that. Paul had written that something was holding it back, and would go on holding it back, until it was taken out of the way. She did not think it would be taken out of the way much longer.

\* \* \*

She could not stay in the house with that photograph on the pillow. A little before nine she drove to Mount Zion and found one light on in the basement.

Pastor Grant was alone in the fellowship hall, stacking folding chairs against the wall, four at a time, slower than he used to. He looked up when the stair door opened, and set the chairs down.

"Kambria," he said. "Sister Bell's son is a deacon at Shiloh. He called me this afternoon to tell me what you did for his mother, and then what happened to you after." His voice was tired and kind. "How are you, child?"

She started to say she was fine. Then she sat down on one of the chairs he had not stacked yet, still in her coat, because he was her pastor, and she told him everything. Mrs. Bell, Linda's office, the badge on the desk. The garage. Ezra's eyes through the windshield, and the truck going down the ramp.

He unfolded a chair across from her and sat, with his hands on his knees. He listened without a word. Then: "They took my sermon down. Twenty-two people have called today to tell me they're sorry. I told each of them the same thing. *If the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you.* We're not being treated any worse than He was, Kambria. We're being treated the same. That's not a punishment. That's fellowship."

She cried then, under the low ceiling where Angela had started a Thursday study with fourteen women, with her face in her gloves.

"Now," Pastor Grant said. "Let me pray for you. And then you're going to do the hardest thing, which is pray for her."

"I can't."

"I know. Do it anyway. Not because she deserves it. Because He told us to. *Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not. (Romans 12:14)* Say it until you mean it."

He laid a hand on her shoulder and prayed for her there, as he had prayed over Angela every morning in her hospice bed.

"Father, this is Your daughter Kambria. She laid down her badge this morning and did not lay down Your name. Hold her tonight. Heal what was done to her, and give her what she cannot find by herself: a heart for the one who did it. In Jesus' name."

When he said amen, she said it with him. He turned off the basement light, locked the side door behind them, and stood on the walk until her car started.

\* \* \*

At eleven o'clock that night, Kambria went to her kitchen window.

The window over the sink faced east, toward the rooftops of Brookland and the dark line of trees along the railroad tracks. The oil lamp sat on the sill where it always sat, burning low. She moved it to the counter. Then she unlatched the window and pushed it open.

The November air came in all at once. Down the block a dog was barking, and a car door slammed somewhere, and the air smelled of frost.

She knelt on the kitchen floor, on the old linoleum, with her elbows on the edge of the counter and the chill on her face. She had opened it because Daniel had, and because he had gone on praying the same way he always had, with the decree signed and the lions waiting. *…as he did aforetime. (Daniel 6:10)* She wanted to pray now exactly as she had before.

She prayed for Ezra first, as she always did. She prayed for Mia and Jalen, and for Loretta, and for Walt, and for Elijah with the napkin in his wallet, and for Monica and her boys, and for Tomás, and for Linda Marsh, and for Mrs. Bell's family. She prayed for Pastor Grant and Mount Zion.

Then she knelt on the linoleum, eyes closed, saying nothing more.

"Lord," she said finally. "I don't want to. You know I don't want to." She swallowed. "Bless Celeste. Keep her. Open her eyes. Don't let her be lost. In Jesus' name."

She felt nothing when she said it, no forgiveness, no warmth. She felt hollow and tired.

She said it again anyway. Then she got up off the floor, and closed the window, and put the lamp back on the sill.

She was turning off the kitchen light when her phone lit up on the counter.

It was Ezra. In a whole year he had never once written to her first. He had answered her messages, when he answered at all, with one word or a thumbs-up. She stood in the unlit kitchen and looked at his name on the screen until it dimmed before she opened it.

There was one word.

*Sorry.*

That was all. No name, no reason, nothing after it. She could not tell whether he meant the garage, or the closet, or the porch on Thursday night, or a year of casseroles taken at the door. Nor could she tell if he meant it at all, or if he had been drinking, or if Celeste was asleep in the next room.

She sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinets and the phone clutched in both hands, and she cried harder than she had in the church with Pastor Grant, or on Ezra's steps. And she did not know, even then, whether she was crying because it was so little, or because, after a year, it was something.
