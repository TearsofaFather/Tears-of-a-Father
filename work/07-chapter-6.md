## Chapter 6: The Deacon

> *Fathers, provoke not your children to anger, lest they be discouraged.* — Colossians 3:21

**Word study: *discouraged* — ἀθυμέω** Greek *athumeō* (Strong's G120). To lose heart, to be broken in spirit. It is built from *a-*, without, and *thumos*, the passion and fire inside a person. A child who is provoked long enough does not only grow angry. Something in him goes out.

*Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the LORD… And if any man said unto him, Let them not fail to burn the fat presently, and then take as much as thy soul desireth; then he would answer him, Nay; but thou shalt give it me now: and if not, I will take it by force. Wherefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD: for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.* (1 Samuel 2:12, 16-17)

\* \* \*

On Sunday, his mother's seventy-first birthday, Ezra put his children in the truck and drove out to Cedar Hollow, which he had not done since the funeral.

The city fell away behind them. The houses got farther apart. Past the last stoplight the county road gave up and turned to gravel, and Mia pressed her face to the window and counted cows out loud, and Jalen, in the back seat, took one earbud out to listen to her. It was the first time in a month Ezra had heard either of them sound like a child.

Loretta Patterson still lived in the white frame house on Old Ferry Road in Cedar Hollow where Ezra had grown up. The porch steps sagged in the same place. The chicken coop out back still leaned toward the creek. Inside, the plastic runner in the hallway was the same, and over the couch the same framed photograph still hung: Deacon Calvin Patterson in his Sunday suit, a white carnation in the lapel, one big hand on an open Bible at the deacons' table at Pilgrim Rest.

Ezra had stopped looking at that picture the year he was eight. He walked past it now without turning his head.

The house smelled of yeast. In the kitchen a big blue bowl sat on the counter under a damp dish towel, and the little television by the bread box was on with the sound low: Bishop Roland Sterling, twenty thousand members and a billboard over the Beltway, smiling into the camera, saying, "Sow your seed into this ministry today, and watch what God does with your harvest." Loretta reached past it and clicked it off without looking.

"Jalen, baby, take your sister out to the coop. There's eggs need finding." She waited until the back door banged. Then she pulled the towel off the bowl. The dough had risen high and pale and domed, pressing at the rim.

"Wash your hands," she said.

"Mama, I—"

"I'm seventy-one years old today, Ezra Calvin Patterson. Wash your hands."

He washed his hands.

She dumped the dough onto the floured counter, and it sighed as it went down. She showed him once: heel of the hand, push it away, fold it back, quarter turn. "Not like you're sanding it. Like it's alive. Because it is." He did it wrong. She slapped flour on the counter and made him do it again. Hands that could plane a door to a thirty-second of an inch did not know what to do with something that gave back.

"This was hers," Loretta said, watching him work. "Angela's. Friday nights. She called me the first year you were married, said, 'Mama Loretta, Ezra says nobody makes pizza like his mother, and I need to know what he means.' I drove into the city with a bag of flour and this bowl. She had flour to her elbows and she was laughing the whole time. Every Friday after that, for twelve years, that girl made this dough." She pushed a strand of gray hair back with her wrist. "She told me you never once said it was as good as mine."

"It was better."

"I know it was. You should have told her."

He kneaded. The dough went smooth under his hands, warm and soft, and the kitchen was quiet except for the slap and push of it and the chickens complaining out back and Mia's voice rising, *I found one! Jalen, I found one!*

"Put your weight in it," Loretta said. Then, in the same voice, without looking up from the pan she was oiling: "You've been dreaming."

His hands stopped.

"Don't stop. It'll tighten up." She set the pan down. "Your daddy dreamed too, at the end. Did you know that? Before he went over. He'd wake up at night shouting."

Ezra pressed the heel of his hand into the dough and pushed it away and folded it back. He looked through the doorway into the front room, at the photograph over the couch.

"Mama," he said. "Was he ever, at home, the man everybody thought he was at church?"

Loretta wiped her palms on her apron. She let the question sit, the way she always had. She folded the dish towel in half, and in half again.

"No, baby," she said finally. "Not one day of his life." She took the dough from under his hands, gently, and turned it once, and gave it back to him. "Keep working it. I'll tell you while it rises."

\* \* \*

Calvin Patterson worked for the railroad. Six days a week he left the house before dawn in his steel-toed boots and his gray work jacket and walked to the rail yard at Hollis Junction, where he coupled freight cars and threw switches in the bitter weather and came home after dark smelling of creosote and diesel. He was strong, and he was good at the work, and the men on his crew were a little afraid of him.

On Sundays he was Deacon Patterson.

Pilgrim Rest Church sat at the crossroads in Cedar Hollow, a red-brick building with a white steeple and a sign out front with changeable letters. In 1988, when Ezra was six, the sign said: *HELL IS HOT. JESUS IS COMING. ARE YOU READY?*

On Sunday mornings the sanctuary was full. Four hundred people packed the pews in their best clothes, the women in wide hats, the men in three-piece suits, and the air was thick with perfume and heat. Ezra sat in the front pew in a clip-on tie that pinched his neck. His mother sang alto in the choir. And his father sat at the deacons' table below the pulpit, in the place of honor, with his Bible open in front of him. Beside the pulpit hung the number board, black felt with white slide-in numbers: the hymn across the top, the psalm for the day under it, and the verse beneath that. Ezra could find his place in the book by those numbers before he could read the words around them, and he found it faster than anyone in the pew.

Calvin Patterson was a big man with a big voice, and when he led devotion before the sermon, the building shook. He could quote scripture for an hour without once looking down at the Bible in front of him. He knew more of it by heart than the pastor did, and everyone said so. He lined out the old hymns in his deep, rough voice, and he prayed long and loud about sin, about drinking and lust and the fire waiting for anyone who didn't repent, and the congregation shouted back, "Pray, Deacon! Tell it!" At the end of every service, when the pastor called for the closing prayer, Calvin stood at the front and lifted both hands high, and four hundred people lifted theirs with him. Ezra lifted his too, when he was small. Then he learned what those same hands did behind the front door on Friday nights, after payday. When he was eight, sitting in the front pew, he slid his hands under his legs and sat on them through the whole closing prayer, and he promised himself he would never lift his hands to God in any church for as long as he lived. He never told anyone why. He had kept that promise ever since.

At six, in the front pew, Ezra was terrified of his father, and terrified of God, and could not have said which was which.

\* \* \*

After service, the deacons counted the offering in the church office, and the last one to count was always Calvin.

One Sunday when Ezra was seven, he went looking for his father to ask if he could have a soda from the fellowship hall, and the office door was open a crack. Through the gap he saw his father alone at the big wooden desk with the offering plates in front of him, counting stacks of bills. He watched his father take a thick fold of twenties off the top of one stack and slide it into the inside pocket of his suit jacket. Then another.

Calvin looked up and saw his son's face in the gap of the door.

He neither jumped nor looked ashamed. He stood up, walked over, opened the door, and looked down at Ezra without a flicker of expression.

"What you see in this office," he said, his voice low and pleasant, "stays in this office. You understand me?"

Ezra nodded. He didn't understand. But he never told anyone. Not his mother, not the deacons, not Angela. He carried it for all those years, the picture of his father's big hand sliding the church's money into his jacket while the choir was still singing in the next room.

\* \* \*

At home, the Deacon was a different man.

In Cedar Hollow, people called him a man of God. Church mothers brought him sweet potato pies. Men stopped him on the sidewalk to shake his hand and ask him to pray for their sons. The railroad gave him a plaque for twenty years without a lost day, and the church gave him another for service, and he hung them both in the front hallway where every visitor would see them. But behind the front door on Old Ferry Road, he was an unjust man. He bought himself new suits every spring and gave Loretta forty dollars a week for groceries and told her to make it stretch. He decided who was right in every argument, and it was always him. He never said he was sorry. When anything went wrong at the yard or at church, somebody at home paid for it. Loretta and Ezra lived with a man the four hundred people in the pews never met, and they knew better than to tell anyone.

What Calvin had at home was a bottle.

He kept it in the cabinet over the refrigerator, behind the cereal, where the church would never see it. On weeknights it was one glass, sometimes two, after supper. On Friday nights, after he cashed his check, he stopped at the liquor store out at Hollis Junction on the way home, and by the time his boots hit the front steps everyone in the house knew what kind of night it was going to be.

Some Fridays Calvin was so drunk he could not stand, let alone drive, and when the cupboards ran empty it was Ezra who went for the groceries, sitting on a stack of phone books in the cab of the truck, barely able to see over the steering wheel.

Calvin did not need a reason. Spilled milk was enough. A look was enough. Loretta's voice a little too loud on the phone was enough. He hit with his open hand first and then his fist, and he hit Loretta more than he hit Ezra, and when Ezra was old enough to stand between them, he hit Ezra for standing there.

And while he did it, he quoted scripture.

"'Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands,'" Calvin would say, breathing hard, slurring the words, while Loretta held her jaw at the sink. "'Children, obey your parents in the Lord: for this is right.' You hear me, kid? This is the Word of God."

Ezra heard him. He learned the verses before he learned to read, and he learned them with the back of a hand. He knew, even then, that his father had them wrong. No one who had sat through as many sermons as Ezra had could miss what his father always left out: *Husbands, love your wives… ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath… (Ephesians 5:25; 6:4)* Calvin never read that part out loud. To Ezra at eight, God was a big man with a loud voice who quoted the Bible while he hurt you. God took the money when he thought no one was looking. God was one man in front of the church and another man behind his own front door.

Later, after Calvin had passed out on the couch, Loretta would slip into Ezra's room in the dark. She would kneel by his bed and lay her cool hand on his face over the bruise and pray for him in a voice so low he could barely hear it. Some nights she cried while she prayed.

"Your daddy's sick, baby," she told him once. "The drink makes him sick."

Ezra lay there and let her pray. But he couldn't understand why she was praying to the same God his father quoted. It seemed to him like asking the man with the bottle to make the hurting stop.

School was no refuge, though he tried to make it one. Ezra carried the house with him through the doors every morning, folded small, the way he carried his lunch. He learned which shirts had sleeves long enough. He learned which mornings to say he had fallen off his bike. He learned that a teacher who bent down and asked, "Is everything all right at home?" wanted him to say yes, and he said yes, because the man whose name was on the plaque in the church hallway would be waiting on the other side of any other answer. Who would believe a boy over Deacon Patterson? Who in that whole neighborhood would even try?

He stopped wanting to be like the other children, and then he stopped trying to be near them. He ate lunch at the far end of the table, and after a while he ate it on the back stairwell, where nobody came. He quit going out to recess. He sat on the cold step with his knees pulled up and watched the other boys run, as if he were watching them through a window. Something gray settled on him that year and did not lift, and it did not lift on weekends either, because the weekend was Friday night. A boy that age should not have known the word *tired* the way he knew it.

There was no one to turn to. That was the thing he understood most clearly, long before he had the words for it. His mother was in the same house taking the same blows, and he would not add his weight to hers. The church mothers patted his head and told him what a blessing his daddy was. His teachers saw a quiet boy with good manners and no friends. And the only God he had ever been taught to call on sounded exactly like the man on the stairs. So he called on no one, and he got very good at it.

He had not yet read the psalm that described him, but it was already true of him. *I looked on my right hand, and beheld, but there was no man that would know me: refuge failed me; no man cared for my soul.* (Psalm 142:4) He only knew that he was a child and tired in a way that sleep did not touch.

There was one thing in that house that Calvin never managed to ruin, and it came out of the oven on Sunday mornings.

Loretta got up before dawn to make her dinner rolls, and by the time Ezra came down the stairs the whole house smelled of warm flour and butter. The kitchen windows were fogged. A dish towel lay over the pan, and under it the rolls sat in rows, golden-topped, shining where she had brushed them with butter. She never measured. She worked the dough with the heel of her hand, folded it and folded it again, and would never say how. Women at church asked her for the recipe every year at the fellowship dinner, and every year she smiled and said, "Some things you keep." When she tore one open for Ezra it came apart in flaky layers, soft and smooth and sweet with butter, and for as long as it lasted in his mouth he forgot to be afraid. Even Calvin ate them quietly, on those mornings, and said nothing at all.

Years afterward he would meet a verse that sounded to him like that kitchen. *O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him.* (Psalm 34:8) He had tasted first. The trusting came much later, and it came slowly.

Whatever else that house taught him, it taught him to respect his elders. *Yes, ma'am. No, sir.* He stood when an older person came into a room. He gave up his seat on the bus. He never talked back to a church mother, not even the ones who patted his head and told him what a blessing his daddy was, and he kept his eyes down and his voice low when the deacons spoke. Loretta required it, and the church required it, and Calvin required it with his hand. But it took root in Ezra deeper than any of them knew, and it outlasted the man who had beaten it into him. Thirty years later he would still call the old man across the street *sir*, and still go quiet and respectful in front of a gray head, even when he was angry enough to break something.

It was in the Book, too, plain as a nail, in the nineteenth chapter of Leviticus, in a line he did not read until he was grown and recognized as already his: *Thou shalt rise up before the hoary head, and honour the face of the old man, and fear thy God: I am the LORD.* (Leviticus 19:32)

It was never that Ezra failed to love the beauty around him. He saw it all: the fogged kitchen windows, the light coming through the church's tall glass on a Sunday, the grain in a plank of scrap wood he found and kept under his bed. He loved it quietly, the way a person loves what he is not sure he is allowed to keep. What he could not do was accept the house those things sat inside. He would not call it his, and he would not call it normal, and so he lived in it the only way he knew, which was out of sight. He kept to the far end of the table and the back stairwell, to long sleeves and short answers.

If there was a place for a boy like that, the psalmist had named it. *Thou art my hiding place; thou shalt preserve me from trouble.* (Psalm 32:7) Ezra had yet to learn whose hiding place it was.

The day he came out of it he was fifteen. He carried his tray past the stairwell door and sat down in the middle of the long table, and stayed. He would not be that boy again, folded small and waiting to be passed over. Whatever it cost him, he would stand where people could see him.

Jesus had told the crowd on the hillside: *Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid.* (Matthew 5:14)

\* \* \*

The worst night came in October of 1990, a few weeks after Ezra turned eight.

It was a Friday, and for once Ezra was walking home with something to carry.

That week he had written his name on the sheet taped outside the gym at Coretta Elementary, under the heading *Boys' Basketball*, the pencil shaking a little in his hand. He had never played. There was no hoop at the house on Old Ferry Road and no one to throw a ball with, and at practice it showed. He traveled every time he picked the ball up. It slipped through his fingers or bounced off his shoes, and the other boys, who had been playing since they could walk, laughed, not meanly, only the way you laugh at something that cannot help being funny. The coach blew his whistle and told him to keep his elbow in.

At the end of that Friday's practice, with the sun going orange in the high gym windows, the coach lined them up for free throws. Ezra stood at the back of the line with his stomach tight. When his turn came he bent his knees the way he had been shown, and he shot, and the ball rose in a lopsided arc and dropped through the net without touching the rim.

For a second nobody said anything. Then the coach said, "Well, look at that," and somebody slapped him on the back.

He walked the mile of gravel home with that one shot in his chest like something warm he was trying to keep alive. He played it over and over: the ball leaving his fingers, the soft sound of the net. He was going to tell his mother. He might even tell his father, who liked sports on television, who might for once look up.

Years later, reading the prophet Zechariah on a cold night, he would stop at a single line and sit very still. *For who hath despised the day of small things?* (Zechariah 4:10) A lopsided shot in a school gym that nobody saw but a coach and a few boys. It had been small. It had also been the only thing all year that was his.

He heard them from the yard. The front door was shut, and it did not matter; the sound came through the clapboard. His father was home hours early, drunk by four in the afternoon, and he was already shouting before Ezra got his key out of his pocket.

"...four hundred of them, and not one says thank you! You think this house runs on prayer? Every dime in here is mine, you hear me, Loretta? Every dime!"

A smaller voice: "Calvin, please. The boy will be home."

Ezra stood on the top step with his hand on the knob. Then he opened the door anyway.

He dropped his bag in the doorway. His mother was on the floor of the hallway with her hand over her mouth and blood between her fingers.

Ezra walked past her and stood in front of his father.

He did not decide to do it. He was there, between them, eight years old and in his school shoes on the plastic runner, his school jacket still zipped to his chin, looking up.

Calvin stared down at him, swaying. Then he reached into the pocket of his work jacket and took out a pistol: a .357 Magnum, heavy and black and long in the barrel, the one he kept in a shoebox on the closet shelf and told Ezra never to touch. He thumbed back the hammer. He put the end of the barrel against Ezra's forehead.

It was cold. Ezra would remember that to the end of his days: how cold the metal was, and how it shook.

Loretta made a sound behind him. Ezra did not turn around.

He looked his father in the eye.

He was not afraid. He waited to be afraid, and the fear never came. What came instead was a flat, hard tiredness. He was sick of it. He was sick of Friday nights, and of his mother's face at the sink, and of the verses, and of the fear itself, and in that moment some small, cold part of him thought that if his father pulled the trigger, at least it would be over. And underneath that lay a second thing, one he understood only halfway: if he looked down now, if he cried or begged or stepped aside, it would never stop. Not for him, and not for her. So he did not look down.

The barrel trembled against his skin. His father's eyes were red and wet and wild. The kitchen clock ticked. A car went by outside. Ezra could smell the whiskey and the diesel on him.

Then Calvin's face changed.

It broke open. Ezra saw him see it: the boy, the gun, the blood on his wife's hand, and himself. His finger came off the trigger. He lowered the pistol and slammed it down on the hall table so hard the lamp jumped, and he stood there breathing with both hands braced on the wood. Then he turned and stumbled out the front door into the last of the daylight and left it standing open behind him.

Ezra closed it. He picked up the gun with both hands, because it was too heavy for one, and put it in the kitchen drawer under the dish towels. Then he got a wet washcloth and knelt down beside his mother on the floor.

That was the night Ezra understood that nobody was coming. Not his father, and not his father's God.

He never told anyone about that night. Not Angela. Not anyone. The shot he had made that afternoon went unmentioned too; it belonged to a boy who had been left somewhere in that hallway.

One person came that night, though he never knew what he had come for. Elijah Brooks was the boy from the next house down Old Ferry Road, a head taller than Ezra and twice as loud, and on the night the gun came out of the shoebox he did the one thing nobody else on that street did. He came. He had stolen his father's car, a 1970 Barracuda his daddy had fitted up to look like the black car in the horror movie he'd watched fourteen times, with the stripe down the hood, the scoop, and an engine that announced you two blocks away. Elijah was ten years old and had to sit on a phone book to see over the wheel. Ezra had come out to sit on the front step in the cold with no coat, and had not gone back in. Elijah saw him there under his mother's lit window, and he leaned across and shoved the passenger door open and said, "Get in."

He didn't ask. He drove them to Whitlock Creek Park, past the old bowling alley with its sign hanging by one bolt and its windows gone, and the two of them sat below the overpass with a pair of borrowed poles and a coffee can of worms, and fished for catfish until the light went. They caught three. Then Elijah let the engine roar and turned the radio up as loud as it would go, and Ezra, eight years old, put his arm out the window into the cold and laughed harder than he had laughed in his life.

They took the long way home, over the railroad tracks and past the jail and up onto the overpass they had been fishing under all evening. On the far side the road dropped toward a parking lot, and a man came around the corner in a pickup, on their side of the line. Elijah yanked the wheel. The Barracuda swung and overcorrected and the back end came around, and then there was a tree. Ezra remembered the sound of it. He remembered Elijah's head going through the windshield. He remembered the quiet after, and the radio still playing, and the smell of gas and cut grass, and thinking, with perfect clarity, that the feeling was back.

Elijah lived. He lay in the hospital for six days with his head wrapped and his jaw wired, and Ezra, with fourteen stitches in his forehead and a story ready for his mother, sat by the bed through most of them. Elijah's father mourned the car for a year and never once raised his voice about it, because the boy had nearly died in it. The doctors said it should have killed them both. On the fifth day Elijah wrote on a napkin, in crooked letters, *Luck or a reason?* and Ezra turned it over a good while before he wrote back, *Don't know.* He had printed it in square block capitals, every letter the same height, the way he would one day write every cut list. They never decided which. They stayed friends every day after that.

Years later, in Angela's Bible, Ezra would underline a line in Esther in pencil and write the name *Eli* in the margin. *And who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?* (Esther 4:14) And in Proverbs, a verse he marked the same night: *A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly: and there is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother.* (Proverbs 18:24) He never told Elijah he had underlined them. He did not have to. Elijah would have made a joke, and then gone quiet, and then said it was probably the other way around.

\* \* \*

After that night, the house went silent.

Calvin came home the next afternoon, sober and gray in the face, and did not say a word about it. There was no apology and no word of any kind. For days he came and went without speaking, and ate his supper without looking up, and slept on the couch in front of the television with the sound turned low. Tanks in the desert. Soldiers loading onto airplanes. A map of a country called Kuwait.

Calvin had been in the Army at eighteen, before the railroad, before Ezra was born. He had told the story of those years at church a dozen times: how he had been a soldier for his country before he was a soldier for the Lord.

In December, he announced that he was going back.

He told the congregation first, on a Sunday morning, standing at the deacons' table. His voice filled the sanctuary.

"Church, I have been called. My country is calling men of faith and courage, and I wore its uniform once, and I will wear it again. I can't sit here and talk about courage while other men's sons go to the desert in my place."

The church leapt to its feet. People cried. The choir sang.

Ezra, in the front pew, knew it was not courage. He had seen his father's face in the hallway that night. His father was not going to war. He was running from his own front door.

Calvin told Loretta and Ezra afterward, in the car.

Ezra was eight years old. He sat in the back seat and listened to his mother stifling her sobs in the front, and his father saying "It's done, Loretta, it's already done," and Ezra caught himself counting the Sundays until January, and slid his hands under his legs.

\* \* \*

They saw him off at the bus depot in Hollis Junction on a raw, sleeting morning in January 1991.

There were other families there, other soldiers, other wives crying and kids holding homemade signs. Calvin wore his uniform. He looked bigger in it than he did in his Sunday suit. He hugged Loretta and kissed her cheek. Then he turned to his son.

Ezra had decided in the car that he was going to do something brave: hold his father's hand.

He had never done it before. Calvin was not a man who held hands. But all around them, other children were holding on to their fathers, wrapping their arms around their legs, and Ezra wanted that, for just one day, to feel the warm love of a complete family. So he stepped forward and reached up and held out his hand, open, palm up, to his father.

Calvin's eyes dropped to it.

Then he reached out and patted Ezra twice on the top of the head.

"You're the man of the house now," he said. "Men don't cry."

Then he picked up his duffel bag and got on the bus.

Ezra stood on the cold concrete with his hand still held out in front of him, open and empty. Then it fell back down to his side. He returned to his mother's side and stood there with his head down. He watched the bus pull out of the station. He watched it turn the corner. He didn't cry. His father had told him not to.

\* \* \*

In the kitchen on Old Ferry Road, all those years later, Loretta pulled the pizza out of the oven, Angela's pizza, the crust blistered and brown, and set it on the stove top between them, and sat down across from her son with flour still on her apron. Out back, Mia was showing Jalen every egg she had found, one at a time, by name.

"I'm going to ask you again," she said. "Because you didn't answer me the first time. You've been dreaming."

Ezra looked up at her, startled.

"I know that look, baby. I've seen it on you since you were a little boy." She folded her hands on the table. "You want to tell me about it?"

He meant not to. But he found himself telling her anyway: the red room, the red moon, the pull at the window. Mia's drawing in Angela's Bible. The verse with his name in the margin. He told it in a monotone. When he was done, his mother sat without speaking while the kettle came to a boil behind her.

"He's calling you," she said finally.

"Who? God?" Ezra laughed, a short, ugly sound. "Mama, I grew up in that church. I know all about God calling. I watched Dad do it every Sunday. Then he went home and got drunk and hit you."

"Your father was not God."

"He talked like he was."

"Yes," Loretta said. "He did. And that is the worst thing he ever did to you. Worse than the drinking. Worse than his hands." She reached across the table, and for a moment Ezra thought she was going to take his hand. Instead she laid her hand on the table between them, palm up, open, and left it there. "Your father knew every word in that book, Ezra. And not a single one of them ever reached all the way into his heart."

Ezra looked at his mother's open hand on the table. For a long moment he didn't move. Then, reluctantly, he took it, and it was warm, and he let her keep it.

\* \* \*

The knock had come on a Saturday in early March 1991, a few days after the ground war ended.

Ezra answered the door. There were two men on the front steps in Army dress uniforms, with their hats tucked under their arms. One of them asked, gently, if his mother was home.

He remembered the sound Loretta made. He remembered her sliding down the wall of the hallway until she was sitting on the plastic runner. He remembered one of the men kneeling beside her. The rest of that day was gone.

The box came six weeks later.

It was a plain cardboard carton sealed with brown tape, with PATTERSON, PERSONAL, SEND HOME printed across the lid in Calvin's own slanting capitals. He had packed it himself in the last days before the ground war, the Army's letter said, and the Army had added what was found on him, and it had come home without him. Loretta opened it at the dinner table with a steak knife while Ezra watched. Inside were his father's things. A folded uniform. A shaving kit. A packet of letters to the other deacons of Pilgrim Rest, all about church business. A Bible: black leather, gold-edged, Calvin's name stamped on the cover.

Tucked inside the Bible's front cover was the church program from his last Sunday, the one with "Deacon Calvin Patterson, Our Soldier of the Cross" printed under his photograph on the front, folded carefully so it would not crease. There was no picture of Loretta. There was no picture of Ezra.

At the very bottom of the box, wrapped in a handkerchief, an Army tag tied to its strap, was the watch.

It was a heavy steel watch with a black face, the one Calvin had worn every day of Ezra's life. The crystal was cracked straight across. The hands had stopped at 3:17.

The letter from the Army said his father had been killed by artillery fire. One of the soldiers who came to the door had said that the watch had been on his wrist when he was hit, and had stopped then.

Ezra picked it up. It was too big for his wrist, and too heavy. He held it in both hands, stared at the stopped hands, and felt nothing.

Then he watched his mother fold the flaps of the carton shut over the rest of it, one at a time, and press the brown tape down with her palm. She carried it to the hall closet and set it on the top shelf, behind the winter coats, and stood there with her back to him.

The Sunday after the funeral, Pilgrim Rest took up a special collection for the Patterson family. The pastor read the total from the pulpit, and the church stood and clapped for the deacon who had given his life for his country. Loretta sat in the choir loft with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the floor. Ezra sat in the front pew and did not clap.

He was eight years old. He was holding a dead man's watch. That night he slid it onto his own wrist, though it was too big and did not run, and then he took it off again and slept with it under his pillow. The box stayed on the shelf, and over the years he stopped seeing it.

He started wearing the watch when he was sixteen, when his wrist was finally big enough to hold it. He never had it repaired or replaced the cracked crystal. He wore it every day for twenty-eight years, stopped at 3:17, and when people asked him why he wore a watch that didn't work, he said, "It was my father's," and they always nodded, satisfied.

That wasn't the reason. Ezra himself could not have said why he wore it.

\* \* \*

He left the kids asleep in his old room, on his mother's promise to bring them home in the morning, and left her house just after dark.

He skipped the turn for home and drove on down Old Ferry Road, because after what his mother had said he needed to see it again, until the old red-brick church came up on the corner. It had closed six years ago. The steeple was still there, but the white paint had weathered to the color of dishwater and was peeling off in strips. Plywood covered the windows. The sign out front had lost most of its letters. The only words still on it said: *FOR LEASE.*

Ezra parked across the street and turned off the engine and sat looking at it.

*Did you ever love me, Dad?*

*Did you ever love Him? Or was it all a show, the Sunday suit and the Bible and the shouting, every word of it, all for the four hundred people in the pews?*

*I held out my hand to you. At the bus station. I was eight years old and I held out my hand. Did you even see it? Did you feel anything at all when you patted me on the head and turned away?*

*I sat at my own table for twelve years and never once took my wife's hand when she reached for me. I only learned how at the very end, when it was almost too late. And now she's gone, and I'm the one who keeps reaching, in the dark, for a hand that isn't there.*

He sat in the truck until his hands were cold.

Then he took his wallet out of his back pocket and opened it. Tucked behind his driver's license was a cream-colored card with a pressed lavender flower on the front. He had moved it there from the kitchen counter a week ago, because he wasn't ready to throw it away.

He turned it over. The phone number was written in looping blue ink.

He looked at the dead church. He looked at the stopped watch on his wrist. Then he took out his phone and dialed.

It rang twice.

"Ezra," Celeste said. "I was hoping you'd call."
