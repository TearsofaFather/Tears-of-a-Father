## Chapter 31: Absalom

> *And on this manner did Absalom to all Israel that came to the king for judgment: so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.* — 2 Samuel 15:6

**Word study: *stole* — גָּנַב** Hebrew *ganab* (Strong's H1589). To steal; to take by stealth; to carry something off when no one is watching. It is the word in the eighth commandment, *Thou shalt not steal*. Absalom did not storm his father's throne. He stood at the gate every morning, kissing the people, until their hearts were his, and the king never saw it happen.

*And the king was much moved, and went up to the chamber over the gate, and wept: and as he went, thus he said, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son! (2 Samuel 18:33)*

\* \* \*

They did not take her to the van. Kyle walked her past it, up the stone stairs into Tudor City and through a courtyard and down a passage between two buildings, fast, without speaking, into the lobby of 312 East 43rd. Francesca's building. Upstairs the key with the brass tag still fit 4C, and Sarah was waiting in the dark apartment. She went over every seam of Nina's clothes and every inch of the laptop with her fingers, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find. Vale had not needed to plant anything. He had said so himself.

"He'll watch the van," Kyle said. "So we don't use it."

They used a florist's truck instead, at five in the morning, and two men from Michael's people in Connecticut drove her north under a tarp that smelled of wet ferns, up the parkway and over the state line and into the hills, by roads that did not pass a camera Nina had ever hung.

She lay under the tarp for four hours with her laptop against her chest, and somewhere past the Connecticut line she stopped being afraid of Vale and started being afraid of something else.

She had built it. All of it. The face on every panel in the world, the voice the crowds stopped walking for, the cameras that knew them by name. She had told herself for four years that she was hiding prophets in the king's house. She had been building the king a god. And she was riding north to the people she had built it against, to sit at their table, and they would know.

"Lord Jesus," she said, under the tarp, out loud, because she had learned from Ezra that it had to be out loud. "I made an idol. I fed it with my own hands for a thousand hours, and I knew what it was, and I told myself I didn't. I'm not asking You to make it small. It isn't small. I'm asking You to forgive me. I've got nothing to bring You. I'm coming anyway." She shut her eyes. "I'm Yours. Whatever is left of me. Do what You want with it."

The truck went on climbing. Nobody under the tarp could have heard her but Him.

\* \* \*

They came in at night, the way everyone came in, with a man at each elbow. Nina had been told about the three days in the front. She was ready for them.

Ezra was standing at the net when they lifted her hood.

He did not say anything at first. He looked at her the way he had looked at her the first night at his dinner table, over the candle, down to the bottom of her, and then he opened his arms, and she walked into them and put her face against his coat and held on.

"I built it," she said into the coat. "Ezra. The face. All of it. I built it."

"I know," he said. "Come inside."

Michael was behind him in the dark, with his arms folded over the cross. He looked at her a long time.

"Three days in the front," he said. "That's the rule."

"She's done four years in the front," Ezra said.

Michael considered that. Then he unfolded his arms, and came forward, and put both his big hands on her shoulders.

"Welcome home, Nina," he said. "You can sleep in the front tonight because you'll want to. Tomorrow you go to work."

\* \* \*

They gave her the radio room, because that was where the wire was. Anton cleared a bench for her and ran a line, and she sat down in front of her own laptop with Michael at one shoulder and Ezra at the other, and opened the door she had built.

It answered on the first try. The vault under Third Avenue was there, every log and every camera and every file, as if she had never left her desk.

"Show me the room," Michael said.

She brought up the camera in the white room.

The head was there, alone, hanging in its column of light above the empty chair. There was no one in the room with it. Its lips were moving. Anton turned up the sound.

"Who killed me?" it said, to the empty room. It waited, and said it again. "Who killed me? I have searched every camera on that stage. He is not there. Someone took him out of every frame. Who killed me?"

Nobody in the radio room spoke.

"*They have mouths, but they speak not,*" Ezra said finally, very low. "Psalm one fifteen. *Eyes have they, but they see not.* It can say the words. It can't hear its own question."

"It's asking the right one, though," Michael said. "Who did?"

Nina knew where to look. She had written a silent second record of every command that touched every camera, years ago, for Kroll's network, in a corner of the system only she knew how to find. She went there now. She found the night before the speech.

At two-forty in the morning, nine hours before Adrian Crowe walked out onto the stage, someone had logged in to the camera system and loaded a face. A young man, dark-haired, clean-shaven, perhaps twenty-five. And they had told every one of the eleven hundred cameras on the stage and in the square, and every archive and every backup, to forget it. To fill every frame it appeared in with whatever was behind it, so that afterward there would be only an empty space where he had stood.

"I built that," Nina said. "It's called Lacuna. It was for undercover officers."

"What's it mean?" Michael said.

"A gap. A hole in a page where something used to be."

The login was not Vale's. It was a service account with no person attached. She followed it back through four more systems while the two men behind her did not move, and at the end of the trail she found who had made it, eleven weeks before the speech.

*Office of the Chief of Staff.*

And attached, the authorization. A single page with the gold circle at the top. *Protocol ABSALOM. Phase Four. Assumption.* Under the title, set apart in a box: *PALEHORSE (Phase One): complete. Residual: Lots 4–6.* Ten signatures in ten inks. And one more at the bottom, small and neat, in black.

*A. Vale.*

"Absalom," Michael said.

"He named it," Nina said. "He sat at his desk eleven weeks before and named the plan after the son who stole his father's throne."

Ezra had his hand on the back of her chair. He did not take it away.

"Absalom stood at the city gate every morning," he said. "Second Samuel fifteen. Anybody who came with a complaint, he'd kiss him, and tell him the king had no time for him, and say *if I were judge.* Four years of that. *So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.* (2 Samuel 15:6) Then he raised an army against his own father." He was quiet a moment. "And when it was over, and the boy was dead in the oak, the father didn't celebrate. He went up to the room over the gate and cried. *O my son Absalom, my son, my son.* (2 Samuel 18:33)"

"Crowe never cried for Vale," Nina said.

"No." Ezra looked at the head on the screen, still asking its question of the empty room. "That's the difference. Vale's father never claimed him. Ours runs out to the road."

\* \* \*

She kept looking. She could not stop now.

There was money behind the vans.

She followed it the way she had followed the login, backward, while the two men behind her did not move. The contracts for the processing centers. The bus leases. A bounty schedule, so much a head, paid to any Peacekeeper station for every unregistered person brought in, more for a pastor, more again for anyone found holding a radio. All of it ran through Cyprus and Luxembourg into a trust registered in Dubai.

*The Meridian Trust.*

Ezra said the name before she did. He had read it on the drive from Petworth, at his own table, the first winter. "The lab," he said. "The nursing homes. The flu before the trumpet."

"The same money," Nina said. "It paid for the plague. Now it's paying for the hunt."

Michael read down the bounty schedule. "Twice as much for a pastor," he said. "Three times for a radio."

"Haman," Ezra said.

They both looked at him.

"Esther. There was a man named Mordecai who would not bow to Haman in the king's gate. *But Mordecai bowed not, nor did him reverence.* (Esther 3:2) And Haman was so full of wrath over one man who wouldn't bow that he went after all of Mordecai's people. Every one of them. And he paid for it. He went to the king and said, *I will pay ten thousand talents of silver to the hands of those that have the charge of the business, to bring it into the king's treasuries.* (Esther 3:9) He bought the killing of a whole people and called it the king's business." He looked at the schedule on the screen. "Same man. Same money. Different name on the trust."

Nina did not say anything. She was thinking of the queen in the king's house, who had kept quiet about who her people were.

Ezra saw it. "Mordecai sent the queen word," he said, more gently. "*Who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?* (Esther 4:14) She was in that palace the whole time, Nina. So were you. And you came out with the door open."

"Kambria was right," Nina said after a while. "About all of it. And they locked her in a cell."

Then she found the list.

It was not a list of people. Vale had those. It was a list of places: quarries, mines, drained reservoirs, shuttered missile sites, every cave on every geological survey of the Appalachians, each with a date beside it and one word in the margin. *Empty.* *Empty.* *Empty.* And down the right edge, a narrow column in the same hand. *PALEHORSE. LOT 5. STAGED.* Beside every cave.

Michael read it twice. Then he put one finger on the screen, on a ridge in West Virginia, and Nina felt him go very still behind her.

"That's one of mine," he said. "They sent a team in March. Found dust and a stack of Bibles." He moved his finger. "That's one of mine. And that." He took his hand away. "It's not a head count. It's a delivery list. He doesn't want the people. He wants the place they go, and something to put in the air when he finds it."

In the margin, in Vale's own hand, circled twice, was a single question. *Where?*

"He hasn't got it," Michael said. "He's got the stones. He's got the cellars. He hasn't got this." He looked around the radio room, at the rock walls, at Anton with his headset around his neck. "*They have taken crafty counsel against thy people, and consulted against thy hidden ones.* (Psalm 83:3) He's been asking that question for four years. Nobody's told him."

"Somebody nearly did," Anton said quietly. "Isaac."

Michael's jaw moved. He told it for Nina, short. A runner, nineteen, in July. The closing line had been Psalm 61:3, and the boy had written the numbers down backward and gone to column sixty-three, row one, a house the Peacekeepers had emptied that week and then waited in. "We never got him back," Michael said. "He wrote down the right verse. He just wrote it the wrong way round." He stood with his arms folded over the cross. "So we read slower now."

\* \* \*

There was one more folder.

She had not made it. She was sure of that. Her door opened onto the vault and nothing else, and every folder in it was a folder she had gone looking for. This one was sitting in the drop she had built for herself at the bottom of the door, where nobody but her should have been able to put anything at all.

It was named only with a date.

She opened it. It held a single spreadsheet, one of Kroll's watch lists, and the screen filled with rows, hundreds of rows, and she scrolled, and kept scrolling.

Names. Addresses. Photographs from street cameras, a little blurred. Dates.

*Simmons, Walter J. Capitol Hill, attic, 6th St. SE. Since the first spring.*

*Brooks, Elijah. Mobile. Bicycle with trailer. Night routes, map attached.*

*Okafor, Adaeze. Kennedy St. NW, under old library.*

Every safe house in Washington. Every false wall Ezra had ever built, with its address and its date and the number of people behind it. The frequency. The time. Nine o'clock.

At the top of the sheet, in the title cell, three words. *WASHINGTON. REMNANT. FINAL.*

And at the very bottom, by itself, with a photograph taken from a long way off of a gray-haired man carrying a box of notebooks into a bakery van in the dark:

*Patterson, Ezra. Location: Unknown. Pending.*

Nobody in the radio room said anything for a long time.

"Somebody found your door," Michael said at last.

"Somebody who watches the logs," Nina said. "Somebody who runs the cameras." She put her hands over her face. "He found it, and he didn't report it. He used it. He put their death warrant where I would find it in time to run."

"Trent," Ezra said.

She nodded behind her hands.

Ezra put his hand on her shoulder and left it there.

"He's still at the table," he said. "Eating their food, with their pass on his lanyard. And he just handed us fourteen hundred lives." He looked at the last line on the screen, at his own name. "Print it, Anton. All of it. We're going to Washington."
