## Chapter 31: Absalom

> *And on this manner did Absalom to all Israel that came to the king for judgment: so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.* — 2 Samuel 15:6

**Word study: *stole* — גָּנַב** Hebrew *ganab* (Strong's H1589). To steal; to take by stealth; to carry something off when no one is watching. It is the word in the eighth commandment, *Thou shalt not steal*. Absalom did not storm his father's throne. He stood at the gate every morning, kissing the people, until their hearts were his, and the king never saw it happen.

*And the king was much moved, and went up to the chamber over the gate, and wept: and as he went, thus he said, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son! (2 Samuel 18:33)*

\* \* \*

Nina did not answer the image.

She stood on her side of the glass wall at one in the morning with her flashlight in her hand and the dried blood stiff on her forehead, and the face of Adrian Crowe looked at her out of the small white room, patient, waiting, with the question still unanswered in the cold.

*Who killed me?*

She knew the answer. She had known it for months, since the moment she saw Vale look at his watch on camera four-seventeen. And she understood, standing there, that the thing behind the glass wanted the answer more than anything in its ten billion records, because that was what Adrian Crowe would have wanted, and it had been built to want what he wanted.

It was not Adrian Crowe. That was the thing she held on to. Ezra had said it on the radio the night after the unveiling, in a room behind a false wall under an old library, and she had heard it a week later on a recording Michael's people smuggled to her in Jerusalem on a memory card sewn into a coat lining. *It is not a man. It is not his soul. His soul is where every soul goes. It is an image. A likeness. A golden calf with a voice. And you do not talk to idols. You do not bargain with them. You do not answer them. You turn your back.*

"You are not him," Nina said.

Her voice shook. She made it stop shaking.

"You are not Adrian Crowe. You're a copy I made out of his leftovers. You don't get to ask me anything."

The face on the screen kept its expression, looking at her warmly through the glass.

She reached past the glass door to the panel on the wall and pressed the switch that cut power to the white room, and the screen went black, and the face went with it.

Then she walked back up the long aisle between the racks to her desk and sat down and put her face in her hands, and stayed that way until her breathing slowed.

When she lifted her head, she opened the laptop. Not to work. To look.

\* \* \*

She had told herself for months that she did not need proof. She had seen what she had seen. Ezra believed her. God knew. That was enough.

But the image had asked, and she understood now that the image would keep asking, and that sooner or later someone else would hear it ask. And if the truth was going to come out of the vault under the Holy of Holies at all, Nina wanted it to come out in her hands and not in the beast's.

She knew where to look. She had written the logging layer herself, three years ago, in New York, before any of this, for Kroll's camera network: a silent second record of every command that touched every camera, kept in a corner of the system that only its author knew how to find. She had built it like everything else she built, a little paranoid, and then forgotten it.

She found the night before the speech. She found camera four-seventeen.

At two-forty in the morning on the day Adrian Crowe died, nine hours before he walked out onto the stage, someone had logged in to the Organization's camera system and loaded a face. One face. A young man, dark-haired, clean-shaven, perhaps twenty-five. And they had told every one of the eleven hundred cameras on the stage and in the square, and every camera in the Organization's network, and every archive, to forget that face. Not to blur it. To erase it. To fill every frame it appeared in with whatever was behind it, smoothly, invisibly, so that the young man in the priest's linen would walk across the back of the stage and up behind the Chairman and put a knife in his neck, and every camera in the world would show an empty space where he stood.

Nina knew that tool. She had built it. It was called Lacuna, and it was meant for protecting the identities of undercover officers. It required the highest clearance in the system.

The login was not Vale's. That would have been too simple. It was a service account, a machine account with no person attached, such as ran automatic processes in the middle of the night. Nina followed it backward through four more systems in an hour and a half, sitting in the cold with her down jacket zipped to her chin, and at the end of the trail she found who had created the service account, eleven weeks before the speech.

*Office of the Chief of Staff.*

And attached to it, the authorization. A document, scanned, a single page with the gold circle at the top. *Protocol ABSALOM. Phase Four. Assumption.* Above the title, in smaller type, ran the four-line index of phases, and under it a fifth item set apart in a box: *PALEHORSE (Phase One): complete. Residual: Lots 4–6.* And beneath it, ten signatures in ten different inks, which she knew by heart now from the sheet Vale had laid on her desk on the third morning, and one more, at the bottom, small and neat, in black.

*A. Vale.*

She sat back in her chair.

He had named it. He had sat at his desk eleven weeks before he killed his father and named the plan after the son who stole his father's kingdom. Nina could not tell whether it was arrogance or grief. She thought perhaps Vale could not either.

She kept looking. She could not stop now.

There was a payment. Of course there was a payment. The young priest had a name, after all, and a bank account, and three weeks before the speech the account had received four hundred thousand dollars from a holding company in the Emirates, and the next morning the account had been closed, and the young man had never existed. Nina traced the holding company back. It took her until nearly four in the morning. It went through Cyprus, and then Luxembourg, and then into a trust registered in Dubai that she had heard of once before, that first winter, at Ezra's dinner table, in a story about Kambria.

*The Meridian Trust.*

The Meridian Trust. She had met it before, on the first page of a drive at Ezra's dinner table, years ago now: the owner of Verity Health, the contract lab behind the sickness in the nursing homes, one of the ten who sat on the High Council. She had known since that winter that the Trust had paid for the plague. Now she knew it had paid for the knife.

She sat in the humming cold. The thirty-base signature Kambria had once found in an isolate from Ohio, the stripe on the gel their own file called PALE, was there again in the Verity manufacturing specifications, written into every batch like a signature so that the Trust could know its own work in the field. The same money. The same man.

The flu that had filled the hospitals in the weeks before the Rapture. The knife in Adrian Crowe's neck. One trust. One signature.

Kambria had been right. She had been right about all of it, the autumn before the sky opened, alone, with a red notebook and a wall of yarn, and they had called her a fanatic and locked her in a cell.

Nina copied everything.

She copied the Lacuna logs and the service account trail and the Absalom authorization with its eleven signatures. She copied the payment and the trust. She copied it onto a memory card the size of her thumbnail, like the ones Michael's people sewed into coat linings, and she wiped every trace of her own search from the logs, line by line, as she had wiped a thousand work orders in Washington, and at six in the morning, when the first shift of technicians came down the stairs into the vault yawning, she was at her desk with a cup of tea, running a diagnostic on rack fourteen, with the memory card in the toe of her left sock.

\* \* \*

She lasted little more than a week.

She went to work. She came home to the cot in the dormitory on the Mount of Olives. She left the white room dark, and when Kroll asked her about it, in a voice thin with fright, she told him it had been damaged in the earthquake and she was rebuilding the interface, and he nodded and let it go. The image upstairs in the shrine came back on the fourth day, and spoke at noon, and the world bowed, and Nina was forty feet underground, as always, at noon.

She sent word to Michael's people through the only channel she trusted, a woman who sold bread in the Old City market and had a cousin with a boat in Haifa. *I have something. I need to come out.* The answer came back on the seventh day, folded inside a round loaf of bread. *Ninth night. Jaffa Gate. Two in the morning. Wear blue.*

On the eighth day, Trent Walker sat down across from her in the canteen.

He had not spoken to her since the railing. He sat down with a tray he did not touch and looked at her across the table with his tired eyes and said, barely moving his lips:

"You pulled the Lacuna logs."

Nina's tea stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Don't," Trent said. "Don't look around. Don't put the cup down. Just listen." He picked up his fork and moved some eggs around his plate. "I run the cameras. All of them. I've been running the cameras on this mountain since before you got here. You think I don't watch the logs? You think I didn't see somebody go into the backup layer at one in the morning, over a week ago, and spend four hours following a service account home?" He took a bite of egg and chewed it and swallowed. "You wiped your tracks good. Real good. I almost missed it."

"Trent," she whispered.

"I saw it on camera four-seventeen too," he said. "The day of. Same as you. I saw that boy walk across the back of that stage, and I took off my headset, because I knew exactly what I was looking at, and I knew exactly who'd erased him, because I loaded the face." He put the fork down. "They sent it to me as a training file. A test of the new Lacuna build. I loaded it at two-forty in the morning and went back to bed."

Nina set her cup down.

"My mama sang in the choir at Greater Mount Calvary for thirty years," Trent said. "She prayed for me every night of my life. And I loaded the face of the man who killed Adrian Crowe into eleven hundred cameras and went back to bed." He laughed, a low dry sound, the worst she had ever heard. "So don't tell me anything. I don't want to know where you're going. I don't want to know who you're going to. I'm still on their side, Nina. I'm sitting at their table. I eat their food. They put a pass on my lanyard and I wear it." He looked down at his tray. "But Vale's office asked for the backup logs this morning. All of them. For an audit. They want them by tomorrow at noon."

The canteen clattered around them. Someone dropped a tray at the far end.

"I can lose them for one night," Trent said. "Not two."

He stood with his untouched tray and looked down at her. Whatever showed in his face was gone before she could read it.

"Come out of her," he said, almost too quietly to hear. "Isn't that what the old man said?"

He walked away.

\* \* \*

She went out at one in the morning on the ninth night, in a blue Organization relief jacket she had taken from a supply closet, with nothing in her hands and the memory card in her sock.

The dormitory was dark. The guard at the end of the hall was asleep in his chair. She went down the back stairs and out through the kitchen and into the street, and walked down the long slope of the Mount of Olives in the dark, past the old Jewish graves, the thousands of white stone tombs on the western slope that had been there for three thousand years, that people said would be the first to open when the Messiah came. She had read it in Zechariah, at Ezra's dinner table. *And his feet shall stand in that day upon the mount of Olives.* She walked among the graves in the starlight with her heart pounding and prayed with every step.

The Kidron Valley was dark at the bottom. She crossed it on the old road and climbed toward the walls of the Old City, black against the sky, with the gold temple behind them on the mountain, lit from below, shining.

Jaffa Gate was on the western side. She had to go around. She kept to the shadows along the walls, under the cameras that she knew were there because she had placed half of them, and she knew, as she walked under each one, that Trent was watching the screen, or not watching it, and that whether she lived or died tonight was in the hands of a man who had loaded the face.

At a quarter to two, a hundred yards from the gate, a black car pulled up beside her on the empty road and stopped.

The rear window came down.

"Miss Cole," said Alexander Vale. "It's very late. May I give you a ride?"

\* \* \*

She got in. There was nothing else to do. Two men in dark suits had stepped out of the front of the car and were standing on either side of her, not touching her, waiting.

The back of the car was dark and smelled of leather. Vale sat in the far corner with his legs crossed and a glass of water in the cup holder beside him, which he did not drink. He turned it a quarter turn. The car pulled away from the curb and drove slowly along the walls of the Old City in the dark, going nowhere in particular.

"You found Absalom," Vale said.

It was not a question. Nina said nothing.

"I wondered how long it would take you. You're very good. Better than anyone else Sebastian has. I told him so when I signed off on you." He looked out the window at the walls going by. "Four hours, Trent says. He was thorough in his report. He reported you yesterday, you know. Before he sat down across from you in the canteen."

Nina felt the floor of the car go out from under her.

"Don't be too hard on him," Vale said. "He was frightened. And he's been useful. Useful for years." He turned his dark eyes on her. "You think I didn't know about your weekends, Miss Cole? The trains to Washington? The old library on Kennedy Street? The radios in the old cabinets? The work orders that went missing, season after season?" He smiled, very slightly. "I knew about you the week you started. I knew about the carpenter before you did. I have known about every false wall he ever built."

"Then why," Nina said. Her voice barely came out. "Why didn't you—"

"Stop you?" Vale turned the question over. "Because you were gathering them. Don't you see? That's what I needed. Scattered, they're impossible. A believer here, a believer there, in a million apartments, in a thousand cities, and every one of them has to be found one at a time. It takes years. I don't have years. I have a very short time." He said it calmly, a line read from a ledger. "But you and your carpenter gathered them. You put them in rooms. You gave them radios, and a song to listen for, and a time, nine o'clock, every night. You taught them to trust each other and tell each other where they were. You made them a church." He spread his hands. "You did my work for me, Miss Cole, and did it carefully, through every season since the trumpet. I only had to let you."

The car turned a corner. Nina could see the gold temple through the window, shining on the mountain.

"Why tell me?" she said.

"Because it doesn't matter anymore." Vale reached into his jacket and took out a small black drive, the same size as the card in her sock, and set it on the leather seat between them. "That is everything you copied. Trent pulled it out of the backup after you wiped it. I've read it. It's very good work. It would prove to anyone who cared to look that I arranged my father's death." He tilted his head. "And no one will care, Miss Cole. Do you understand? No one. Not one person who bows at noon will care. They have his face. They have his voice. He speaks to them every day and tells them he loves them. What does it matter to them who held the knife? He's more alive now than he ever was." His voice did not change. "And the ones who would care are in your carpenter's rooms. And they'll be dead before the season turns."

He touched the drive with one finger and pushed it across the seat toward her.

"Take it," he said. "Take it to him. Let him play it on his little radio at nine o'clock. I'd like him to know." He looked at her with an expression she could not read at all. "I'd like him to know that I know his name. And his son's name. And the name of the song."

The car stopped.

They were at Jaffa Gate. Through the window, in the shadow of the old stone arch, Nina could see a woman in a blue relief jacket like her own, standing beside a white van, waiting, holding a round loaf of bread.

"Your friends are here," Vale said. "Go on."

Nina did not move.

"I'm letting you go," he said patiently. "Because you'll go straight to him. And wherever you go, Miss Cole, I'll be watching. You'll lead me home." He turned his glass of water a quarter turn. "Good night. Thank you for your work."

One of the men in dark suits opened the door.

She got out. She stood on the pavement in the dark with the black drive in her hand and the memory card in her sock and looked back at the car. Vale had already turned his face away, toward the far window, toward the temple on the mountain.

The car pulled away and was gone.

\* \* \*

She told the woman with the bread everything, standing in the shadow of the gate, in a whisper, in thirty seconds. The woman listened without changing her expression. Then she took the black drive out of Nina's hand and dropped it down a storm drain at the foot of the wall, and took Nina's arm, and walked her not to the white van but past it, through the gate, into the Old City, into the maze of dark stone streets and covered markets and stairways, fast, without speaking, turning and turning again, until Nina had lost all sense of where she was.

In a courtyard behind a shuttered spice shop, the woman stopped and made Nina strip down to her underclothes and stand under a bare bulb while she went over every seam of every garment with her fingers. She found nothing. She went over Nina's skin. She found nothing. Then she made her take off her shoes and socks.

The memory card was still in the toe of her left sock.

The woman held it up to the light. She looked at it, and then at Nina.

"He did not find this one?"

"No," Nina said. "He only had the copy Trent pulled. From the backup." She stopped. "I didn't make this one from the backup. I made it from the live system. Before I wiped it." She looked at the tiny card in the woman's fingers. "Trent didn't know there were two."

The woman nodded. "Then perhaps Trent did not know everything," she said. "Or perhaps he knew more than he told." She handed the card back. "Put it somewhere better than your sock."

They did not go to Haifa. The woman said Vale would be watching Haifa now, and every boat in it. They went south instead, that same night, in the back of a produce truck full of crates of tomatoes, down the long road out of the mountains toward the Dead Sea, with the gold temple shrinking behind them in the dark. Before dawn they turned east, onto roads Nina could not see, and in the first gray light she sat up among the crates and saw red mountains rising ahead of them out of the desert, jagged and bare and the color of old blood, and the woman beside her said one word.

"Edom."

Nina looked at the red mountains, and thought of the families she had watched from the railing in the spring, walking quickly away down the eastern road with their bundles and their children, toward the desert, when the old men told them to flee.

*Then let them which be in Judaea flee into the mountains.*

"The others went here," she said. "The ones who listened."

"Many of them," the woman said. "Into the rock. Thousands. More every week." She looked at Nina sidelong. "You will not stay. You will go on to your carpenter. There is a plane, a small one, in two days, from a place I will not name. But tonight you will sleep in the rock with them." For the first time the woman's mouth eased. "They have Bibles there. Someone hid them in the caves a long time ago. They are reading the prophets, and then they are reading on, into the parts they never read before. You will see."

\* \* \*

She saw.

They came into the red city at dusk, on foot, the last two miles through a narrow canyon where the cliffs rose so close on either side that she could touch both walls at once. And then the canyon opened, and the rock went up on every side, red and rose and gold in the last of the light, carved into great doorways and columns and tombs by people who had lived and died here two thousand years ago. And in every doorway, on every ledge, in every cave mouth up and down the cliffs, there were people. Thousands of them. Families, old men, children. Cooking fires. Laundry hung on lines between the columns. A child ran past her laughing.

That night she sat at a fire in the mouth of a cave halfway up a red cliff, with a family from Tel Aviv who had walked here in May, and an old man with a long white beard opened a book on his knees by the firelight and began to read aloud in Hebrew. Nina did not understand the words. But she saw the book. It was a Bible, a thick old English one, water-stained, with a Hebrew Bible open beside it, and the old man was reading from the Hebrew and then turning to the English and reading the same verse again, sounding out the words, while a young woman beside him translated for the children.

It was Zechariah. Nina knew it, because Ezra had read it on the air.

*And they shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him, as one mourneth for his only son, and shall be in bitterness for him, as one that is in bitterness for his firstborn.*

The old man stopped reading. He looked up from the page at the fire, and his eyes were wet.

"Whom they have pierced," he said in English, slowly, to no one. "Pierced. Who is pierced?" He turned the pages of the English Bible, back and back, past the prophets, past the end of the Hebrew he knew, into the part at the back that he had never read in his life until this spring, and stopped, and put his finger on a line, and read it aloud, in English, haltingly.

"*But one of the soldiers with a spear pierced his side, and forthwith came there out blood and water.*"

No one around the fire spoke.

"*As one mourneth for his only son,*" the old man whispered. He closed both books and held them against his chest and rocked a little in the firelight.

Nina thought of David in the chamber over the gate, weeping for the son who had stolen his kingdom and died hanging by his hair in an oak. *O my son Absalom, my son, my son.* She thought of Vale at the glass in the vault, saying *he was my father* to a machine. She thought of Ezra on a kitchen floor in the dark with a dead laptop and his hands in the air, hearing a cello cry out to an empty seat.

The tears of a father. All of it, all the way through the whole Book, from Genesis to the red mountains of Edom. A Father weeping for His children, and calling them home.

\* \* \*

She did not sleep that night. Near dawn, sitting in the cave mouth with a blanket around her shoulders and the old man snoring behind her, she took out the memory card, and the small rugged laptop the woman with the bread had given her, and plugged it in, to make sure the files had survived the trip.

They had. The Lacuna logs. The service account. Absalom, with its eleven signatures. The payment. The trust.

And one more folder, at the bottom of the list, that she had not copied. She was sure she had not copied it. She had copied five folders from the live system in the vault, and there were six.

It was named only with a date.

She opened it.

It held a single spreadsheet. She knew the format; it was one of Kroll's, from the camera network, the kind that held watch lists. She opened it, and the screen filled with rows, hundreds and hundreds of rows, and she scrolled, and kept scrolling.

Names. Addresses. Photographs from street cameras, a little blurred. Dates.

*Simmons, Walter J. Capitol Hill, attic, 6th St. SE. Since the first spring.*

*Brooks, Elijah. Mobile. Bicycle with trailer. Night routes, map attached.*

*Okafor, Adaeze. Kennedy St. NW, under old library.*

Row after row after row. Every safe house in Washington. Every false wall Ezra had ever built, with its address and its date and the number of people behind it. Every room she had protected since the first snow. The radio frequency. The time. Nine o'clock.

At the very top of the sheet, in the title cell, someone had typed three words.

*WASHINGTON. REMNANT. FINAL.*

And at the very bottom, in the last row, by itself, with a photograph taken from a long way off of a gray-haired man in a work jacket carrying a box of notebooks into a bakery van in the dark:

*Patterson, Ezra. Location: Unknown. Pending.*

She understood then who had put the sixth folder on the card, and when. Not Vale. Vale had thought the card in her sock did not exist.

Someone had added it in the live system, before she copied, knowing she would copy the folders she had found, and that she would take whatever was beside them. Someone who ran the cameras. Someone who could not come out himself, not yet, and who was sitting at the beast's table, eating its food, with its pass on his lanyard, and had found the only way he had to hand the remnant their own death warrant in time to run.

Nina sat in the mouth of the cave in the red mountains of Edom with the laptop on her knees and the sun coming up over the desert, and wept for Trent Walker, and then she closed the laptop and went to find the woman with the bread, because there was a plane in two days, and there was no time to spare.
