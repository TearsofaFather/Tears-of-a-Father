## Chapter 28: Whose Right It Is

> *I will overturn, overturn, overturn, it: and it shall be no more, until he come whose right it is; and I will give it him.* — Ezekiel 21:27

**Word study: *overturn* — עַוָּה** Hebrew *avvah* (Strong's H5754). A ruin; a heap of rubble; a thing turned upside down until nothing of its old shape is left. Ezekiel says it three times, the strongest a Hebrew writer could say anything. The crown of David would be overturned, and overturned, and overturned, and no one would wear it rightly again until the One came to whom it had always belonged.

Zedekiah was the last king to sit on David's throne in Jerusalem. He had sworn an oath by the name of the LORD to the king of Babylon, and broken it, and he had stopped his ears against the prophet. So the word of the LORD came to Ezekiel by the river Chebar, against him: *And thou, profane wicked prince of Israel, whose day is come, when iniquity shall have an end, Thus saith the Lord GOD; Remove the diadem, and take off the crown: this shall not be the same: exalt him that is low, and abase him that is high.* (Ezekiel 21:25–26)

\* \* \*

The white car with the gold circle on its door sat in front of Ezra's house for forty minutes that morning in March, and then it drove away, and it did not come back that day, or the next.

It came back on the third day with a second car behind it.

Ezra watched them from the upstairs hall, through the gap in the curtains of Jalen's room. Four of them this time, two from each car, in the soft blue Organization jackets that were meant to look like relief workers and not like police. They went up Mrs. Okafor's walk first and knocked, and she opened the door, and Ezra could see from upstairs how her shoulders stiffened. They talked to her on her step for five minutes. One of them held a tablet up to her face and she let him. Then they thanked her and came down her walk and stood on the sidewalk in front of the Pattersons' house, looking up at the front window, where the lamp was.

They skipped the knock and wrote something down. Then they crossed the street to Walt's.

Walt let them in. Ezra had told him to, if they came, so that it would look ordinary. He watched the old man's front door close behind them and stood at the window counting. Nineteen minutes. When they came out, they were smiling, and one of them shook Walt's hand on the porch.

Walt came across the street an hour later with his thermos and sat down at the dinner table and took off his cap.

"They wanted to know if I was registered," he said. "I told them I was an old man and I didn't get out much, and the relief center scared me with all the lines, and I'd been meaning to. They said they'd send someone with a mobile unit. They were very kind." He set the thermos down. "Then the young one asked me about you."

"What about me?"

"Whether I knew the man across the street. Whether I'd ever heard of a radio program at night. He said there'd been complaints from the neighborhood about interference on the emergency band." Walt looked at him. "I told him I was too old for the radio. I lied to him, Ezra. I sat in my own kitchen and lied to a boy younger than my grandson would've been, and I don't know if God forgives that."

"Rahab did it," Ezra said. "For the spies."

"I know Rahab did it." Walt rubbed his face. "Hebrews eleven thirty-one. It's in the record." He frowned into his thermos. "Knowing it doesn't make it feel any better."

That night Nina came down from New York on the late train, and she did not take her coat off, and she did not sit down.

"They have the frequency," she said. "Not the location yet. The frequency. There's a work order in the system to put three receiving vans on it, triangulating, starting Monday. Northeast quadrant." She was very pale. "I can lose the work order for a few days. I can't make it disappear. If you broadcast from this house again after this weekend, they'll be at that basement door inside an hour."

Nobody spoke.

"Then we move it," Ezra said finally.

"Move it where?"

"I don't know yet." He looked around at the twenty-three people crowded into his kitchen and dining room and hall, sitting on the stairs and the counters and the floor, because the hidden church had outgrown the coal bin in February and was outgrowing the house now. "But I know we need batteries, and lamp oil, and water filters, and a way to carry the transmitter, and we can't buy any of it, because none of us have a card." He stood up. "I'll go out tomorrow before light."

"Alone?" Elijah said.

"Alone. One man looking for one thing. Two men is a patrol."

\* \* \*

He went out at four-thirty the next morning in the truck, with the headlights off for the first six blocks.

The hardware store on Bladensburg Road had been looted in the first week after the Rapture and again in January, and he had heard there might still be something in the back. It was a long, low building of painted cinder block with a sign that said FREEMAN BROS. HARDWARE SINCE 1952, half of it shot out. Somebody had pried the steel gate halfway up and left it hanging. He parked around the side and went in through the gap with a headlamp turned down to its red setting and an empty duffel bag over his shoulder.

The front of the store was stripped to the shelving. But in the back, past the plumbing aisle, there was a stockroom with a steel door that someone had tried and failed to force, and Ezra had hung a hundred doors like it. He had it off its hinges in six minutes with a flat bar and a hammer.

Inside were things nobody had thought to steal. Boxes of D batteries, still sealed. Three cases of lamp oil. A carton of ceramic water filters, the kind you set in a bucket. Coils of rope. Tarps. He was filling the duffel bag as fast as he could, kneeling on the concrete with the red light on his forehead, when he heard the engine.

It came up Bladensburg Road fast, a deep diesel growl, far too heavy for a car, and braked hard outside, and a door slammed.

Ezra switched off the headlamp and did not move.

Boots crunched over the broken glass at the front of the store. One man, moving without waste or hurry. A white light swept across the ceiling of the main room, then down the aisles one by one, then toward the back.

"Who's there?" A man's voice, low and hard. "I can hear you breathing. Come out with your hands where I can see them."

Ezra had every reason to stay hidden. The stockroom had a back door with a push bar. He could be through it and around the side to the truck in fifteen seconds, if it was not chained. He could not tell if it was chained.

He did know something else. He had spent four months in the Word, every night, as he had once spent his apprenticeship learning to read lumber. In the Proverbs, which Angela had loved, there was a verse he had copied into the front of his notebook in January, because he had needed it for his own temper: *A soft answer turneth away wrath.* And in Hebrews there was a line about the ones who were full grown, *who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil.* He had been praying for months to have his senses exercised. He did not want to run without knowing what he was running from.

He stood up with his empty hands raised, and stepped into the doorway of the stockroom.

The white light hit him full in the face.

"It's just me," he said, keeping his voice even. "It's just Ezra. I'm not armed. I come in peace."

The light held on his face. Then it dropped to his chest, and Ezra could see the man holding it.

He was big, bigger than Ezra, and dressed for war: helmet, dark plate carrier with pouches across the front, a rifle at his shoulder with the light mounted under the barrel, a pistol on his thigh, gloves, boots. His face under the helmet was weathered and bearded, nearer sixty than fifty, with eyes that stayed on Ezra's hands. He did not look like a Peacekeeper. Peacekeepers wore blue. Nor did he look like one of the gangs that had come out of the suburbs in January. He was too disciplined, and too still.

And hanging outside his vest, on a plain steel chain, swinging slightly against the dark plate, was a cross.

It was small and silver and worn smooth. In a city where the faith of Jesus Christ had been declared divisive in the first weeks and dangerous soon after, where people had been taken from their homes for less, a man was wearing it on the outside of his armor, where anyone could see.

Ezra looked at it. He could not help himself.

The man caught him looking. Then the man's eyes dropped from Ezra's face to Ezra's collar and stopped there, and a look crossed the hard bearded face, wary and then astonished.

Ezra had forgotten he had it on. A second cross, smaller, plain silver on a leather cord at his own throat. Angela had given it to him on an anniversary more than ten years back, and he had not worn it once since, until the morning the watch began to tick.

Two crosses, in a looted store, in a city where either one could get a man taken from his house.

Neither of them moved.

"You're a believer," the man said slowly.

"Since November," Ezra said. "You?"

"Since November."

The rifle came down until it pointed at the floor.

They stood in the dark hardware store looking at each other, two strangers in the wreckage of the city, and Ezra let his hands come down.

"Michael," the man said, and lowered the light out of Ezra's eyes. "My name's Michael."

"Ezra."

"I heard." Michael studied him. "You're not *the* Ezra? On the radio? The carpenter."

"I'm the carpenter."

Michael nodded slowly. "We've been tracking you since you crossed the tree line by the Arboretum. One truck, no headlights, nobody behind you. A man that alone on that road is up to something, so I came in to find out who you were, and whether you'd take everything or only what you needed." He glanced at the duffel, which held only batteries, oil and filters, and at Ezra's open hands. "I'd have bet on a looter. We've been listening to you since the first night. Me and my men. We pick you up off a repeater on a hill north of Baltimore. Every night at nine. That cello." Then his face hardened again, careful. "Show me your hands. Both sides. And your forehead. Push your hair back."

Ezra understood. He turned his wrists over in the light, backs and then palms, and pushed his hair off his forehead with both hands, and held still. No red string. No scan scar. No mark. It had not come yet, not the real thing, only the registration. But they were already checking each other.

"Now you," Ezra said.

Michael's eyebrows went up. Then a smile moved in his beard. He stripped off his gloves and held out his hands and turned them over. He pushed up the front of his helmet. Clean.

"There's two more in the truck," he said. "You want to check them too?"

"You bet I do."

Michael laughed, a short bark, startled out of him. "Good. You'd be surprised how many don't."

The two in the truck were younger, a tall Black man with a shaved head named Dorsey and a red-bearded kid from West Virginia named Pruitt, and they were clean, and they checked Ezra again, and by the end of it all four of them were standing in the first gray light beside the armored truck on Bladensburg Road, laughing, with no good reason to laugh at all. Ezra had not laughed in weeks.

Ezra looked at Michael across the hood of the truck. "You're the ground-hum man."

Michael's beard shifted. "You fixed it?"

"Took me a week."

"Took you a week." Michael nearly smiled. "I was about to drive down there and do it myself."

*Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity.*

\* \* \*

Michael helped him load the duffel. Then he leaned against the armored door with his arms folded over the cross and considered Ezra for a full minute.

"You can't go back to that house," he said.

"I've got twenty-three people—"

"I know what you've got. I've heard you every night. The old man with the record, and the joker who cooks, and the lady who cries." Michael's voice was flat and certain. "The Peacekeepers start moving through the Northeast quadrant Monday. Not the relief workers. The other ones, with the vans. House to house. Anybody unregistered goes on a bus to a processing center in Maryland, and some come back with a card, and some don't come back. They've got your frequency."

"How do you know that?"

"Because they've got mine too, and I've been running for three months." Michael pushed himself off the truck. "I'm not asking you to leave your people. I've got men. I've got trucks. I've got places all over this city and up the coast. My men will move your twenty-three tonight, a few at a time, into houses where the walls are thick and the neighbors are ours. Your old man too." He paused. "And you come north with me."

"North where?"

"Where the supply comes from." Michael held his gaze. "You've got the Word, Ezra. I've heard you. You've got more of it in four months than Grace's preacher got through in fifteen years, and I'd know. I drove her there every Sunday and sat in the truck. But you don't know how to stay alive, and you don't know how to keep anybody else alive, and you're about to have a lot more than twenty-three. You need what I've got. And God knows I need what you've got." He held out his hand. "Come and see."

Ezra thought of the coal bin, and the lamp in the window, and Walt's thermos on the dinner table. He thought of the young Peacekeeper on Walt's porch, smiling, shaking the old man's hand.

He took the hand.

\* \* \*

They moved the twenty-three that night in four trips, in a delivery van with a bakery's name still painted on its side. Walt went last. He would not go until he had seen every one of the others out the back door and into the van. Then he stood in Ezra's kitchen with his cane in one hand and his green ledger under his arm and looked around at the table and the candle and the clippings on the dining room wall, and said, "Well," and nothing else.

Michael was waiting in the back doorway. "He comes with me first," he said to Walt. "I want a look at your man before I take on his people. Then I'll send for the rest of you."

Walt looked him over: the armor, the beard, the cross. "And if you don't like what you see?"

"Then you'll have him back sooner."

Walt grunted. He put his free hand on Ezra's shoulder. "Go and learn something. Then read it to us." He went out the back door into the dark, with Pruitt holding his elbow.

Ezra took the lamp out of the front window last of all, and blew it out, and wrapped it in a towel, and carried it to Michael's truck. His own truck he left in the driveway with the keys on the seat; Pruitt came back for it the next night, and by the end of the week it was in a barn in Maryland, painted flat gray, hauling lumber for the walls.

They drove north all the next day on back roads Michael knew, the long way up through Maryland, doubling back twice, around checkpoints that Dorsey called in on a handheld radio from a mile ahead, through towns where every third house was dark and the churches were boarded up and spray-painted with the gold circle. On some of them, under the circle, a different can had added six words in black: *REPENT YE, AND BELIEVE THE GOSPEL.* Ezra knew them. It was the first thing Jesus preached in Galilee. "Houses on the line," Michael said, without slowing. "See that, and somebody within three streets will feed you." It was dusk when they crossed the Mason-Dixon line into Pennsylvania, and full dark when Michael turned off a county road onto a logging track that climbed into the South Mountain woods, bare and black.

He stopped in a clearing and cut the engine. Nothing sounded but the ticking of the cooling block.

"We walk from here," he said.

They walked a mile uphill in the dark with no lights, Michael in front and Ezra stumbling behind him over roots and rocks, until the woods opened onto a steep slope of jumbled boulders under a cliff, and Michael stopped before a crack in the rock no wider than a doorway.

Before he went in, he did something Ezra did not understand. He took a coil of fine fishing net from a pouch on his vest, nearly invisible, and strung it across the mouth of the crack, hooking it onto small eyes screwed into the rock, and touched a wire at one end to something hidden in a crevice. Then he cut two boughs from a hemlock beside the opening and laid them across the mouth, so that from ten feet away it looked like nothing but rock and a tree.

"Anything touches that net, it rings a bell inside," he said. "Deer, sometimes. But we're ready if it's not."

Inside, the crack became a passage that sloped gently downward, back into the hill, much farther than Ezra would have believed, until it opened all at once into a chamber so large his small light did not reach the far wall.

Michael lit a fire.

It was a great round room of stone, domed overhead, forty feet across. High in the center of the dome was a natural chimney in the rock, and Ezra watched the smoke rise up through it into the night while cold, clean air came down the same way and moved across his face. The firelight lit the walls orange and gold, and the walls hid it completely. From outside, Michael said, you could stand on top of the hill and never know there was a fire under your feet.

Back on the county road, in the last light, Michael had shot a grouse with a bow. He cleaned it now beside the fire, spitted it on a green stick, and turned it over the coals until the fat dripped and hissed, and handed Ezra half of it on a flat stone.

Ezra had not eaten fresh meat since November. He held it a moment, though it burned his fingers, and bowed his head over it. He didn't know the words men said over food. Angela had always said them. "Thank You," he said. "For the bird. And for him." It came out short and awkward, and he ate before it cooled.

When the meat was gone his mouth was dry as sawdust. He looked around the chamber for a jug, a canteen, anything, and found nothing, and did not ask. Michael watched him look.

"Thirsty?"

"I'll make it till morning."

Michael regarded him across the fire a while. Then he pulled a square of white cotton cloth from a pack and tossed it over.

"You won't have to," he said. "Go down that passage about thirty feet. Relieve yourself on that. All the way. Then bring it back."

Ezra stared at him.

"Well," Michael said, "you're the one that's thirsty."

He did it. When he came back holding the wet cloth at arm's length, Michael had set a small scorched tin can into the edge of the coals. He had Ezra drop the cloth into it. When the can began to steam and then to boil, Michael set a collapsible camp pot upside down over it, a pot with a little pouring spout on its rim, and tilted it just so. The steam rose into the cold pot and gathered on its inside and ran down, drop by drop, out through the spout into a tin cup Michael held beneath it.

It took twenty minutes to fill the cup. Michael handed it to him.

It was water. Clear and warm and clean, tasting of nothing at all.

Ezra did not put his face down to it. He drank sitting up, with his eyes on the passage they had come in by. He was not sure yet how far to trust the man across the fire. Michael saw it, and said nothing.

Then Ezra lowered the cup and looked into it, at the cloth and the can and himself, and he started to laugh and could not stop, and Michael sat across the fire with the corner of his mouth turned up in his beard.

\* \* \*

Over the fire that night, Michael told him a little. Only a little.

"Twenty-two years a soldier," he said. "Desert Shield, Desert Storm, every bad place after. Then six years with a badge in a county north of Baltimore. Then a desk, nights, keeping strangers out of a bank's computers." He turned the stick in the coals. "I had a wife named Grace. A boy named Daniel. Twelve years old. I drove them to church every Sunday for fifteen years and sat out in the lot with the engine running and a laptop on the wheel, watching other people's networks. Thinking about the temper I'd spent my whole life keeping a hand on."

The fire popped. He waited it out.

"Twenty-sixth of November I came off a night shift at six. Pulled into my own driveway. Front door standing open. Daniel's bicycle on its side in the yard." His voice did not change, and that was the worst of it. "Grace's side of the bed was made. And empty."

"Everybody who was in the book was taken," he said, staring into the fire. "Everybody who was ready. Fifteen years in a parking lot, and I wasn't ready." He was silent. "But we still have a chance. Don't we? The ones left. Tell me that. You're the one who reads."

Ezra had been asked that question twenty times since December, at the dinner table and on the air. He had never been asked it by a man in armor in a cave.

"Yes," he said. "John saw it. A great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, standing before the throne in white robes. And one of the elders asked him who they were. And the answer was, *These are they which came out of great tribulation, and have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.* (Revelation 7:14) That's the ones who were taken, Michael. The ones who were ready." He leaned toward the fire. "But John saw others, too. Under the altar, the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and for the testimony which they held. *And white robes were given unto every one of them…* (Revelation 6:11) That's for the ones who come to Him now, whatever it costs. God's still calling people. Jesus told the church at Sardis, *…I will not blot out his name out of the book of life…* (Revelation 3:5)"

Michael's jaw was tight. "The church," he said roughly. "When they left, the restrainer left with them. That's what Grace used to tell me in the truck on the way home. *only he who now letteth will let, until he be taken out of the way.* (2 Thessalonians 2:7) And now all hell's loose on the ones who are left."

"The restraint is gone," Ezra said. "Second Thessalonians two. The hand that was holding it all back has stepped away, and it's coming. But the Spirit didn't leave, Michael. I felt Him come. On my kitchen floor, in the dark, with my hands out, after they were all gone. The Comforter came anyway." He held Michael's eyes. "Joel says it. *…I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh… whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered…* (Joel 2:28, 32) That's not a promise for before. That's a promise for now."

Michael held his gaze across the fire. Then he bowed his head over his folded hands, and his shoulders moved once, and Ezra looked away into the dark and let him be.

\* \* \*

Michael kept him in the front of the cave for days and nights.

He called it resting up. There was nothing to do in the front of the cave but wait, and Ezra waited. Once a day a nameless man brought a tin plate of beans and a heel of bread, and left it without a word.

On the first night a young man came in shivering from the cold outside, and Ezra gave him his blanket and sat the rest of the night against the wall. On the second day there was half a loaf left over and no one in the cave but him, and he broke it in three and left two pieces on the ledge by the passage for whoever came by. When a man with a lantern asked him whether he had gone farther into the hill in the night, he said that he had walked ten steps down the left passage looking for water, and turned back when the dark began to frighten him. It would have cost him nothing to say no.

Those days were a stripping down. He sat in the dark front chamber with a candle and Angela's Bible and nothing to fix, nothing to build, no one to read to and no one to perform for, and he prayed more in those days than in the forty-four years before them. *That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire.*

On the fourth morning, Michael came down the passage with a lantern and stopped in front of him.

"Grace and peace unto you," he said.

Ezra stood up and dusted off the knees of his pants. "And unto you."

"Follow me."

Past the great dome the cave split three ways. Michael stopped at the fork and held the lantern high so Ezra could see all three mouths.

"Pick one."

Ezra looked from one to the next. "Does it matter?"

"Not to me. Front's kept dark on purpose. Whichever one you take, forty feet in you step on a plate and a light comes on behind you." Michael tipped his head at a black notch high in the left wall. "And a man up in there has your whole left side."

"With a rifle."

"With a bow. A rifle tells the whole hill."

"You'd shoot a man for picking the wrong hole?"

"There's no wrong hole. He picks one and he gets a light. What he does in the light is up to him." Michael took the left passage. "This one. Mind your head."

At the end of it was a small chamber, and in its back wall a slab of gray rock taller than either of them. Michael set his palm flat on one spot and leaned. The slab swung inward without a sound.

Ezra couldn't help it. "Who hung that?"

"A man who'd have liked you." Behind the slab stood a steel door, and behind the door an elevator with a single brass button. "Army put in the steel. My father put in the rock."

"Your father."

"Hold the rail. It's a long way down."

When the doors opened, Ezra did not step out. He stood in the opening and stared.

Concrete corridors ran off in three directions under steady white lights. Men and women of every age went by in work clothes and sweaters, carrying crates, pushing carts, talking low. None of them wore blue.

"What is this place?"

"An old volcano. Fell in on itself before Adam." Michael stepped out and waited for him. "The Army dug into the wall of it in the fifties, for a war with the Russians that never came, and then forgot it. My father didn't forget it. Bought it for scrap in eighty-nine. Paid less than he paid for his truck." A woman with a clipboard nodded to him as she passed, and he nodded back. "He left it to me. I gave it to the church."

"Which church?"

Michael looked down the corridor at the people. "This one."

On the wall facing the elevator, painted in plain black letters a foot high, was the first half of the verse Kambria had copied under Angela's prayer:

*AND AT THAT TIME SHALL MICHAEL STAND UP, THE GREAT PRINCE WHICH STANDETH FOR THE CHILDREN OF THY PEOPLE.* DANIEL 12:1.

"Children of Thy People," Michael said. "That's what the sign says. The ones who live here just call it the Rock. My mother named me out of that verse. I never read it till December." He glanced at Ezra sidelong. "You and me both, I guess."

He put something into Ezra's hand: a smooth white stone the size of an egg, with nothing written on it.

"What's this for?"

"Nobody comes down here who isn't brought. Three days in the front, and the elders hear how you spent them. You gave away your blanket. You split your bread three ways. You told the truth about ten steps in the dark when a lie was free. And the first night, you drank with your eyes on the door." He closed Ezra's fingers over it. "Everyone who stays gets one of those. What's written on it is between you and Him."

Ezra turned the stone over. "What's on yours?"

The corner of Michael's mouth moved. "Nice try."

Jesus promised it to the church at Pergamos: *To him that overcometh will I give to eat of the hidden manna, and will give him a white stone, and in the stone a new name written, which no man knoweth saving he that receiveth it.* (Revelation 2:17)

The storerooms went back farther than the lights. Sacks of rice and wheat and beans, cans and jars, water in blue drums stacked three high.

"How many does this feed?" Ezra said.

"Two hundred and twelve, as of this morning. Four years, if nobody gets greedy. Three if they do."

"And if they do?"

"Then God will make provisions for His people."

In the radio room a map of the Eastern seaboard covered one wall, crossed with lines in colored marker, the routes the supply trucks ran. Along each line sat small round stickers, each with a number in black. Michael touched each one as he passed. Four along the Baltimore line. Two in Delaware. One he did not name, in the Pennsylvania hills.

"These are our distribution channels," he said. "Each one counts. Each one guards. Each one knows only the next. No one but me knows what all the numbers mean."

"Seven," Ezra said. "Seven candlesticks."

Michael looked at him. "You counted."

"Twenty-some years of measuring twice."

"Good. We could use an eager mind and a good memory down here."

At the console, a young operator in a headset, a thin kid named Anton with a pencil behind his ear and a milk crate of burned discs under his chair, turned and said, "Boss. Peacekeeper traffic."

Michael took the headset from the young man, listened, wrote one word on the pad with his father's square capitals, and held it up for Ezra to see.

*PALEHORSE.*

"Third time this week," Michael said. "Cold-chain trucks. Registered clinics only. The dispatcher says it like a lot number. 'PALEHORSE Lot Four, staged at Fort Detrick.' I don't know what it is."

Ezra had to wet his lips. "I do."

He told him, standing in the radio room with the numbered map at his back: the drive in the spine of the concordance, the files, the nursing homes, the woman in the cell and the camera that had watched her go. When he finished Michael had put the headset down and did not seem to know what to do with his hands.

"A plague that picks its victims by the ledger," Michael said, very low. "Revelation six, verse eight."

"*And power was given unto them over the fourth part of the earth, to kill with sword, and with hunger, and with death, and with the beasts of the earth.*"

"Then there's no hiding in the registry," Michael said. "And no hiding outside it, if they decide to come after us in the same breath." His jaw set.

Michael led him down a long corridor to a steel door and stopped with his hand on it. "You've been hearing it for an hour."

Ezra listened. A deep, even hum came up through the floor. "I thought it was the lights."

"It's what runs the lights." Michael opened the door. "Water."

The door gave onto a gallery cut out of the living rock. Thirty feet below, a black stream ran fast and cold through a stone channel and turned the blades of two iron wheels set into the flow. Heavy cables climbed the wall from the wheels and ran off into the dark. Over the door, in the same black letters as the sign by the elevator:

*He shall dwell on high: his place of defence shall be the munitions of rocks: bread shall be given him; his waters shall be sure.* (Isaiah 33:16)

"I didn't pick that one either," Michael said. "I read it in January and found it was already true."

"Where's it come from? The water."

"Off the rim. Rain and snowmelt go into the mountain through a fault the old miners never found. We dammed it upstream behind a wall you could walk past all day."

"And when it floods?"

"Side channel. Sinks back into the stone. This floor's never seen an inch." He pointed down at the wheels. "Those turn the generators. Lights, pumps, fans, the radio. All of it off one creek."

"No fuel," Ezra said.

"No fuel to haul, nothing for a drone to smell, and no switch on a desk in New Jersey." Michael leaned on the rail. "That's the part I like."

Below the wheels the water stepped down through a stair of stone basins, gravel, then sand, then charcoal, then white cotton stretched on wooden frames.

Ezra looked at the cotton a long time. "That's the rag."

"Same as the cloth I gave you that first night. Only bigger."

"You could've just told me."

"You'd have forgotten it by morning." Michael pushed off the rail. "You won't forget that cloth."

Where the cold water ran closest to the food rooms, the rock had been hollowed into long chambers where the air stood at the edge of freezing. "A cold room the Lord built," Michael said. "All I did was dig out the door."

Ezra pointed up at a round iron grille in the ceiling. "And those?"

"Lungs. Forty shafts to the surface, under brush and boulders and a dead oak stump. Clear day, the caps come off and the whole mountain breathes." Michael laid his palm on the passage wall and waited until Ezra did the same. The stone was warm, warm as a hand. "The old fire, way down. It's like this all year."

In the last gallery men and women worked with hammers and chisels in a pale cloud of dust, widening a passage by lamplight. Masons, quarrymen, a few old tunnelers out of the subway. "The stone company," Michael said. "What they cut comes out as walls and steps. The rest goes up to the growing rooms, and whatever's left goes into the floors of the passages we'd rather nobody came down."

"Who taught you to build like this?"

Michael walked several steps before he answered. "My father. Always ready. Cans in the basement when other men on our block were buying lottery tickets. Water barrels. A generator he ran on the first of every month to make sure it still would. They laughed at him on the block, and at church." He tapped the wall twice with two fingers. "He used to say a man who prepares isn't afraid, he's obedient. Noah. Joseph in Egypt. He deeded me forty acres of mountain and a bunker the Army had given up on, the year I came home from the Army, with his water drums already lined up inside. He was in his chair the morning of the twenty-sixth. Now he's not."

At the next door Michael tried the handle, let go, and tried it again.

"You did that at the last one too," Ezra said.

"I count things. Doors, drums, people. My mother called it a sickness. My father called it inventory."

"What do you call it?"

Michael thought about it. "Staying alive long enough to be useful."

The gallery narrowed to a cut no wider than a man's shoulders, and Michael stopped at its mouth with his lamp held low. The channel ran on ahead of them, a hand's breadth of black water in a groove as straight as a rule.

"The masons dug this from both ends," he said. "Two crews, one working up from the water and one working down from the grow rooms, cutting toward each other by the sound of the chisels."

"How close did they come?"

"A finger's width." He kept his eyes on the channel. "We'd read how it was done before we ever picked up a hammer." *This same Hezekiah also stopped the upper watercourse of Gihon, and brought it straight down to the west side of the city of David.* (2 Chronicles 32:30) "He closed the springs outside his walls so the Assyrians would come and find nothing. *Why should the kings of Assyria come, and find much water?* (2 Chronicles 32:4) Vale's patrols have walked this whole ridge. They found a creek that runs thin in August and goes under the rock. Everything it carries comes out in one place, and that place is not on any map."

"They heard each other through the rock," Ezra said. "The two crews."

Michael looked at him. "Through the rock. That's the whole trick of this place."

The cut opened into a long hall under blue-white lamps, and Ezra smelled green before he saw it. Troughs stood in racks to the ceiling, lettuce and beans and young wheat, and water ran down the racks in a thin bright sheet, over the roots and out the far end, where it was drawn off, run through the charcoal and sand, and sent round again. The beds were not filled with earth. They were filled with stone, ground fine and sifted, the same stone the masons had taken out of the channel.

"Nothing leaves the mountain," Michael said. "Not the water, not the rock. What we cut out, we grow in." He put two fingers into a trough and let the water run over them. "The channel was Hezekiah's way of keeping a city alive through a siege. This is the same channel. It only ends in something green."

But the water did not stop at the green. Past the last rack the channel dropped through a cut in the floor, and Michael took a lamp off its hook and led him down eleven steps into a room he had saved for last.

It was a pool. Square, cut straight down into the living rock, as long as a pickup truck and deep enough to stand in to the chest. Stone steps went down into it on two sides. The channel spilled in at one end over a lip of rock, in a sheet as clear as glass, and left by a grate at the other, on its way back to the wheels. The water was so still and so clean that the bottom looked closer than it was. One lamp hung over it. Above the inflow, cut into the rock in the same black letters as the verses upstairs, was a single word.

*SILOAM.*

And along the lip where the channel spilled in, smaller, the masons had chiseled two more: *ENGEDI* on the near side of the stream and *ENEGLAIM* on the far.

"The masons named it," Michael said. "I didn't argue." He set the lamp on the top step. "Jesus spat in the dirt and put it on a blind man's eyes and told him, *Go, wash in the pool of Siloam, (which is by interpretation, Sent.)* (John 9:7) Hezekiah's tunnel came out at that pool. Ours comes out at this one." He looked at the water. "Everybody who stays goes down into it. Not because the water does anything. Because He said to."

Ezra crouched and touched the letters on the lip. "What's this?"

"Ezekiel forty-seven." Michael didn't need the Book. "He sees water coming out from under the threshold of the temple. Ankle deep, then knee deep, then a river a man can't cross. It runs down into the Dead Sea, and the dead water turns sweet. *And every thing shall live whither the river cometh.* (Ezekiel 47:9) And then fishermen stand on the shore *from Engedi even unto Eneglaim*, spreading their nets." (Ezekiel 47:10)

He nodded at the near side. "Everybody knows where Engedi is. Springs over the Dead Sea. David hid in the caves there with his men while Saul hunted him." He nodded at the far side. "Nobody knows where Eneglaim is. Men have dug for it for two hundred years. One says it's a spring on the north shore. One says it's across the water in Moab. One says it's under the salt. Nobody's found it." He picked the lamp up. "The masons figured it this way. The river starts at a place everybody can find, and it ends at a place nobody can. You don't get there by a map. You get there by following the water."

Ezra stood at the edge. The cold came up off the surface into his face.

"I was baptized," he said. "At Pilgrim Rest. I was six." He heard his own voice go flat. "My father put me under. Deacon Patterson, in his white robe, with the whole church singing. He held me down a little too long. I came up choking, and he laughed, and four hundred people laughed with him."

Michael didn't say anything for a while.

"What did it mean?" he asked finally.

"That my father was a deacon."

Michael nodded slowly, as if Ezra had answered a different question, the right one. He picked up the lamp and went back up the steps and left Ezra standing alone at the edge of the water, looking at the word on the wall.

And there was one more room, with a long table and shelves to the ceiling. Michael opened it, and stood back, and let Ezra enter alone.

It was a library. Bibles, hundreds of them, in every size and binding. Concordances. Old commentaries with cracked spines. Hymnals. Children's Bibles with pictures. And on one long shelf, notebooks: handwritten copies of whole books of Scripture, Psalms and John and Romans and Isaiah, written out from memory and from borrowed pages by people who had watched the printed ones seized and burned.

"They're burning them now," Michael said from the doorway. "Not everywhere. Not yet. But in the cities the Organization's collecting them. Divisive literature. My men pull them out of the fires when they can, and out of the warehouses, and out of the empty churches. We keep the Word two ways down here: on the page, and in the heart. Somebody memorizes, somebody copies." He paused. "You ever hear about the Bibles of Petra?"

Ezra shook his head.

"My grandmother told it to me. Years ago, Christians carried Bibles into the caves at Petra, in Jordan, and hid them there, waiting for the day Israel would have to flee into the wilderness and need them." Michael shrugged. "I don't know if it's true. I live by it anyway. Every cave on my routes has Bibles hidden in it for whoever comes after us."

Ezra stood among the shelves with his hand on the long table and looked at the spines.

He thought of Baruch, Jeremiah's scribe, whom he had read about in January. The king had sat by the fire in his winter house and cut Jeremiah's scroll with a penknife, three or four columns at a time, and thrown it in the fire until it was consumed. And God had said to Jeremiah: *Take thee again another roll, and write in it all the former words.* So Baruch sat down and wrote it all again, from Jeremiah's mouth. And added, the Book said, *many like words.*

"Who does the copying?" he asked.

"Whoever can," Michael said. "Not enough of us."

"I can," Ezra said. "I'm a scribe."

Michael nodded. "Then you'll need hands. Who do you want up here? Not all twenty-three. The ones you'd build with."

Ezra didn't have to think. "Walt. Elijah. Mrs. Okafor, she copies faster than I do. Dolores. Marcus."

"The joker who cooks, and the lady who cries."

"The lady who cries prays all night," Ezra said. "You'll want her."

Michael wrote the five names on his pad in square capitals. "Four days."

\* \* \*

He stayed twenty-one days.

Michael called it a Daniel fast, and kept it with him, as every new leader in the camp had kept it. *I ate no pleasant bread, neither came flesh nor wine in my mouth, neither did I anoint myself at all, till three whole weeks were fulfilled.* Vegetables, beans and water. Daniel had been told, at the end of his three weeks, that his prayer was heard on the first day, but the prince of the kingdom of Persia had withstood the angel one and twenty days, until Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help.

The first days had been the testing. The middle days were the training. Michael's men took him through the passages until he could walk them blind. They taught him the routes and the safe houses and the code words, and how to speak on a radio so that anyone listening would hear nothing they could use. They taught him how to read a checkpoint from a mile away and when to turn around. They were men who had come to Michael with nothing, Dorsey said, the way the distressed and the debtors and the discontented had come to David in the cave of Adullam, four hundred of them, and he became a captain over them. Dorsey drilled the first rule of escort work into him on the fifth night: you never walk a stranger anywhere he hasn't earned. Everyone who came to the Rock came in at night, hooded or behind a blacked-out windshield, with a man at each elbow, and spent three days in the front rooms being watched before anyone showed him a door. Every morning and every night, Ezra taught them the Word at the long table in the library, and they heard him gladly.

On the sixth day Michael put the bow in his hands himself. Ezra's first arrow went into the wall a yard left of the straw.

"You're aiming," Michael said.

"That's the idea."

"Quit it. Look at the spot you want. Your hands know where your eyes are."

The second arrow went into the straw, low. Michael grunted. "Now you're a danger to rabbits."

In the hours between lessons, he copied. He sat at the long table under the white lights with a stack of blank notebooks and a box of black pens and a Bible open before him, and he wrote out the Gospel of John word by word in his square capitals, because a carton of children's Bibles had been burned in Hartford and somebody had to replace them. His hand cramped. He kept writing. *In the beginning was the Word.* He thought of his son in the studio, writing a song in a notebook one measure at a time, for seven years, for a father who never came to hear it.

On the eighth night the bell rang inside.

Ezra was at the long table copying when Dorsey put his head in the door. "Van's at the net. Boss says you'll want to see this."

They came in hooded, one at a time, with a man at each elbow, as everyone did. Five of them. The first stopped dead in the mouth of the passage and said from under his hood, "If this is a kidnapping, young man, I'm seventy-four and I bruise."

"Walt," Ezra said.

Dorsey pulled the hood off. Walt blinked in the firelight at the dome, at the smoke going up the chimney, at Ezra standing there thinner than he had left him. He set his cane against the rock with great care.

"Well," he said. Then he said nothing at all, and held on to Ezra by both arms.

Elijah came out of his hood already talking. "Three hours on the floor of a bread truck. I counted every pothole in Delaware. Ez, you look terrible. Is there food? Tell me there's food."

Behind them came Mrs. Okafor, then Dolores with her hand on Pruitt's arm, then Marcus, rubbing his back.

Michael stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms folded over the cross and gave them a minute. Then he stepped in.

"Three days in the front," he said. "Same as he did. Same as everybody."

Walt looked up at him. "You sent for us."

"Said I would."

"I've listened to him on a tape in a bread box for a week." Walt tapped the green ledger under his arm. "He says you count things."

"I do."

"So do I. We'll get along fine, or we won't."

The corner of Michael's mouth moved in his beard. "Dorsey. Find this man a good chair."

Ezra was not allowed to go to them, and he did not try.

On the second day Dorsey reported that Elijah had talked the man who brought the beans into letting him season them. On the second night Dolores prayed out loud until nearly dawn, for every soul in the hill whose name she knew, and when she ran out of names she prayed for the ones she didn't. Marcus gave his blanket to Walt. Walt complained about the cold, the dark, the beans and the hour, and on the third morning, when the man with the lantern asked whether he had gone down any of the passages in the night, he said, "Twice. Found a wall and my own bad knee."

Michael came for them on the fourth morning with five white stones in his pocket.

Ezra's nine o'clock reading had been recorded at the long table in the library every night since his first week in the hill. Anton ran a cable down the corridor from the radio room, and the tapes went south. On the fourteenth night there were two microphones on the table instead of one.

Walt sat at the new one with his ledger open. Ezra sat at the other with Angela's Bible. Elijah leaned in the doorway with a dish towel over his shoulder, Mrs. Okafor beside him, and Michael stood at the back with his arms folded.

"Rolling," Anton said.

The cello came first, off the tablet Michael had carried out of his son's room. Then Walt: "This is Walt, with the record." He read the week. Bread, fuel, the Census, the rain that had not fallen in Jerusalem. Then Ezra read the chapter. Then the boxed line.

"Out," Anton said.

Nothing went out from the mountain. At dawn the tape went south under the flour in a bread box, and the next night at nine, from a rooftop in Washington, Pruitt played it into the transmitter and moved to another roof before the vans could turn their heads.

After that the long table was never empty. Mornings, Ezra taught. Walt sat at his elbow with the ledger and stopped him when he got a date wrong. Mrs. Okafor copied Romans at the far end, faster than anyone. Elijah fed them all and had the masons laughing by the second day. Dolores took the night watch in the radio room with Anton and prayed over every tape before it went south. Marcus learned the routes from Dorsey and could draw them from memory inside a week. Michael stood at the back with his arms folded, and some mornings he stayed for the whole of it.

On the seventeenth day, at night, Ezra went down into Siloam.

He asked. Nobody asked him. He found Michael in the radio room after the last watch change and said it the way the eunuch said it on the Gaza road, because it was the only way he knew to say it: "See, here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized?"

Michael took off the headset. "*If thou believest with all thine heart, thou mayest.*" (Acts 8:36–37)

"I believe," Ezra said. "With all of it. What's left of it."

They went down at midnight. Word had gone through the passages, and when Ezra came down the eleven steps in a white T-shirt and his work pants, there were people on the stairs. Thirty, forty, sitting close on the cold stone with their knees drawn up, the masons still gray with dust, Walt on the top step with his cane across his knees, Elijah one step down, Anton with his headset around his neck, Dorsey at the bottom with a towel over his arm. Somebody had hung a second lamp.

Michael was already in the water to his waist. He held out his arm, not his hand, and Ezra took it at the elbow and went down the steps into the cold.

It took his breath. It came up his legs and over his belt and into his chest, and he stood there gasping in the lamplight, and the people on the stairs began, very low, to sing. *Wade in the water.* The old spiritual. The masons knew it. Anton didn't, and learned it by the second line. Walt knew every word.

Michael put one hand flat between Ezra's shoulder blades and the other over his folded hands.

"Ezra Patterson," he said. "Upon your profession of faith in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was buried and rose again, I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost." His voice dropped. "*Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life.*" (Romans 6:4)

Then he laid him back.

Under, it was black and cold and absolutely silent, and for one long second Ezra was six years old in a white robe with a man's hand holding him down too long. Then the water closed over his face and the second passed, and there was nothing under there but quiet and the far, steady hum of the wheels coming through the stone. No one was holding him down. He was being held.

Michael brought him up.

He came up into the lamplight with the water pouring off his head and his first breath tearing in, and the stairs were full of people singing, and Michael was holding him by both shoulders and looking straight into his face.

"Sent," Michael said.

Dorsey wrapped the towel around him at the top of the steps. Ezra stood there dripping and shaking with cold, and he could not stop smiling, and he did not try.

The last days were the rising. He was thinner than he had been since he was twenty; he had punched two new holes in his belt with Dorsey's awl. On the eighteenth day he led the prayers in the bunker for the first time, a hundred and forty people standing in the main corridor with their heads bowed. On the twentieth day he rode out with Michael's men on a supply run to a church basement in New Haven and carried sacks of rice down a back stairway in the dark to families who had not eaten in four days.

On the twenty-first day, in the radio room, Anton called them in. Walt came behind them with his ledger.

"Jerusalem," he said, and turned up the speaker.

It was the two witnesses.

It was not coming through Cyprus, or Maine, or any relay Anton knew. It was on the dead band at the bottom of the dial, the same band Ezra read on, every word clear, and the needle on the signal meter sat at full and did not move.

Anton was turning knobs. "There's no carrier," he said. "Boss, nothing's transmitting. I've got no source."

"Then quit looking for one," Michael said.

Two old voices speaking English in turns, standing on a stone plaza in a city where it had not rained in weeks. They were speaking to the Council directly. The delegates had come out onto the balcony of their new offices above the plaza to listen, because the whole world was watching and they could not pretend otherwise.

"*And thou, profane wicked prince of Israel,*" one old voice said, "*whose day is come, when iniquity shall have an end, Thus saith the Lord GOD; Remove the diadem, and take off the crown…*"

And the other: "*…exalt him that is low, and abase him that is high. I will overturn, overturn, overturn, it: and it shall be no more, until he come whose right it is; and I will give it him.*"

Somewhere a jammer came on. They heard it start, a rising whine under the voices, and climb until the speaker buzzed with it. The two old voices went on over the top of it, every word clean. The jammer made no difference.

"*…until he come whose right it is,*" the first voice said. "Ye build a temple and a law for the nations, and leave Him out. Jesus told you so himself: *Ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord.* (Matthew 23:39)"

Then the old men were done speaking, and there was nothing but hiss.

It did not stay quiet. Within the hour the band was full. Kyle, from his basement in Brooklyn: *Did you hear it?* A ham in Ontario. A woman in Lagos who had never keyed a radio in her life. A trucker outside Amarillo on a CB that should not have reached past the next exit. All of them had heard it, clear, on whatever they had. Anton logged them until his pencil broke.

"Salazar owns the whole sky," Michael said. "And he can't shut up two old men."

The radio room was silent. Michael stood with his arms folded. Ezra stood beside him with his hand on the back of the operator's chair.

"That's for us," Ezra said finally. "All of it. That's what the radio is for."

Michael did not answer at once. He had stood at the back of the library every night for three weeks while Anton recorded Ezra's nine o'clock reading, and he had listened to every one, the way he listened to everything, for the structure under the words. Nothing went out from the mountain itself. The tapes went south in the supply trucks. "Everything above us is Salazar's," Michael had told him the first night. "If it goes up, he hears it. If it's warm, he sees it through a roof. So we use a radio a ham could have built in 1950, and we carry the tapes out by hand."

"You close every night on one line," he said. "Same place in the reading. A boxed line, you told me."

"It's how she marked a page."

"The operator logs them." He took a ledger from under the console and turned it to the last page. "Twenty-one nights. Twenty-one lines. A hundred and fifty psalms in the Book, and I've watched you go through a dozen of them, and no one in Washington who hears the tapes thinks it odd that a preacher closes on a psalm." He laid his finger on the ledger. "I laid out my own map in the fall, before I ever heard your voice. I numbered it the way my father numbered his shelves, by the Book. A hundred and fifty columns, for the psalms. Rows by the verse. I told myself it was habit."

"A hundred and fifty columns," Walt said. He was already running a finger down his own right margin. "I've written a verse beside every day's news since the summer."

"I know," Michael said. "I've been reading your tapes."

"Then you know I don't get them wrong."

"That's why it'll hold."

Ezra looked at the map on the wall, at the grid of fine pencil lines across the mountains and the river valleys and the cities, and then at the ledger.

"A reference is a place," Michael said. "*He shall cover thee with his feathers, and under his wings shalt thou trust.* (Psalm 91:4) Column ninety-one, row four. To a stranger, that is a verse. To one of ours, it is a house with a cellar, and a man at the door who will take you in."

"And the notebooks?"

"Tell them what is in the square." Michael had thought it through, and Ezra could see it in the way he said it, one line at a time, each one set before the next. "You open to the color for the night. The gold one, for the church: a shelter, a household of ours. The green: supplies, food and water and medicine and fuel, what is stored and where. The red: a road, a crossing, a guide who will meet you. The blue: a door in the mountains. Anyone can copy the pages, and half the city has. Without the verse that names the square, they are a Bible study."

"And the hidden stores?"

Michael almost smiled. "Those are in no color, and on no map." He nodded at the black notebook under Ezra's hand. "The back pages. A date, a psalm. In your own square capitals, in your own ink, so that nobody who ever gets the book away from you will find anything but a workman's list. I'll tell you the stores. You write them down. If I am taken, you will still know where the bread is."

Ezra thought of Angela, drawing a box around the one line she wanted to survive. He thought of her saying she did not trust any page to keep her words but one.

"The same words open the servers," Michael said. "I'll have Nina key every box we run to the day's verse, spoken aloud. A password can be taken off a man. A verse he knows by heart cannot."

"I'll read them as Scripture," Ezra said, "because they are."

"From tonight you open to the page I give you," Michael said, "and not before."

"That's the only way they'll hold."

\* \* \*

They went back to Washington on the twenty-second morning, in the back of a bakery van. Ezra had Kambria's lamp wrapped in a towel on his knees and a box of handwritten Gospels of John at his feet. Walt had a white stone in his pocket and the ledger on his lap. Elijah was asleep on the flour sacks before they reached the county road.

The hidden church had not shrunk while they were gone. It had grown. Michael's safe houses were full, and the people in them had brought their neighbors, and their neighbors had brought theirs. Walt was living in the attic of a row house on Capitol Hill that belonged to a retired schoolteacher, keeping the record by lamplight and broadcasting it every other night through a battery transmitter Nina had built into a hollowed-out console radio from the nineteen forties. On the nights between, the same radio played the readings Ezra had recorded in the mountain, which came down the line in a bread box under the flour. Nobody looked twice at an old console radio. Elijah was running food at night on a bicycle with a trailer, singing under his breath, making the old folks in the dark rooms laugh along with him.

Ezra built false walls. He built them in basements and attics and the backs of closets, in row houses and churches and the stockrooms of shuttered stores, all over the city, three or four a week, with lumber Michael's men brought down from the north in trucks marked as relief supplies. Behind every one, there was soon a room full of people, reading. In every one, at nine o'clock, someone switched on a radio, and the low line of a cello came out of it, and then a working man's voice, reading the plans.

They taught each other a watchword that spring, from Isaiah, because the people were so afraid. Ezra said it to the first family he put behind a false wall, an old couple from Anacostia and their grown grandson, and they said it back to him, and by summer it was in every room in the city.

*Strengthen ye the weak hands, and confirm the feeble knees. Say to them that are of a fearful heart, Be strong, fear not.*

He thought about that verse more than any other. He had sworn at eight, sitting on his hands in the front pew of his father's church, that he would never lift them to God as long as he lived. Now he spent his days teaching frightened people to lift up the hands that hung down.

\* \* \*

The hidden church grew from dozens to hundreds that year, and past a thousand the year after, scattered through the city in rooms behind walls. The two old men in sackcloth went on preaching on their plaza in Jerusalem, and it did not rain in Israel, and the Council built its temple anyway, on the mountain, with water brought in by truck. The springs came and went. Walt filled one green ledger after another. Ezra's hair went gray at the temples.

And on a night in a later spring, in a room behind a false wall under an old library on Kennedy Street, Nina came down the hidden stairs from New York with her coat still on, as she always did, and stayed standing.

Her eyes were red and her hands shook.

"They're connecting Continuity to Jerusalem," she said. "The servers stay in New York, in the vault. But they've built a shrine in the new temple, a lens in the center of the floor that throws a face up into the air, and they want cameras and microphones all around it, so the thing in the vault can see the crowd and talk to it from six thousand miles away." Her voice dropped. "Kroll's sending me over to wire it. Twelve days. They want it running by Passover."

Ezra looked up from the table, where he had been copying the twenty-fourth chapter of Matthew into a fresh notebook.

"Crowe's going to Jerusalem for Passover," Nina said. "To the temple. To speak. Vale arranged the whole thing." She sat down at last, across from him, her legs giving out. "Ezra. They built a copy of a man who's still alive, and now they're carrying it into the holy place. Why would they need a copy of Adrian Crowe, unless they already know he's not coming home?"
