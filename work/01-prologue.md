## Prologue: The Trumpet

> *And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him.* — Genesis 5:24

**Word study: *walked* — הָלַךְ** Hebrew *halak* (Strong's H1980). To walk, to go, to keep going. Here it is in a form that means to walk about continually, as a way of life. Enoch did not take one walk with God. He lived walking with Him, day after day, until God took him home.

Long before the flood, a man named Enoch walked out past his fields with God and never walked back. His son searched until dark. The sandal prints ran down the path and ended, the last one pressed deep, the dirt beyond it smooth. He *was not found, because God had translated him: for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God.* (Hebrews 11:5)

\* \* \*

The sound came first.

Everyone in Washington heard it. Ezra Patterson, a widowed carpenter and the father of two, slept through all of it.

He would hear it described a thousand times in the weeks that followed, by people whose hands still shook when they told it.

It began with light: one white flash, lightning with no storm behind it, that threw every window in the city into noon for the length of a heartbeat. Then the sound came down.

It was thunder and trumpet together, a single colossal crack that fell out of the sky from every direction at once and hit the city like a wall, a sonic boom with a horn's voice inside it. Glass burst in its frames up and down every street. Storefronts came down in sheets. Windshields went white. The kitchen window over Ezra's sink split from corner to corner. Car alarms screamed by the thousand, and the shards were still falling when the sound came back across the rooftops a second time, and a third.

Behind it came the wind. It had no direction. It came down the avenues and up the alleys at the same time, tore the last leaves off the trees in one breath, flipped trash cans, peeled tin off a church roof on Rhode Island Avenue and sailed it over three houses. On the Potomac the water stood up in the channel, gray and white, and ran backward against the tide, and the river roared under the bridges like something alive.

Grown men dropped to their knees on the sidewalk. Some of them did not get up. A bus driver on Georgia Avenue pulled to the curb and slumped over the wheel, and two of his passengers went down with him, and their hearts had simply stopped. People who heard it said the same thing afterward, in every language on earth: it was the most terrible sound they had ever heard, and it sounded like it was calling someone.

Ezra slept. He had fallen asleep with a drink in him, the way his father used to, and the dead sleep of it held through all of it.

He was on the couch, still in yesterday's work jeans, one boot on. The television was murmuring to itself. He had been dreaming the red dream again: the red room, the red moon, the whole sky the color of blood. What woke him in the end was the silence that followed, and he came up out of the dream with his shirt stuck to his back. It smelled of the bar: stale beer, chicken wings and onion rings. Under that, faint, a perfume that wasn't Angela's. He lay there hating the man who had worn that shirt. In the half second between sleeping and waking he thought, *It's the dream again. The red room.*

But the room wasn't red. It was dark.

Beyond the front window the sky was black, the streetlights burning along the rooftops, with no trace of morning in it anywhere. Only the dream, then. Relief ran through him, warm and brief.

Then he heard what was under the quiet. Car alarms, dozens of them, all over Brookland, thin and far off and endless. A siren that climbed and did not come down. Cold air was moving across the floor from the kitchen, where it had no business coming from. The furnace was silent. The television had gone to a soft gray hiss.

He sat up. His late father's watch said 3:17. It had stopped at 3:17 more than thirty years ago.

"Jalen?"

Nothing.

He took the stairs two at a time. His eighteen-year-old son's door was open, and that was the first wrong thing; Jalen slept with the door shut and a towel pushed along the bottom so his father wouldn't hear him playing recordings at two in the morning. He had come home from his dorm downtown earlier that week for the Thanksgiving holiday. The bed was unmade, the sheet still holding the long shape of him. The cello leaned against the wall by the window, its bow laid across the seat of the chair, rosin dust pale on the strings. It was the cello he had played every night in the studio they had built together, back before Angela got sick. On the pillow, Jalen's phone was ringing with an alarm that no one was there to silence.

"Jalen. This isn't funny."

He crossed the hall to the room of his six-year-old daughter, Mia. Her nightlight was still on, throwing paper stars across the ceiling. Her nightgown, the yellow one with the moons, lay in the bed in the shape of a small sleeping girl, the blanket still tucked neatly around nothing. On the pillow lay her birthday book, *The Kingdom of Little Things: Captain Crumb and the Tangled Web*, open face down where Jalen had stopped reading.

He touched it. It was warm. And the small hope he had been holding, that it had only been the dream, went out of him all at once.

He called her name, then called it louder, his voice cracking. He checked the closet, under the bed, the bathroom, the attic stairs, the garage. He ran out into the backyard barefoot on one side and booted on the other, and stood on the cold grass shouting his children's names into a sky still black.

Across the fence, Mrs. Okafor, his next-door neighbor of fourteen years, was screaming too. Not words. Just one long sound.

When she saw him, she grabbed the top of the fence with both hands. "You heard it," she said. "Tell me you heard it. Ezra. The horn."

"What horn?"

She stared at him. Then she began to cry, and she could not stop.

At the corner, a car had drifted into a lamppost and stopped, its engine still running, its driver's door hanging open. No one was inside. A coffee cup sat in the holder, still steaming.

Then he heard the plane.

It came in low from the south, too low and too fast, its engines making a sound engines should never make. He watched it drop below the treetops toward the river. He felt the impact through the soles of his feet a full second before he heard it.

His hands were shaking so badly he dropped the phone twice. He called his mother. It rang and rang. He called Mia's school, though it was hours before anyone would be there, and got a recording. He called nine-one-one and got a busy signal, which he had not known nine-one-one could give.

Then his thumb found her name.

Kambria. Angela's best friend. The scientist with the notebooks. The woman who had stood on his porch a week ago, crying, begging him to open a book he had shoved in the hall closet. The woman he had told to get off his property.

It rang four times. Then her voice, warm and steady, recorded only days ago:

*"Hi, you've reached Kambria. If you're hearing this and I don't call you back, it already happened. Don't be afraid. It's not too late. Read Joel 2:32. Call on the name of the Lord."*

He hung up. He could still see her face on his porch, wet with tears, as he shut the door on her.

He went back into the kitchen, glass grinding under his one boot. The refrigerator hummed. On its door, under a magnet shaped like a cello, was Mia's last drawing.

Crayon. Their house, with its crooked porch light. Above it, a night sky colored black from edge to edge, and in the middle of all that black, a moon. Huge. Round. Colored red, scrubbed in so hard the paper had pilled.

A blood moon.

She had drawn it a week ago and stuck it there herself, standing on a chair. He had walked past it every day since without once looking.

Ezra Patterson slid down the refrigerator to the kitchen floor and sobbed. Then he screamed it at the ceiling, at the roof, at whatever was above the roof.

"Where are they? Where are my kids? You took her. Now You took them. What do You want from me? Answer me! Answer me!"

He did not know yet that God was listening.

The answer had been sitting in his hall closet for a year, in his dead wife's handwriting.

It had started long before that, at a dinner table.
