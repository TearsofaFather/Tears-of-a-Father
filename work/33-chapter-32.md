## Chapter 32: A Measure of Wheat

> *And I heard a voice in the midst of the four beasts say, A measure of wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny; and see thou hurt not the oil and the wine.* — Revelation 6:6

**Word study: *measure* — χοῖνιξ** Greek *choinix* (Strong's G5518). A dry measure of a little less than a quart. It was what one grown man ate in a day. The penny, a Roman *denarius*, was what one laborer earned in a day. John heard the price of a day's bread set at a day's wages, with nothing left over for a wife or a child, while the oil and the wine, the luxuries of the rich, were not to be touched.

*And the word of the LORD came unto him, saying, Arise, get thee to Zarephath… behold, I have commanded a widow woman there to sustain thee… And she said, As the LORD thy God liveth, I have not a cake, but an handful of meal in a barrel, and a little oil in a cruse: and, behold, I am gathering two sticks, that I may go in and dress it for me and my son, that we may eat it, and die. And Elijah said unto her, Fear not… make me thereof a little cake first… And the barrel of meal wasted not, neither did the cruse of oil fail… (1 Kings 17:8–16)*

\* \* \*

The second famine winter came early to Washington, in the last week of October, with a hard frost that killed the gardens on the rooftops overnight.

Ezra stood on the roof of the row house on Sixth Street on the morning after, in the gray light, and looked at the beds he had built in the spring out of scrap lumber and dirt carried up four flights in buckets. Tomatoes, beans, squash, collards. They were black. All of them. The leaves hung limp and dark and glassy, and when he touched one, it broke.

Below him the city spread out under a low sky, quiet, smokeless, half of it dark. On the corner of Sixth and East Capitol, an Organization screen glowed on its tall black panel in its gold frame, and the face of Adrian Crowe looked out of it at an empty street, smiling, speaking to nobody. In ninety minutes it would be noon, and the face would ask the street to bow.

He went back down the ladder through the hatch into the attic, where Walt was sitting in his chair by the window with his ledger open on his knee and his red pen in his hand.

"All of it?" Walt said, without looking up.

"All of it."

Walt nodded and wrote it down.

*LATE AUTUMN. Hard frost. All rooftop beds lost. Bread $58/loaf at the Seal markets. Rice not available. Wheat by ration only, marked accounts only.*

And in the right margin, as he had written it in some form a hundred times since the first frost, because it was what the days were like: *Revelation 6:6.*

\* \* \*

There were fourteen hundred of them now, in the city alone. Fourteen hundred and some, Nina said, on the night she came home from Edom that autumn, thin and sunburned and older, and laid the stolen list on the long table in the room under the old library and stood back from it with her hands at her sides.

Ezra had read it standing up, the whole thing, every row, with the others crowded behind him in silence. Every safe house. Every false wall he had ever built, with its address and its date. Every name. Walt in his attic. Elijah's bicycle routes, mapped in red. Mrs. Okafor under the old library. The young couple from the next block, who had a baby now. Dolores. Marcus the bus driver. Fourteen hundred people who had trusted him with their lives, and the beast had known where each of them slept since the first hard frost.

And at the bottom, by itself: *Patterson, Ezra. Location: Connecticut. Pending.*

"He let us," Nina had said. Her voice had been flat. "He let us gather them. He said so. He said scattered they were impossible, and we made them a church, and all he had to do was wait." She had looked at Ezra. "I did his work for him. For all this time."

"No," Ezra had said. He had put the list down on the table. "You did God's work. And Vale watched. That's not the same thing."

"He knows where everybody is."

"He's known for years. And everybody's still alive." Ezra had looked around the room, at the faces. "Daniel was in a den of lions all night. The lions knew exactly where he was."

No one had slept that night. They had sat around the long table until morning, Ezra and Michael and Nina and Walt and Elijah and Dorsey, with the list between them, and made a plan, because there was only one plan to make. They had to get out of the city. All of them. Fourteen hundred and twelve people, out of every safe house at once, before Vale's final sweep, and down Michael's routes to the caves and the farms and the Rock in the north.

"You can't move fourteen hundred people in this city without the cameras seeing every one of them," Nina had said. "Not even at night. Not even a few at a time. Not anymore. The new network sees in the dark. It sees heat. It knows a face from a block away, through a car window, at sixty miles an hour. I helped build it." She had pressed her palms to the table. "If even a hundred of us start moving, he'll know, and he'll close every bridge and every road in an hour, and we'll be caught in the open instead of behind walls."

"Then we need a night when nobody's looking," Michael had said.

"There's no such night," Nina had said. She had laughed, and it was not a laugh. "Not anymore. Not in this city."

The name went unspoken, though they had all thought it. "The sixth folder on the card," Nina said. Her voice shook. "I didn't put it there. Somebody who knew exactly what I'd do when I opened it put it there. Somebody who wanted me to find it." She looked at the table. "I think every one of us knows who."

Nobody said the name.

So they had begun to plan anyway, on faith, as Noah had built a boat in a country where it had never rained. Michael and Dorsey laid out the routes on the maps in the radio room: which streets, which bridges, which safe houses on the way north, how many people each truck could carry, how many trucks they had, and what they would need that they did not have. They divided the fourteen hundred into companies of fifty, each with a captain, as Moses had divided Israel in the wilderness on Jethro's advice. They taught the captains the routes by heart, and the code words, and the signal. *When the time comes,* Michael told them, *you will hear it on the radio at nine o'clock. You'll hear the song, and then you'll hear a verse. If the verse is Exodus fourteen thirteen, you go. If it's anything else, you wait.*

*Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD.*

And then they waited. Through September and October, with the list in Vale's hands and the vans going by in the street, they waited for a door that only God could open, and they did not know when He would open it.

And the famine came.

\* \* \*

It was the mark that made it a famine for them, not the weather. The weather was bad, and worse than bad: the summer had been the hottest and driest on record across half the world, and the harvests had failed in Kansas and the Ukraine and the Punjab, and the price of wheat had tripled and tripled again. But the marked still ate, mostly. The Seal markets in the city had bread, at a price. They had rice some weeks and meat once a month. The Organization trucks came in every morning. The ration was thin but it was there, for anyone who would put out his right hand under the scanner. Ezra prayed that night, as he did every night now, by name: for Kambria, who had gone up from a cell with her cuffs still locked, and for Celeste, who had not, whatever she had been. He had promised himself he would.

For the fourteen hundred, there was what Michael's trucks could bring down from the north, and what the rooftop gardens could grow, and what Elijah and the other runners could find at night, and what the marked neighbors who still had some pity left could slip through a back door. In the first famine winter it had been enough, barely. In the second, with the gardens dead and the trucks stopped twice at checkpoints in Maryland and once on the bridge at Havre de Grace, with two of Michael's men taken and not returned, it was not.

By the middle of November, the ration in the safe houses was one cup of dry rice or beans per person per day, and a handful of whatever else there was. By December it was less.

*A measure of wheat for a penny.* A day's bread for a day's wages, with nothing left for anyone else. Walt had written it in red in the margin of the business page of the *Post* in the October before the Rapture, under a story about bread prices, and Kambria had read it upside down at his dinner table while she took his blood pressure. Now it was no longer a distant warning. It was close to what they had for supper.

\* \* \*

Michael chose thirty men, and he chose them like a man choosing the last thirty planks of a boat.

Dorsey, who could walk a checkpoint like a church aisle. Pruitt, who had not been caught in four years. Men who knew a back stair in every ward, who could carry sixty pounds a mile in the dark and not scrape a wall. He gathered them in the library room at midnight, with the city map flat on the long table and the numbered stickers marked in red, and he did not make a speech.

"Eleven crates," he said. "Four hundred pounds. We will not eat it. We will hide it."

A man near the end said, "Hide it where?"

"Everywhere." Michael laid a finger on the map and drew it across the river, the ridge, the park, the rail yards. "Thirty drops. One per man. Under a church floor, in a drained cistern, behind a boiler. Each box is locked to a verse and marked with a color. Green means food and water. Gold, a bed. Red, a road. Blue, a door in the hills." He tapped the ledger. "And every night at nine, Ezra reads the notebook for that color and closes on a verse. To the Peacekeepers, that's a preacher closing on a psalm. To our runners, it's an address: which drop, which color, which lid. The broadcast is the supply line." He looked around the circle. "A runner who hears it and gets scared enough to reach out doesn't knock on an empty house. He finds the box, reads the verse, and the lid opens. This is what's in the ground before he comes."

He did not say that it came out of the dead part of their own winter. Ezra, at the end of the table, did the arithmetic anyway. Four hundred pounds, split thirty ways, was thirteen pounds a box. It would feed nobody for long. It would feed the first nine people who found one, for a week.

"It's not enough," a man said quietly.

"No." Michael did not blink. "It's what's coming, and then more, and then the Lord's hand. It's the first thing in the ground." He closed the map. "A man who prepares isn't afraid. He's obedient. Go."

They went out in pairs and threes, and Ezra went with Michael, because Michael did not let him go alone anymore.

The first drop was a church basement in Hyattsville, a loose stone in the old furnace room behind the coal door. Michael did it himself. He knelt, set the box in the dark, laid a rag over it, and wrote the day's psalm in pencil on the underside of a stair tread. Eleven minutes.

The second was a rail yard. A searchlight swept across the cars before they reached it, and Michael put a hand flat on Ezra's chest, and they stood against a tank car for four minutes in the snow while two Peacekeepers walked the track a yard from their boots, talking about a football game. One of them stopped, ten feet away, to light a cigarette. The flame lit the side of his face. Ezra watched the smoke climb and counted his own heartbeats and heard, in Michael's quiet breathing, a man he had once seen swing a cane at a lock and not let go.

The Peacekeeper flicked the match into the snow. He walked on.

"Count," Michael breathed.

"What?"

"Count to thirty. Then move."

Ezra counted. At thirty they ran.

By four in the morning the radio came alive in short pops, men reporting in a code of numbers and half-verses. *Psalm 46, down.* *Psalm 23, down. Dry.* *Psalm 91, down.* One by one, twenty-nine of them. Michael stood at the console with his father's ledger and a pencil, and made a tick for every voice.

The thirtieth did not come.

It was Pruitt's, in Anacostia, the oldest drop on the map. Michael listened to the empty hiss. He did not say anything for a long time, and Ezra could see him not saying it.

"He'll be late," Ezra said.

"He's never late."

At twenty past four the headset crackled. *Psalm 121. Down.* A pause, and then Pruitt's voice, breathless and cracking. *Sorry, boss. Dog.*

Michael's shoulders came down an inch. He put the pencil down. He made the last tick, and looked at thirty marks in a row, and wrote beside them in his father's square capitals, *Thirty. All in.*

"Hundreds will find the first one," Ezra said. "Then another. By spring..."

"By spring I want a hundred," Michael said. He closed the ledger. "Tell Ezra to open on the green notebook tomorrow and say the psalm slowly. Somebody's waiting for it."

\* \* \*

The quarrel started in a basement in Petworth, behind a wall Ezra had built in the first spring.

He heard about it from Elijah, who came up the attic stairs on Sixth Street at eleven at night in the first week of December with snow on his shoulders and a cut over his eye that he would not explain, and sat down on the floor by Walt's chair, out of breath.

"They're fighting over food, Ez," he said. "In the Petworth room. Two families. One of them had a sack of flour hid behind the boiler. Twenty pounds. They've been eating out of it at night for a month, by themselves, while everybody else is on the cup. The other family found it." He rubbed his face. "Marcus had to pull them apart. There's a man in there says he'll go to the Peacekeepers. Turn the whole room in. Says at least in a processing center they feed you."

Ezra went that night.

It took him two hours to get there on foot through the snow, keeping off the main streets, out of the cameras' sight as well as he could, which was not well. The room in Petworth was in the basement of an old Baptist church that had been closed since the first year, behind a false wall at the back of the boiler room. Thirty-four people lived there. When Ezra came through the hidden door at one in the morning, every one of them was awake, sitting on mattresses and folding chairs in the light of two battery lanterns, in two groups on either side of the room, not looking at each other. In the middle of the floor sat a sack of flour, slit open, with a little white spill beside it.

He did not shout. In all that time since the sky opened he had raised his voice at no one.

He sat down on the floor beside the sack. He took Angela's Bible out of his coat, the soft black one with the cracked spine, and opened it to the second chapter of Acts, and he read it to them aloud in the lantern light, as he had read it at his own dinner table the first winter.

*And all that believed were together, and had all things common; and sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need.*

Then he turned to the sixth chapter.

*And in those days, when the number of the disciples was multiplied, there arose a murmuring of the Grecians against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily ministration.*

He looked up at them.

"The first church fought over food too," he said. "Did you know that? The very first one. In Jerusalem, a few years after Jesus went up. The widows on one side were getting fed, and the widows on the other side weren't, and somebody noticed, and it got ugly." He set the Bible down on his knee. "So the apostles didn't take sides. They didn't figure out who was guilty. They called everybody together and said, pick seven men, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, and put them in charge of the food. And the food got shared out fair. And the church grew."

On one side of the room, the father of the family with the flour, a big gray-bearded man named Leon who had driven a city bus for thirty years, was staring at the floor with his jaw clenched.

"Leon," Ezra said. "Why'd you hide it?"

Leon kept his eyes down. When he finally looked up, his eyes were red.

"My grandbaby," he said. "She's three. Born after. She was down to nothing, Ezra. Bones. She wasn't crying anymore. A baby stops crying, you know what that means." His voice cracked. "I found that sack in a bakery on Georgia Avenue that got hit. I should've brought it here. I know it. I knew it then. I just looked at her and I couldn't."

The room was quiet.

On the other side, a woman named Patrice, whose husband had been taken in a van the spring before last and who was raising two small children, born in the years since the sky, alone on a mattress by the boiler, said, "My babies are bones too, Leon."

"I know they are," Leon said. "I know." He put his face in his hands.

Ezra waited. Then he stood up and picked up the sack of flour, and set it on the folding table by the wall, where the room kept its common food.

"Here's what we're going to do," he said. "Leon's going to say he's sorry, to all of you. And you're going to forgive him, because he's a grandfather and he was scared, and every one of us in this room would've been tempted to do the same thing, and you know it." He looked around at them. "Then you're going to pick seven people. Tonight. Full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, like it says. Not the loudest. The fairest. And those seven are going to keep the food from now on, and share it out every morning, and the children and the widows eat first. Every day. That's the law of Christ. Bear ye one another's burdens."

No one stirred.

Then Patrice got up from her mattress and crossed the room, and stood in front of Leon, and put her hand on his gray head.

"I forgive you," she said. "Your baby can have some of my boys' rice tomorrow, if she needs it."

Leon broke down entirely.

They picked seven before dawn. Patrice was one of them. So was Leon, at Patrice's insistence. The man who had threatened to go to the Peacekeepers went up the stairs at four in the morning and out the back of the church into the snow, and no one stopped him or knew whether he would come back with a van. He did not come back at all. A week later Elijah heard that he had taken the mark at a relief center on Kennedy Street and was eating at a Seal market, and had said nothing to anyone about a church basement in Petworth.

Ezra walked home in the dark with the snow coming down, and his legs were shaking under him, and he did not know if it was the cold or the hunger or the hour.

\* \* \*

Three nights later, with the snow still coming and the ration down to a cup of rice, Elijah produced a deck of cards from inside his coat as if he were smuggling a pistol.

"Spades," he said. "Me and Ez against you and Dorsey. Loser washes the pots."

"There's no water to wash them in," Dorsey said.

"Then loser licks them."

They played at the long table in the room under the old library, by one lantern, with a coffee can for the books and a pencil stub for the score. Elijah talked through every hand. He called his bids like a man selling something, slapped down his cards hard enough to make the lantern jump, and bid blind nil twice on pure nerve and made it once. Dorsey played in total silence and caught every card that had been played. Michael played the way he did everything, with his face closed and his eyes moving, and when he cut Elijah's ace with a trump, he said "Mm" in the voice of a man who had been waiting all night to say it. It was the first time in weeks that anyone in that room had laughed with their whole chest.

By midnight Dorsey had gone to check the transmitter and Elijah had fallen asleep face down on the table with a fan of cards under his cheek, and Michael dealt Ezra a hand of solitaire that neither of them looked at.

"You never did say what you were running from," Michael said. "When you first sat in that church of yours. A man doesn't build false walls for fun."

"I wasn't running. I was hiding. It's different." Ezra turned over a card. "I got good at it when I was a kid."

He told it plainly, the way he had never once told it aloud to anyone. "My father could quote scripture for an hour without looking down," Ezra said. "Friday nights, that house. There was a pistol in a shoebox. I was eight. I put it away myself, both arms around it, under the dish towels." He turned a card. "Long sleeves in July. I ate lunch on the back stairwell at school, on the cold step, by myself, so nobody'd ask. I got real good at not needing anybody. Real good at calling on no one."

He told about the night Elijah, ten years old on a phone book, stole his own father's black Barracuda and came and found him on the front step with no coat. "Drove me to a creek, and we fished for catfish till dark, and on the way home he put us into a tree at the bottom of the overpass," Ezra said. "Went through the windshield head first. Lived. We both lived." He looked at the sleeping man. "Neither of us ever worked out why. So we stayed."

"*When my father and my mother forsake me,*" Michael said slowly, "*then the LORD will take me up.*" He had to clear his throat. "My pastor read that. I didn't hear it."

"I didn't hear it either, not till I was forty-four." (Psalm 27:10) Ezra turned a card. "I loved the world, Michael. The light, the wood, the sound of a good room. I just couldn't stand the house it was in. So I stayed out of sight in all of it. The day I came out, I'd decided I was done being that boy."

Michael shuffled the deck once, slowly. Then he told his.

"Neighborhood where nothing was ever enough," Michael said. "You learned early the one thing you could count on owning was what you could defend. I was quick to anger and quicker with my hands, and half of it wasn't even mine. It was the whole block's, handed down like a coat." He turned the deck over. "Football gave it a place. Friday nights I hit people till it was spent, and the coaches called it heart. It never stayed spent. Then Goju. There it had rules, and a referee, and a name for what I was doing. Semifinal, I took a man who'd already quit fighting back and beat him past anything the sport could forgive. League put me out for good. I stood in that parking lot with my knuckles split open, and I knew nothing in me had been won."

"The Army, the badge, the pew in the back with my arms crossed," he said. "All of it was a uniform over the thing I couldn't put down." He quoted it low. "*He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city.* (Proverbs 16:32) I'd taken cities, Ezra. I never ruled a thing." He laid the deck on the table. "Then it all went, and I had nothing left to hit. No wife to hide it from. No boy to look up at me. Just me, standing in an empty house, and two roads. Be what I was, or be something else, and I didn't know how."

"Paul had blood on his hands too," Ezra said. "He held the coats while they killed Stephen." (Acts 7:58)

"I know the one." Michael almost smiled. "*Forgetting those things which are behind… I press toward the mark.* (Philippians 3:13–14) He didn't say the past didn't happen. He said it didn't get to decide."

Michael turned a card he did not play. "First night I heard you on the radio, I nearly shut it off," he said. "Then the cello came on." He watched the lantern. "My father built me a mixing board when I was nine. Out of parts, at the bench where he labeled the water drums in his own capitals and checked every one on the first of the month. The block called him crazy. He counted everything, and he never raised his voice, and I got the counting from him and the temper from somewhere else. When a frightened family asks me what we do if the water stops, I have an answer, because he asked first. He said a boy ought to have a way to make something that wasn't noise. I came up in that basement cutting beats with the whole block banging on the wall, and when I had a boy of my own, he had his hands on that board by five." He was quiet a while. "Nobody had played a real instrument in my house for a long time. And here was some old man's son on a radio in a cave, playing something that sounded like a man trying to say a thing he couldn't."

He kept a few of his own discs in the truck, and on the bad nights he put one on low in the radio room. Down there, nobody was afraid on those nights, and the people who had come in shaking stopped.

Elijah snored, once. Neither of them looked up. Outside, under the library floor, the snow went on falling on a city that had forgotten both of them, and two men who had each been a hard boy sat on either side of a lantern and found that the thing they had carried alone for thirty years weighed less with two people holding the other end of it.

*Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.* (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10) Ezra thought of that verse when he looked at the sleeping man, who had spent his whole life afraid of being alone, and who had been, for thirty years, the one standing beside him. He did not wake him. He let him sleep.

In the morning Elijah woke with the seven of clubs stuck to his face and demanded a recount. He had lost by two books. Nobody washed the pots.

\* \* \*

The raid came eleven days later, and it did not come to Petworth.

It came to a row house in Trinidad, in Northeast, at three in the morning, behind a false wall in an attic, where twenty-two people were sleeping. It was not Vale's final sweep. Michael was sure of that afterward. It was a local thing, a Peacekeeper unit working its own list, chasing a tip from a neighbor who wanted a bonus on her account. They came in through the front door with a ram and up the stairs with lights and dogs, and they found the false wall because the dogs found it, and they broke it down.

Eleven people came out of that house alive. They were taken in a van to the processing center in Maryland. One of them, a girl of sixteen named Keisha, came back in March with the mark on her forehead and would not speak to anyone.

Eleven did not come out.

Ezra was there before dawn. Michael had tried to stop him. He went anyway, on foot, and stood across the street in the falling snow behind a parked car with no windows, and watched the Peacekeepers carry them out of the house one at a time under sheets and lay them on the sidewalk in a row, in the snow, while the neighbors watched from their windows and did nothing. He knew them all. He had built that wall. He had sat in that attic in September and read them the twelfth chapter of Revelation by candlelight, *and they loved not their lives unto the death*, and an old man named Clarence had wept and held his hand.

Clarence was the fourth sheet.

*They shall fall by the sword, and by flame, by captivity, and by spoil, many days.* It had been in Kambria's notebook. Daniel eleven. She had dated it the autumn before the sky opened.

He held it in on the street, where the cameras were. He stood behind the car until the van pulled away and the Peacekeepers had gone and the sheets were lifted into a city truck and taken, and he did not know where. Then he walked back to Sixth Street, two hours in the snow, and climbed the attic stairs, and sat down on the floor at Walt's feet with his back against the old man's chair, and wept until he could not see.

Walt said nothing. He put a hand on Ezra's head and kept it there.

\* \* \*

The young men wanted blood.

Dorsey told Michael first, and Michael told Ezra. There were nine of them, from three different safe houses, young men in their twenties who had lost fathers and brothers in Trinidad, and one of them had a cousin who had been a Marine, and the cousin knew where the Peacekeeper unit slept, in a converted school on Benning Road. They had rifles. They had a plan. They were going to go in on Christmas Eve and make the men who had carried the sheets out into the snow pay for it.

Michael called them to the room under the old library on the twenty-second of December, all nine, and sat them down at the long table, and did not let Ezra speak first.

He stood at the head of the table in his armor, with the cross hanging outside his vest, and looked them over in silence. They were angry, and they were frightened, and they were hungry, and every one of them looked at him with the same thing in his face: *You of all people. You were a soldier. You know.*

"I know," Michael said, answering what none of them had said. "I know exactly what you want to do, and I know exactly how to do it, and I could do it better than you. I could go in that school tonight with Dorsey and Pruitt and come out before they knew we'd been there, and every man who carried a body out of that house would be dead in his bed by morning." He let that sit. "And then what?"

None of them answered.

"Then they come for the rest of us," Michael said. "All fourteen hundred. Tomorrow. Not with one unit. With all of them. Vale's been looking for a reason to stop waiting, and you'd hand it to him." He leaned on the table with both fists. "But that's not why. That's the smart reason. I don't care about the smart reason. Here's the real one."

He took a small battered Bible out of a pouch on his vest, the one Ezra had given him in the cave that first winter, and opened it without looking, because he knew where it was.

"*Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.*" He closed it. "Romans twelve. Vengeance is His. Not mine. Not yours. His. And He will repay. Every sheet on that sidewalk is written down in His book, and every man who carried one, and He doesn't forget anything. He's going to come with a sword out of His mouth and judge every one of them, and every one of us, and He doesn't need nine hungry boys with rifles to help Him."

One of the young men, the one with the Marine cousin, a lanky kid named Andre whose father had been the second sheet, said hoarsely, "So we just let them."

"No," Michael said. "We don't let them. We get our people out. We keep them alive. We keep them fed. We keep them reading, and singing, and praying. We hold the line God gave us, and we don't step over it." He looked at Andre, and his hard face changed. "Son. I had a boy. Twelve years old. He's with the Lord now. If he was looking down at me tonight, I'd want him to see his daddy keeping the line. Not crossing it."

Andre's face crumpled.

Michael came around the table and put his arms around him, armor and all, and held him while he cried. Then the next one. Then the next.

None of them went to Benning Road.

Later, walking up the hidden stairs with Ezra, Michael said, low, "I wanted to, you know. Go myself. I lay awake two nights planning it."

"I know," Ezra said.

"*Be ye angry, and sin not.*" Michael shook his head. "Hardest verse in the whole book."

\* \* \*

Walt stopped eating sometime in January.

Ezra did not see it. That was what he would never forgive himself for, though he knew he was forgiven. He was moving from house to house almost every night by then, through the snow, with Michael and Dorsey, reading, praying, settling quarrels, carrying the dying to whatever doctor they had, teaching the captains the routes again. He came back to the attic on Sixth Street every third or fourth night to sleep a few hours on the floor, and Walt was always in his chair by the window with his ledger, and his thermos of whatever passed for coffee now, and his red pen, writing.

He looked thinner. Everyone looked thinner. Ezra had a new hole punched in his own belt every month. He gave it no thought.

It was the schoolteacher who owned the house, Mrs. Fairley, who told him, after.

"He gave it away," she said, standing in the attic doorway with her hands knotted in her apron. "Every day. His cup. He'd take it from the seven in the morning when they shared it out, and then he'd come back up here, and the children from the second floor would come up the stairs in the afternoons, the Bakers' two and the little Harmon girl, to hear him read, and he'd give it to them. Little by little. A spoonful each. He'd say he wasn't hungry. He'd say old men don't need much." Her voice broke. "I saw him do it. I thought he was eating something else. I thought somebody was bringing him something. I didn't ask. God forgive me, I didn't ask."

Ezra found him on the morning of the eleventh of February.

He came up the attic stairs at dawn, after a night in Anacostia, with snow on his coat, and the attic was gray and bitter cold, and Walt was in his chair by the window.

He was sitting upright, with his cardigan buttoned wrong, as it always was, and his slippers on, and his reading glasses down on the end of his nose. His big-print Bible was open on his knee. His hands were folded on top of it. His red pen had rolled off the arm of the chair and lay on the floor by his foot. His face was turned a little toward the window, toward the gray light coming over the rooftops of Capitol Hill, toward the east, and his eyes were closed, and his face was the most peaceful Ezra had ever seen it.

He was cold. He had been gone since sometime in the night.

Ezra knelt down on the floor in front of the chair.

He did not cry at first. He knelt and looked at the old man's face. Then he looked at the Bible on his knee. It was open to Revelation, the second chapter, and Walt had marked a verse with his red pen, as he had marked ten thousand verses in his life, with a neat red line beneath it.

*Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life.*

Beside the chair, on the little table, the green ledger was open. The twelfth one. Ezra picked it up.

The last entry was dated the day before. The handwriting was shakier than it had been, but it was still Walt's, square and careful and exact.

*DEEP WINTER. Bread $71. No trucks 9 days. Snow. Bakers' boy better. Harmon girl better.*

And in the right margin, where the verse went, not a prophecy, for the first time in twelve green ledgers:

*1 Kings 17:16. The barrel of meal wasted not.*

And beneath that, smaller, in the same red ink, the last thing he ever wrote:

*Lord, I knew all about You for seventy-four years. Thank You for the four I knew You.*

Ezra sat down on the cold attic floor with the ledger in his lap and his back against Walt's chair, and wept as he had wept on the morning after Trinidad, when the old man's hand had rested on his head. There was no hand now.

*And I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him.* Walt had written that in a margin, and the pale horse had passed through the city all winter, and the old man had walked in front of it with his bread in his hands. Death had taken the body. It had not been given the rest.

Then he got up, and went downstairs, and told the others.

\* \* \*

They could not give him a funeral. There was no cemetery that would take an unmarked man, and no way to carry a body through the streets without the cameras seeing. So they buried him that night, in the frozen backyard of Mrs. Fairley's house, behind the shed, under the old dogwood tree, in a grave Ezra and Michael and Elijah dug with picks and a spade by the light of a single lantern, taking turns, sweating in the cold.

Ezra built the box himself, in the basement, out of pine boards from a closet he tore out. He built it as he had built the studio for his son, with square corners and every joint tight, because it was the last thing he could make for the old man, and he would not let it be anything but right. On the inside of the lid, before he closed it, he wrote in black marker, in his square carpenter's capitals:

*WALTER J. SIMMONS. TYPESETTER. WATCHMAN. FAITHFUL UNTO DEATH.*

And under it: *REVELATION 2:10.*

Thirty people stood in the yard in the snow that night, from the safe houses nearest, as many as Michael would let come. They stood around the grave with their heads bare and their breath smoking in the lantern light, and Ezra read. He read the twentieth chapter of Revelation, the fourth verse, the one Angela had boxed at the back of her notebook. *And they lived and reigned with Christ a thousand years.* He read the fifteenth chapter of First Corinthians, *O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?* He read Job, because Walt would have wanted Job. *I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee.*

Then they sang the hundred and sixteenth psalm, low, so that the sound would not carry past the fence, thirty hungry people in the snow around an open grave, and Ezra could not get through the second verse, and Elijah carried it for him, in a cracked voice, with tears running into his beard.

When it was done, the Harmon girl, who was three, born in the years after the sky went quiet, came forward out of the crowd in a coat three sizes too big for her and dropped something into the grave on top of the pine box. It was a red pen.

"So he can keep writing," she said. "In heaven."

After that there was nothing to say.

They filled the grave in. Ezra set a flat stone at the head of it, from the garden wall. And when the others had gone back inside, he stood alone in the yard in the snow until his feet went numb, with the lantern at his feet, and he understood what he would carry to the end.

The old man had done it. He had been faithful unto death. He had not taken the mark or bowed at noon or stolen a crumb. He had given his own bread to three children, a spoonful at a time, and kept the record to the last day, and died in his chair with his Bible on his knee and his face to the east, and not one person in the beast's whole kingdom had been able to make him do anything else.

It could be done. Walt had shown them. It could be done.

*Be thou faithful unto death.*

\* \* \*

Some nights later, in the room under the old library on Kennedy Street, at nine o'clock, Ezra was sitting at the long table with the microphone in his hand and the low line of his son's cello playing out of the console radio behind him, getting ready to read. The others were around him, as always. Nina at the transmitter. Elijah in the corner, thinner than he had ever been, with a bruise yellowing on his jaw from the night of the raid that he still would not explain. Michael at the bottom of the stairs, by the door, with his rifle across his knees.

Nina faded the song down under his voice. Ezra lifted the microphone.

Before he could speak, the receiver on the shelf crackled.

Not static: a voice, on their own frequency, on the dead channel near the bottom of the dial, cutting in over the cello, close and clear, as if the speaker were in the next room.

"Ezra. Ezra Patterson. Don't say anything. Just listen."

Everyone in the room went still. Michael was on his feet with the rifle up before the second word.

Ezra knew the voice. He had not heard it in years, not since his own driveway, joking about a camera contract, the October before the world ended.

"It's Trent," the voice said. "Trent Walker. Your old apprentice." A pause. A breath. "You got my list. I know you got it. I'm sorry I couldn't give it to you any better than that."

Nina had both hands over her mouth.

"Listen to me," Trent said. "Vale's sweep starts on the twelfth of March. All of it. All of them, every row on that list, in one night. He's been waiting for the mark deadline to pass, and it passes at midnight on the eleventh." His voice was steady, but it was very tired. "I can't stop it. I can't switch anything off, and I won't pretend I can. Every camera in this city sees in the dark, and I'm the one who taught them how. But on the night of the eleventh, every Peacekeeper in the District who hasn't taken the Seal yet has to stand in a line indoors and take it before midnight. They pulled half the night patrols off the streets for it. I know, because the rosters come through my office. The streets will be thinner that night than they've been in four years. Not empty. Thinner."

The room kept its peace.

"Tell Michael I know about the companies of fifty. I know about Exodus fourteen thirteen. I know everything, man. I've known everything for years. That's my job." A sound that might have been a laugh. "Mama would've liked you, Ezra. You know that? She'd have liked what you did with my list." A pause. "Have the trucks waiting outside the city, past the river, where the routes start. Not inside it. I know every alley and rail cut and culvert in this town, because I spent four years learning where people go when they don't want to be seen. I'll walk your people out to the trucks myself. On foot. I'll be at the corner of Twelfth and Rhode Island at ten o'clock on the eleventh, under the first camera I ever put up. If I'm not there by ten-thirty, I didn't make it, and you go anyway." His voice softened. "When I was little and the lights got cut off, Mama used to say that when you can't see the way, you put your shoes on and wait by the door for God. So put your shoes on."

The voice dropped.

"I gotta go. Don't answer. Don't ever talk back on this frequency after tonight." And then, very quietly, almost too low to hear, the last thing before the static came back: "Pray for me. I don't know how to come out yet. But I'm trying."
