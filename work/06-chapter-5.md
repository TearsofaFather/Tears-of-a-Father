## Chapter 5: Red Sky

> *The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come.* — Joel 2:31

**Word study: *terrible* — יָרֵא** Hebrew *yare'* (Strong's H3372). To fear, to be afraid, to stand in awe. The day of the LORD is called terrible because it will make the whole earth afraid. It is the same word used for the fear of the LORD, which is the beginning of wisdom. One fear comes too late. The other saves.

*…Stretch out thine hand toward heaven, that there may be darkness over the land of Egypt, even darkness which may be felt. And Moses stretched forth his hand toward heaven; and there was a thick darkness in all the land of Egypt three days: They saw not one another, neither rose any from his place for three days: but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.* (Exodus 10:21-23)

\* \* \*

After the funeral, the house went quiet in a way Ezra had never heard before.

It wasn't the quiet of an empty house. The kids were there. Mia, five years old now, padded through the rooms in her socks, murmuring to her teddy bear. Jalen, seventeen, came and went from the studio at the back of the yard at all hours. On Saturday mornings a gray Buick pulled up at the curb, and Jalen carried his cello out to it and stayed gone until dark. Lionel Hart, his teacher, had been doing that since Angela first got sick. Ezra watched them go from the couch. He was glad someone was looking after his son, and he was ashamed that it wasn't him. Ezra's mother, Loretta, drove over every afternoon to fold laundry and fill the freezer. But underneath all of it lay a silence that filled the whole house. It was the silence of Angela not humming in the kitchen. Angela not calling up the stairs. Angela not laughing at her phone in the next room.

Ezra couldn't sleep in their bed. The first night after the funeral he lay awake until dawn on his side of the mattress with his back to hers, unable to turn over and see it empty. After that he slept on the couch in the living room, with the television on low for the noise. He told the kids it was because of his back. Nobody believed him.

The noise was the news, most nights, because he never bothered to change the channel. He lay there in the blue light with his eyes open and let it run over him. A ceasefire in Ukraine that lasted two days. The Organization for World Peace meeting in Geneva, and a thin, dark-haired man in a gray suit standing behind the chairman's chair, saying nothing. The new flu out of Asia, in Seattle now and Los Angeles, and hospitals in both cities putting up white tents in their parking lots. Phoenix rationing water: no lawns, no car washes, showers on a schedule. Farmers in California leaving whole fields unplanted because there was nothing left to water them with. Ezra watched all of it and felt nothing. None of it had anything to do with him. Angela would have said all of it did.

Every morning, she died again.

It happened in the first second after he woke. Before he opened his eyes his hand went across the cushion for her, the way it had gone across their bed every morning for twelve years, to find her hip, her shoulder, the warm small of her back. It found the back of the couch. And then it came, all of it, the hospice bed, the dish towel, the grave, falling on him in the gray light as if no one had told him yet. Every morning the news was new. Every morning he lost her at six-fifteen and had to get up and pour cereal for her children.

He kept buying her yogurt. The peach one, the only one she ate. He came home from the store with it three weeks in a row before he opened the refrigerator and saw the shelf, twelve cups, lined up and waiting, and closed the door and sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against it.

He could not say her name. He tried once, alone in the truck at a red light on Rhode Island Avenue, just to hear it. *Angie.* It came out of him as something else, a sound like a dog hit in the road, and the man in the next lane looked over and then looked away fast. Ezra drove the rest of the way home with his hand over his mouth.

Her phone lay on the kitchen counter where the hospice nurse had left it, plugged into the wall, because nobody could stand to turn it off. On the ninth day it lit up and buzzed against the laminate.

*Jalen: can u get me at 4 today, mr h cant*

Sent out of habit, from the high school parking lot, by a boy who had texted that number every school day for nine years. Twenty minutes later: *nvm.* Ezra picked him up at four anyway. Neither of them mentioned the phone.

But Ezra had his own habit, and it was worse. At two in the morning, on the couch, with the bottle on the floor beside him, he typed to her. *Where are you.* *Come home.* *I prayed. At the end I prayed. Did you hear me.* *The kids need you. I can't do her hair.* *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.* He pressed send. And from the dark kitchen, ten feet away, her phone lit up the ceiling and buzzed against the counter, once for every message, and he lay there and listened to himself arriving in a room where nobody was.

The week before Christmas it went down to nine degrees, and at eleven o'clock at night he got in the truck and drove to the church.

The cemetery gate was chained. He climbed it. The ground over her was still a raw mound, frozen hard, the flowers on it gone black and stiff. He stood over it a long time in the wind. Then he took off his coat, his heavy canvas work coat, and knelt and spread it over the dirt, over her, tucking the edges down into the frozen clay with his bare hands.

*It's cold down there. Mommy doesn't have her blanket.*

He lay down beside the mound on the frozen grass in his shirtsleeves with one arm over the coat and his cheek against the ground, and he talked to her. He did not know afterward what he said. He talked until his teeth were knocking so hard he couldn't make words, and then he just lay there and shook. A patrol car's spotlight found him a little after one. The officer stood at the fence with the light on him and didn't come in. After a while he turned the light off and sat in his car with the engine running until Ezra got up and climbed back over the gate, without the coat.

He left it there. He never went back for it.

He lost twenty-two pounds before New Year's. His belt went in two notches, then three. On a job in Takoma Park in January he looked up from the table saw because a woman walked past the window in a straw hat, in January, and the blade took the edge off his glove and a quarter-inch of his thumbnail, and he stood there bleeding on the plywood and could not remember, for a full minute, where he was or why it mattered.

One evening in the first week of December, he came in from the garage and found Mia alone in the front hall, standing by the door in the last ashen light from the window beside it.

She was wearing her mother's shoes. They were Angela's black Sunday shoes, with the low square heels, and Mia's small feet were lost inside them. She stood on the old pine floorboards with her head bowed, the front of her dress bunched in both fists against her shoulder, studying her feet with great seriousness.

"Baby," he said. "What are you doing?"

She kept her eyes down. "Practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Walking where Mommy walked." She took a step in the big shoes, and then another, and the heels knocked on the boards. "They're too big right now," she told him. "But Mommy said I'll grow into them."

Ezra had to hold on to the door frame. He didn't trust himself to speak, so he didn't. He stood and watched his daughter walk the length of the hall in her mother's shoes, one slow knock at a time, with her head bowed the whole way, and he thought he had never seen anything so small look so certain of where it was going.

On the wall beside her, next to the front door, hung the small framed drawing from the night of one of their anniversaries. He had taken Angela to a showing at a gallery downtown, the kind of place with a man at the door holding a tray of champagne. They had both worn leather jackets, hers the brown one she'd owned since college and his the black one he never wore, and they had gone from wall to wall with their hands in each other's back pockets, laughing at the prices. Then she had stopped in front of this one and gone quiet. A little girl in a woman's shoes, in pencil and gray wash: the feet lost inside them, the head bowed, both fists gathered in the front of her dress. He had paid for it before they left, and they had eaten by candlelight afterward, at a table too small for the two of them. The next weekend she stood in this hall with her arms folded and told him to make sure it was level. He hung it, and she tipped her head and held up one hand and tilted her fingers, left, then right, as if it were crooked every time he took his hand away. "Higher. No, lower. Is that level to you?" He laughed, and she laughed, and he finally put both hands on the frame and held it there until she came and stood behind him and rested her chin on his shoulder. He had walked past it every day since without once really looking. He looked now. The girl on the paper and the girl in the hall stood exactly the same way, and in the bottom corner, in small careful letters, the artist had signed his name: *Michael Banks.*

He went back to work in January because he didn't know what else to do.

Work, at least, made sense. A board was straight or it wasn't. A joint fit or it didn't. He took every job his foreman offered: a kitchen remodel in Takoma Park, a back deck in Silver Spring, custom bookshelves for a professor near the university. He worked until dusk and drove home with sawdust in his hair, and some nights the ache in his hands was the only thing he could feel.

Everything else had gone numb.

He noticed it in small ways. Elijah, his oldest friend, told a joke at Rooney's that would have had him laughing so hard he couldn't breathe a year ago, and Ezra smiled politely and stared at his beer. Mia brought home a drawing of a horse from kindergarten, and he said "That's nice, baby" without looking up from his phone, and then saw her face fall, and hated himself, and still felt nothing but tired.

Kambria came by every Sunday after church with a casserole and a stack of mail she'd pulled from the overflowing box at the curb. She was gentle with him and never pushed. But every week, before she left, she stood in the kitchen doorway and said the same thing.

"Pastor Grant asked about you again. We'd love to see you there, Ezra. The kids would too."

And every week he said, "Maybe next Sunday," and they both knew he meant never.

On the counter by the coffee maker, under a pile of unopened sympathy cards, there was one card he had opened. It was cream-colored, with a pressed lavender flower glued to the front. Inside, in looping handwriting, it said: *Thinking of you and holding space for your grief. You don't have to carry this alone. — Celeste.* And beneath that, a phone number.

He hadn't called it. But he hadn't thrown it away, either.

\* \* \*

She had touched a great many people, and every one of them had to go on living in a world with a hole in it the exact shape of her.

Jalen did not play for thirty-one days. The studio windows stayed dark. He went to school and came home and took his plate out the back door and shut himself in with the cello in its case, and no sound came out. Mr. Hart's gray Buick came on the first Saturday, and the old man sat at the curb with the engine running for half an hour, and Jalen did not come out, and the Buick drove away. It came the next Saturday too. And the next. Jalen's grades came in the mail in January: two Ds and an F in a class he had never once gotten less than an A in. Ezra put the envelope in the drawer with the takeout menus and didn't open it again.

Mia stopped sleeping through the night. She had been dry since she was three. Now Ezra woke on the couch at two in the morning to her standing beside him in a wet nightgown, not crying, just standing there, holding her teddy bear by one ear, waiting to be told what to do. Her kindergarten teacher called in the second week of December. Mia had told the whole class at circle time that her mother was "at the big supper," and that she was "saving Mia a seat," and then she had gone to the corner by the cubbies and pulled her coat over her head and would not come out until pickup. "I just thought you should know," the teacher said, and her voice wasn't steady either.

Every drawing Mia brought home had her mother in it. Angela at the stove. Angela at the swings. Angela in a yellow dress with a crown. And in every one of them, Mia had drawn the hair and the dress and the arms, and left the face blank. When Ezra finally asked her why, she said, "Because I can't remember which way she was smiling," and went back to her crayons.

Kambria still drove to Twelfth Street every Thursday at a quarter to seven. She parked across from the brick house with the crooked porch light and sat in the car with the four colored notebooks on the passenger seat and her Bible in her lap and the engine running. The kitchen window stayed dark. She never went to the door. At seven-thirty she drove home. At the lab she relabeled the same rack of samples three times in one afternoon, because her hands kept writing the wrong date, and on a Tuesday in January she went into the walk-in cold room for a box of reagent and stood among the frozen shelves until her fingers went white, because it was the only room in the building where nobody could hear her.

She tried to pray and found she couldn't. For the first time in her life the words came up empty. *Thursday is still Thursday,* Angela had said once, when the study shrank to two. Now Thursday was a car in the dark with the engine running, and Kambria did not know what it was anymore.

In the third-grade classroom four blocks away, nobody moved Mrs. Patterson's desk. The substitute taught from a folding table by the window. The children had taped their cards to the empty chair, so many that it looked upholstered in construction paper. The boy who had waved at the casket kept his card in his backpack. He told the substitute he was saving it for when she came back, and the substitute did not know what to say, so she said nothing.

Elijah stopped telling jokes at Rooney's. He came in at four and sat on the stool nearest the door and drank until close, and when Sal asked him if he was all right, he said, "She prayed for me, Sal. Laying there dying, she held my hands and she prayed for *me*," and put his face down on the bar.

Across the street, Walt Simmons stood at his front window every night at nine and watched the dark house. He had pretended for twelve years not to like any of them. Twice in December he crossed the street with a plate covered in foil and stood on the porch with it, and twice he walked back across without knocking and ate it himself, standing up, at his kitchen counter.

Up in Erie, Pat stopped baking. The church kitchen ran its Christmas bake sale without her for the first time in thirty-one years. A box came in the mail in January with no letter in it, only a sleeve of chocolate cupcake liners and one birthday candle, and Ezra stood in the hallway holding it and understood exactly what it meant and could not bear it. Joe called every Sunday at four o'clock. He did not say much. Sometimes he did not say anything at all. Ezra would answer, and there would be breathing on the line, and then a man's voice clearing itself and saying, "Well," and then, "Kiss those kids for me," and then the line would go dead.

And Ezra lay on the couch in the blue light of the news and was the center of all of it, the place where every one of those losses ran together and pooled, and he did not move.

\* \* \*

Some mornings, he couldn't get up at all. It came without warning, usually on weekends, when there was no job to drive to. A stone lay on his chest, and his body would not obey. He lay on the couch and watched the light crawl across the ceiling and listened to the kids moving around the house without him.

*Since you left, Angie, I've been traveling somewhere I've never been. It's a shadowed country, and the roads all go down. There are signs along the way, and I've read them all. Grief. Then anger. Then a long road called Nothing, where I can't feel anything at all. I keep walking, looking for you. This isn't where you went.*

On those mornings only the nine seconds could get him up. He watched her laugh until he could breathe, and then he put his feet on the floor for the kids, because she had told him to stop worrying, and because she had said *God's got me*, and for nine seconds at a time he could almost believe it. The small handprint stayed low on the television all winter. Nobody cleaned it.

\* \* \*

On the first Saturday in March, Mia made dinner.

She had been planning it all week with her grandmother. It was boxed macaroni and cheese and hot dogs cut into coins, with a bowl of grapes in the middle of the table, and she had set three places with real napkins, folded into triangles as Angela used to fold them. Jalen came in from the studio and sat down without a word. Ezra sat at the head of the table, in front of a plate he had no appetite for, and felt his stomach tighten at the sight of the folded napkins.

Then Mia bowed her head, and reached out both her hands.

She did it exactly as her mother used to. Her right hand went to Jalen, and he took it. Her left hand lay open on the table, palm up, reaching toward her father.

Ezra stared at it.

Twelve years he had left that hand lying open on the table. He thought of the hospice blanket, and how long it had taken him to finally reach, and how short a time he'd had to hold on.

He reached out and took his daughter's small hand in his.

Mia squeezed. Across the table, Jalen looked up at his father, surprised, and a look crossed Jalen's face that Ezra couldn't read, one that might have been hope.

"Dear Jesus," Mia said in her clear, careful voice. "Thank You for the macaroni. Thank You for Daddy and Jalen and Grandma. Please tell Mommy we miss her. And please help Daddy not be sad. Amen."

"Amen," said Jalen.

Ezra could not say it. His throat had closed. But he held on to Mia's hand long after the prayer was over, until she giggled and said, "Daddy, I need it to eat," and he let her go.

Afterward, while she carried her plate to the sink, he stood at the refrigerator with the dish towel in his hand. The calendar there was in Jalen's handwriting, and one square was circled twice in red marker: *Thursday, April 16. RECITAL. 7 PM.* "You're coming, right, Daddy?" Mia said from the sink, not turning around. "Jalen said you're coming."

"I'll be there," Ezra said.

She held up her little finger over her shoulder without looking, and he hooked his own around it.

\* \* \*

That night, he fell asleep on the couch earlier than usual.

The house had gone quiet for the night. The kids were in bed. Out in the studio, Jalen had been playing late again; he played late most nights now, and the last thing Ezra heard before he drifted off was the melody: the slow, grieving one, low on the cello's deepest string. It had changed since Angela died. It went lower now, darker, before it began to climb. And it climbed higher than it ever had, searching, reaching, until it held one long, trembling note at the very top.

Then it stopped. It still didn't have an ending.

Ezra slept.

When he opened his eyes, the room was red.

Not dim. Not dark. *Red.* A deep, glowing crimson light filled the living room from wall to wall, pouring through the big front window and lying across the carpet and the couch and his own hands. It colored everything: the lamp, the television, the darker rectangle on the carpet where the hospital bed had stood.

And it was silent. Completely silent. No furnace. No traffic. No cello.

Ezra eased himself upright. His heart knocked in his throat. He got up off the couch, and walked to the front window, and looked out.

The sky was red.

The whole sky, from one edge of the world to the other, was the color of blood. And hanging in the middle of it, low and enormous above the rooftops across the street, was the moon. It was huge, bigger than he had ever seen it, and red too, a deep, smoldering red. Its light lay over the street and the houses and the bare trees, and nothing moved anywhere.

He stood at the window, and he felt it.

He knew what it was, and he would not name it. There was no voice and there were no words, only a pull on his heart, steady and strong. Out there beyond the red sky and the burning moon, Someone was looking straight at him, through the glass, through the night, through every locked door inside him, and calling him by name.

*Ezra.*

He took a step back from the window.

*No,* he thought. And then, out loud, his voice hoarse in the red silence: "No."

He woke up on the couch.

The living room was dim and colorless and ordinary. The television was murmuring to itself. Through the front window, the sky over the rooftops was black and full of stars, and a thin white moon hung over the trees, small and pale and nothing at all like the thing in his dream.

He was drenched in sweat. His hands were shaking. He sat forward on the couch with his head in his hands and breathed until his heart slowed down.

A dream. Just a dream. Grief did that to people. He'd read about it somewhere.

But he couldn't get back to sleep.

\* \* \*

At three in the morning he gave up and went upstairs.

He hadn't been in their bedroom in weeks, getting his clothes from the laundry room so he wouldn't have to. Now he stood in the unlit doorway, and the room still smelled faintly of her, of the lavender lotion she kept on the nightstand. He turned on the small lamp by her side of the bed.

Her Bible was there, on the nightstand, where it had always been. Worn soft at the edges, the gold letters almost rubbed off the cover. No one had touched it since before the hospital bed came. Ezra had walked past it a hundred times and never let himself look at it.

He picked it up because the dream had shaken him, and because it was the only thing in the room that was still hers. He sat on her side of the bed with it in his lap and thought of her hands holding it every Thursday night for six years at the dinner table.

He flipped through it and found the page quickly, a place that had been opened many times before.

A sheet of paper lay tucked between the pages, folded in half, soft with age.

He unfolded it.

It was a child's drawing in crayon. A house: their house, with its crooked porch light. Above it, a sky colored red from edge to edge, the crayon laid on in thick, waxy strokes. At the center of the red was a small yellow circle, with light pouring out of it. And in the window, Mia had drawn a small rectangle, and inside it, a brown shape lying on a couch.

Beneath it, in Angela's neat, round handwriting, it said:

*Mia, age 4. "That's what the sky does when Jesus is almost here. That's Daddy on the couch. He doesn't see it because he's sleeping."*

Ezra's hands began to shake again.

He looked at the date Angela had written in the corner. It was from more than a year ago, before the diagnosis, before the chemo, before any of it. Before he had ever slept a single night on that couch.

He looked down at the page he had found. One verse was underlined, twice, in Angela's blue ink, the same blue as the spiral notebook she kept for Israel, and beside it, in the margin, she had written a single word: *Ezra.*

*The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come. (Joel 2:31)*

He sat on his wife's side of the bed until the sky outside the window began to lighten, holding the drawing in one hand and her Bible in the other, and he did not move.

On his wrist, his father's watch still said 3:17. It had not moved in all these years.

Neither had he.

\* \* \*

The phone rang at seven.

He let it go to voicemail. It rang again. On the third time he answered it, because only one person on earth called three times in a row.

"It's my birthday Sunday," Loretta said. No hello. "Seventy-one. I'm not asking you, Ezra. I'm telling you. You're going to put those children in that truck and drive out here, and you're going to eat at my table, and you're going to help me make it."

He opened his mouth.

"Angela's pizza," his mother said, and her voice broke on the name and came right back. "The one I taught her. I've got the yeast. You bring yourself."

The line went dead.

Ezra sat on the edge of the bed with the drawing in his lap and the phone in his hand and the sky going pale in the window. For the first time since the funeral, there was somewhere he had to be.
