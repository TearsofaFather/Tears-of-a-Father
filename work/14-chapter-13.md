## Chapter 13: Blow the Trumpet

> *But if the watchman see the sword come, and blow not the trumpet, and the people be not warned; if the sword come, and take any person from among them, he is taken away in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at the watchman's hand.* — Ezekiel 33:6

**Word study: *watchman* — צָפָה** Hebrew *tsaphah* (Strong's H6822). To lean forward and look into the distance; to keep watch and wait. It is the word for a man on a city wall, straining his eyes toward the road. A watchman is not a man who knows the future. He is a man who will not stop looking.

Ezekiel was a priest who never served in the temple. The Babylonians had carried him away from Jerusalem with the other captives, and he lived among them at Tel-abib, by the river Chebar (Ezekiel 1:1; 3:15). When the word of the LORD first came to him, he went and sat among the exiles where they sat, astonished, and remained there seven days (Ezekiel 3:15). At the end of the seven days the word of the LORD came to him again, and he was made a watchman unto the house of Israel (Ezekiel 3:16-17). If he saw the sword coming and gave no warning, the wicked would die in their iniquity, and their blood would be required at his hand (Ezekiel 3:18). Ezekiel had grieved with the captives for seven days. Now he had to tell them what was coming, and he knew most of them would not want to hear it (Ezekiel 3:7).

\* \* \*

Kambria Elizabeth, Angela's best friend, slept badly on Saturday night.

She had come home from Walt's on Friday at eight in the morning, still in yesterday's clothes from the lab, and lain down on top of the covers, and stared at the ceiling until noon. All that day and the next, she kept coming back to the same things. She thought about the margins of his newspapers. *DAN. 9:27. GEN. 11:4. That no man might buy or sell.* She thought about the old man folding the *Journal* into thirds with the edges lined up exactly, to avoid answering her. On Saturday she scrubbed a bathroom that was already clean, and she went to bed again at nine, and before dawn on Sunday morning she gave up.

She made coffee in the dark. The small oil lamp on her kitchen windowsill was still burning; she kept it filled and lit every night, and nobody but Angela had ever asked her why. *So someone out there can always see a light on,* Kambria had told her once, and Angela had laughed, and then stopped laughing. At the table she laid out her Bible and the heavy blue Strong's Concordance Angela had given her for her fortieth birthday, its spine split from use, and found the place by the ribbon.

She read the prophets in order, a chapter or two every morning. She had been in Ezekiel since October. This morning the ribbon was at chapter thirty-three.

She read the first six verses, and then she put her cup down.

*Son of man, speak to the children of thy people, and say unto them, When I bring the sword upon a land, if the people of the land take a man of their coasts, and set him for their watchman: if when he seeth the sword come upon the land, he blow the trumpet, and warn the people… (Ezekiel 33:2-3)*

She knew the passage. Every Christian who had ever read prophecy knew it. She and Angela had underlined it years ago in the gold notebook, with a star in the margin. But she had never read it at four in the morning, so soon after the Council opened in Jerusalem, with Walt's red pen still before her eyes.

She pulled the concordance across the table.

The first word she looked up was *watchman*. She ran her finger down the long column until she found Ezekiel 33:6, and then the number beside it, and then the number in the Hebrew dictionary in the back. *6822. Tsaphah.* To lean forward. To peer into the distance. To observe, to watch, to wait for.

Kambria read it twice. Every watchman she had ever pictured stood tall and calm on a wall. The word said otherwise: a man leaning forward, straining, his eyes on a road nobody else was watching. It was a tired word. It sounded like her.

The next word was *warn*. She found the verse and the number. *2094. Zahar.* She read the definition, and then she read it again with her finger under each word. *To gleam. Figuratively, to enlighten by caution. To admonish, to teach, to warn. To shine.*

To shine.

She leaned back from the page. The concordance listed every place the word appeared, and she went down the list one by one, as Angela had taught her, turning the thin pages of the Bible to each verse. Most of them were in Ezekiel, the watchman chapters, *warn* and *warn* and *warning*. Then, near the bottom of the column, she found one that was not about warning at all.

Daniel, chapter twelve, verse three. The last chapter of Daniel, the end of the end.

*And they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament; and they that turn many to righteousness as the stars for ever and ever. (Daniel 12:3)*

*Shine.* The same word. *Zahar.*

Kambria laid her palm on the page. In Ezekiel the word meant to warn a man that the sword was coming. In Daniel it meant to shine like the sky. It was the same word, from the same root, in the same Hebrew Bible, and she had read both verses a hundred times and never once known they were joined. To warn someone was to shine a light on the road in front of him. That was all a warning was. Light, held up in the dark, so a man could see where he was going before he got there.

She saw Angela on the sixth floor, the infusion line taped to her arm, holding Celeste's hand and promising to pray for her. She saw Angela at the dinner table on Thursday nights, her hand held open for her husband at every meal, and never withdrawn, even when he left it lying there. Angela had never preached at anyone. She had only shone. Kambria had always believed those were two different things. Now she was not sure they had ever been different at all.

She turned back to Ezekiel and read on to verse nine, the one she had always skipped past because it frightened her less than the others.

*Nevertheless, if thou warn the wicked of his way to turn from it; if he do not turn from his way, he shall die in his iniquity; but thou hast delivered thy soul. (Ezekiel 33:9)*

She read it three times.

For so long she had carried every person she loved, and never once set them down. Ezra. Walt. Elijah at the bar. Her sister in Richmond. The people at the lab. She had gone to bed every night feeling that if they were lost, it would be because she had not found the right words. The verse said otherwise: her part was to blow the trumpet. Whether they turned was between them and God. *…whether they will hear, or whether they will forbear… (Ezekiel 2:5)* She had read that one too, in Ezekiel's second chapter, and never understood it as mercy. It was mercy. It meant she did not have to be God. She only had to be faithful.

The knot she had carried for months came loose, and she wept a little at the table, the coffee going cold at her elbow.

Then she opened her newest notebook to a clean page and wrote the date at the top. *November 15, 2026.* Under it, in pen, she wrote three lines.

*Tsaphah: lean forward. Keep looking.*

*Zahar: to warn is to shine.*

*Blow it. Whether they hear or not. Then leave them with God.*

\* \* \*

At seven she picked up her phone to check the weather, and saw that Jalen's video had passed a million views.

She had posted it herself, at eight minutes to midnight in April, from her car outside the Pattersons' house, an hour after Jalen had said she could. *Untitled. Jalen Patterson, cello.* It had found eleven thousand people by morning, and then it had gone quiet for the summer, as most things did. In September a cellist in Seoul with a famous name had shared it, and the numbers had started to climb again. Now, sometime in the night, it had crossed a million.

She scrolled through the newest comments. A grandmother in Manila. A soldier in Germany. A father in Toronto who wrote that he had driven four hours that morning to see his son. A woman in Ohio who said she had played it at her husband's funeral. *nina_v*, again, who had played it for her whole studio. And underneath almost every one of them, in a dozen languages, the same question.

*Who isn't here?*

*Who was he talking to?*

*Please tell us who "someone who isn't here right now" is.*

Kambria knew. She had been sitting in Row C with her phone held up, and she had watched Jalen's eyes go to the empty seat on the aisle with *DAD* written across the program in black marker, again and again, all through the piece. She had copied the video onto a thumb drive for his father herself, that same night. She did not know if Ezra had ever found it.

She started to type an answer. She deleted it. It was not hers to tell.

She put the phone down on the table, face up, and stared at it until the screen went dark on its own. Then she picked it up again and opened the settings for her voicemail.

\* \* \*

It took her four tries.

The first time, she got as far as her own name and started to cry, and she had to stop. The second time, she sounded stiff and careful, the voice she used for briefing the health department, and she deleted it. The third time, she said too much. She was halfway through Joel chapter two before she heard herself, and she laughed out loud in her empty kitchen, and deleted that one too.

The fourth time, she sat up straight, and held the phone with both hands, and said it plainly, as she would to a frightened caller at three in the morning.

"Hi, you've reached Kambria. If you're hearing this and I don't call you back, it already happened. Don't be afraid. It's not too late. Read Joel 2:32. Call on the name of the Lord."

She played it back. The voice was steady and warm and only a little tired. For a minute she felt foolish in her robe, picturing her section chief ringing about a sample and getting that. Then the foolishness went out of her, and peace was what lay under it.

If she was taken, people would call her. Ezra would call her. Walt would call her. They would call because there was no one else to call, and the phone would ring, and they would hear her voice. She could not leave them a letter. She could leave them this.

She pressed *Save*.

\* \* \*

After church she called her sister. Monica, three years older, a real estate agent in Richmond with two teenage boys, answered from a Kroger aisle where the eggs had been gone for two weeks. Kambria told her what she could bear to hear, then said the rest plainly.

"Grandma told us on that porch in Petersburg and I believe it harder every day. Jesus is coming back. I want you ready when He does."

"Kam, you sound like Grandma's revival tent. I love you. I'm just not doing this in the middle of Kroger."

"Then do it later. Read Joel 2:32. That's all I'm asking."

"Okay," Monica said, the way she said it to her boys when they asked for what they would not get.

Kambria sat in her car in the church lot with her sister's voice still in her ear. The sadness was older than the call. Then she remembered the verse. *…thou hast delivered thy soul. (Ezekiel 33:9)* She had blown the trumpet. She said her sister's name out loud to God, and started the car.

\* \* \*

On Monday night she went to Rooney's.

She had not been inside a bar in years. Rooney's was a narrow room on the corner of Twelfth Street, six blocks from the Pattersons' house, with a long wooden bar, a jukebox nobody played, and a television over the register. The bartender, a heavy, gray-bearded man named Sal, looked up when she came in, still in her coat, and raised his eyebrows. Two men at the far end were already too drunk to sit straight on their stools. Paul's list from Galatians came to her without her asking for it, the one she and Angela had copied into the gold notebook: *drunkenness, revellings, and such like… (Galatians 5:21)* She put it away; she had not come to judge.

Elijah Brooks was on his stool, the third from the wall, where he had sat every night for as long as anyone could remember. He was Ezra's oldest friend, and the funniest man in the neighborhood, and when he saw her his face broke into a wide, delighted grin.

"Well, look at this," he said loudly, so the whole bar could hear. "The doomsday club has sent a delegate."

A couple of men down the bar laughed. Kambria took off her coat and sat down on the stool beside him.

"Ginger ale," she told Sal.

"Ginger ale," Elijah repeated, with deep sorrow. "Sal, you hear that? She came all the way to a bar for ginger ale. That's how you know the end is near."

"I came to talk to you," Kambria said.

"To me?" Elijah laid a palm over his heart. "Kambria. I'm touched. Are you finally leaving the church for me? Because I've been waiting for years."

"I came to tell you something," she said, "and then I'll leave you alone. I promise."

Her tone stopped his smiling. Then the smile came back, thinner. He turned his glass in a half circle on the wood and waited.

So she told him. She told him the same things she had told Monica, plainly, in order. The Council. The drafting committee it had left sitting in Jerusalem. The verses in Daniel, and what Walt had worked out on the bench by the Washington Monument. "Jesus is coming back, Elijah," she said. "Not someday. Soon. And there's a day when it'll be too late to get ready. This isn't that day. That's why I'm sitting on this stool."

Elijah listened without interrupting, which she had never seen him do. When she was done he said nothing. Over the register, Sal had the news on with the sound up. He had kept the sound up since the summer. An earthquake had struck a mountain town in Peru during the Sunday Mass, and the bell tower of the old church lay across the plaza in pieces. Men in hard hats were passing stones hand to hand, and women stood behind the tape with their rosaries, waiting for names. Beneath the pictures, the crawl went on by itself without anyone reading it: Iran, the flu, a ferry lost off Manila.

"You know what I said to Ezra once?" Elijah said finally. "Right here. This stool." He tapped the bar. "I said, if it was true, wouldn't there be a sign? Wouldn't we know?" He laughed, but it came out wrong.

Kambria didn't answer. She looked up at the television.

Elijah looked at it too. He watched the men passing stones until the picture changed. For a few seconds his face was bare: no joke, no grin, nothing to hide behind. Kambria saw how frightened he was, and had been, for years.

"What am I supposed to do, Kambria?" he asked, and for once he was not joking.

"Call on Him," she said. "That's all. Call on the name of the Lord. He'll do the rest."

He held her eyes past the end of the joke. Then the grin came back, all at once.

"Write it down for me," he said. "In case I forget. I'm an old man. My memory's going."

She took a pen out of her purse and pulled a napkin from the stack by Sal's elbow. She put the date in the corner, as she did on everything, and set the numbers down before the words. On it she wrote, in a cramped, careful hand: *Joel 2:32. Romans 10:13. For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.*

Elijah took the napkin and folded it in half, and then in half again. He took out his wallet, and put the napkin in behind his driver's license, and patted it.

"For emergencies," he said, and winked.

"For emergencies," Kambria said.

She finished her ginger ale and put a five-dollar bill on the bar. She was putting on her coat when Elijah spoke again, without looking at her.

"He doesn't come in much anymore," he said. "Ezra."

Kambria stopped with one arm in her sleeve.

"Hasn't for months. He's got somebody." Elijah shrugged. "Don't ask me who. He won't say. I asked him, and he just looked at me." He turned his glass on the wood. "I've known him half my life, and he won't tell me her name. You know what that means."

Kambria thought of a small silver car parked at the curb outside Ezra's house in October, with a red string hanging from the mirror. She said nothing and finished putting on her coat.

"Goodnight, Elijah," she said.

"Goodnight, doomsday." He raised his glass to her without turning around. "Drive safe. Don't get raptured on the Beltway."

\* \* \*

On Tuesday night she was back at the hospital.

The CDC had kept a small team there since the flu came back in October, in a borrowed room off the hospital's microbiology lab, swabbing the sickest patients and sending the samples up to Hyattsville. Most of what came back was the flu, the same strain as the first two waves, only meaner. But on Monday afternoon, three samples from a nursing home in Petworth had come back as something else.

Kambria ran them twice. It was a bacterium, the kind that causes pneumonia, and it would not die. It shrugged off every antibiotic on the panel, even the last two, the ones doctors kept locked away for when nothing else worked. She had seen resistant germs before; every lab had. What she had never seen was its genes. There were pieces in it from organisms that did not live in the same places and had no way to meet, lined up neatly in a row. When nature mixed things, it made a mess. This was not a mess. It was tidy.

She sat looking at the screen until the words stopped meaning anything.

Then she pulled the three patients' charts. She had the access; the CDC agreement gave her team every record they asked for. Three women, all over eighty, all from the same nursing home on Georgia Avenue. All three had been enrolled in October in a free wellness program, run at the home by a company called Verity Health: vitamins, blood pressure checks, and a nasal spray to "support seasonal immunity." Kambria knew the name. Verity Health Labs in Rockville was the contract bench that ran the agency's overflow sequencing; half her confirmation runs since July had gone through its doors, stamped clean. She had never once wondered who owned it. She looked it up on her phone, standing by the sequencer. Its website was clean and white and said almost nothing. At the very bottom, in small gray type, it said that Verity Health was a portfolio company of the Meridian Trust, Dubai.

She stood in the hallway with the phone dark in her hand.

Then she wrote her report and flagged it urgent and sent it up the chain. And before she logged off, she did something she had never done in all her years at the CDC. She copied the raw files onto a thumb drive from her own purse, every read from all three samples, and she zipped the drive into the inside pocket of her coat. It was against every rule she had ever signed. She knew that. But the same quiet voice that had told her to photograph a license plate in October told her that the report might disappear, and that the files might disappear with it.

In the ninth notebook, under the date, she wrote one line. *Pestilence, like Rev. 6:8: a pale horse. Pangs, not the seal. Matt. 24:7-8.* Under it, smaller: *Verity Health. Meridian Trust. Vale.*

Then she tried to tell the people she worked with.

She did not stand up in the break room and preach; she had promised herself she wouldn't. She waited until people came to her, and when they asked her how she was holding up, she told them the truth. She told Dana, her partner on the CDC team, while they loaded the sequencer. She told two of the hospital's young lab techs at the coffee maker. She told a respiratory therapist named Paul, who had been raised Pentecostal and laughed and said his mother told him the same thing every Easter.

Most of them listened politely and changed the subject. Dana told her, kindly, to keep it out of the lab. One of the young techs, a quiet man named Tomás who had only been at the hospital since August, waited until the others had gone, and then asked her, without looking at her, if she could write down "that verse." She wrote it on the back of a specimen requisition. He folded it into the pocket of his scrubs and went back to work.

Celeste came into the break room at a quarter to three with a cup of tea, and her red hair pinned up, and her cheeks pink from the cold of the parking garage. The flu had pulled half the sixth floor down to the emergency department, and she was one of the volunteers who had come down to help. She had been glowing for weeks. Everyone had noticed it. She sat down beside Kambria and put a hand on her shoulder.

"I heard you've been telling everyone the world is ending," she said, and smiled.

"Not ending," Kambria said. "Changing. He's coming back."

"Oh, honey." Celeste squeezed her shoulder. "That's fear talking. You know it is. Fear isn't from God, Kambria. Angela used to say that."

Kambria looked at her. Angela had said it. She had said it on the sixth floor, to a frightened woman in the next recliner, with Celeste standing right there smoothing a blanket.

"Fear isn't," Kambria said. "Warning is."

Celeste laughed, low and musical, and gave her shoulder a pat. Her phone buzzed on the cushion. She glanced down at the screen, and a warmth crossed her face, and then she turned the phone over so the screen was facing down.

Kambria had seen a man do exactly that, at his own kitchen counter, in the spring.

She looked away.

\* \* \*

That morning, when she got home from the hospital, she did not go to bed. First she took down the heavy blue concordance Angela had given her for her fortieth birthday, the one with the split spine, slid the thumb drive down inside the spine where the binding had come loose, and taped the spine shut with packing tape, as Angela had mended her own. Then she put the book back on the shelf between the others, where it had always been.

Across the street, under the streetlight that had been out since summer, a gray Chevrolet Malibu sat with its lights off. She looked at it for a long moment, then wrote down the time.

She sat under the oil lamp in the window with the ninth notebook open, and read back over the last three days. Monica, in Kroger. Elijah, with the napkin in his wallet. Tomás, with the requisition in his pocket. Dana, who had told her to keep it out of the lab. Celeste, who had turned her phone face down. She dated every line now. If it ever came to it, someone would need to see when she knew.

There was one more person.

She had told herself for weeks that it was not her place. He was a grown man and a widower. He had made himself very clear. Every Sunday on his front porch, he took the casserole dish out of her hands and thanked her, and never asked her in. She had let that be her answer, because it was easier, and because she was afraid.

She looked at the verse that headed the page. *His blood will I require at the watchman's hand.* She looked at the second line she had written on Sunday. *Zahar: to warn is to shine.*

Angela had left him a notebook. She had written it for him, every page of it, in her round, patient handwriting, and she had written on the cover *For Ezra. When you're ready.* And he had put it in a closet the night of the funeral, and he had not opened it in a year.

Kambria did not know how much time there was. She only knew there was less than there had been.

She wrote one more line beneath the rest, and underlined it twice.

*Thursday. Ezra.*

She was reaching for the light switch when her phone chimed on the table.

It was an email from the CDC's case system, the automatic kind, with no name at the bottom. *Your submission regarding specimen cluster DC-1117 (Petworth) has been reviewed and closed. No further action is required.*

Kambria read it standing up. Then she sat back down and read the time stamp.

She had flagged the report urgent at nine minutes past two that morning. In all her years, she had never seen an urgent report assigned to a reviewer in less than three days.

This one had been closed at two thirty-one. Twenty-two minutes after she sent it.

Nobody read that fast. Nobody even found it that fast. Not unless they had been sitting there, at two in the morning, waiting for it to come in.
