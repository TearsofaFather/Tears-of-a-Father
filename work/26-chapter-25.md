## Chapter 25: A Way to Escape

> *There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it.* — 1 Corinthians 10:13

**Word study: *a way to escape* — ἔκβασις** Greek *ekbasis* (Strong's G1545). A way out; literally, a stepping out through the end of something, the one road open when every other way was walled in. Paul did not promise the Corinthians that the wall would never come. He promised that God would cut a door in it.

*And she caught him by his garment, saying, Lie with me: and he left his garment in her hand, and fled, and got him out.* (Genesis 39:12)

\* \* \*

The message sat on the screen in the dark cab of the truck, and the cursor blinked under it.

*I'll leave the door open.*

Ezra knew that door. Red paint, a brass knocker shaped like a hand, three steps up from a narrow street in Mount Pleasant. He knew the sound the lock made, and the lamp in the front window, and the smell of the hallway, candles and wine and something sweet underneath. He knew exactly how it would be if he went. She would be frightened, and he would hold her, and for one night neither of them would have to be alone with what had happened. It would feel like comfort. It would feel, for an hour, like the oldest kind of mercy.

His thumb was on the screen. He had started to type. *On my w*

He stopped.

It was not virtue that stopped him. He would be honest about that later, when he told it. It was Angela's Bible lying open on the wheel, which carried her handwriting in the margin of Ezekiel: *For Ezra. You are not him.* He looked from the phone to the page and back to the phone, and he understood that he could not hold both of them in his hands at the same time. One of them would have to go down on the seat.

He set the phone face down on the passenger seat. He sat with the engine running and the open graves of Arlington going black on the hill behind him.

That was when the other voice started.

It did not sound like a voice. It sounded like his own thinking, which was what made it dangerous. It was calm and reasonable and sad.

*You know what you are,* it said. *You were in her bed while your son played to an empty seat. You were in her bed the night your daughter waited outside in the wet grass. You were there when the trumpet sounded, as good as there. You slept through it. Did you think you could cry for one night on a kitchen floor and be clean? Men like you don't get clean. You had twenty years of chances. You used every one of them up.*

He tried to answer it and found nothing to say.

*Go to her,* the voice said gently. *At least she wants you. Nobody else does anymore. They're all gone, and they went without you, and that tells you everything. The door is shut. You heard it shut on Thursday morning. Go where a door is still open.*

Ezra put the truck in gear.

He drove north across the river with the phone face down beside him, and he did not know, for the first ten minutes, which way he was going to turn when he reached the city. The bridges were lit by work lights on generators, and soldiers in Organization armbands waved him through without looking. The streets were dark. At the light where one road climbed toward Mount Pleasant and the other ran east toward home, he slowed almost to a stop. A car behind him flashed its lights. He sat at the fork with his blinker off.

Then he took the right fork, toward Brookland, toward the empty house, and he did not feel good about it. He felt worse, with the last warm room in the world falling away behind him.

\* \* \*

The house was cold when he came in. The power had come back on sometime in the afternoon, and then gone off again, and the clock on the stove was blinking a time that meant nothing. He lit the candle on the dinner table with a match from the drawer. His wife's shoes were still at the front door, side by side, pointed out. He left them where they were. He could not imagine moving them.

He laid Angela's black notebook and her Bible on the table and opened neither, his hands on the closed covers.

The voice had followed him home.

*There's a verse,* it said, *about men like you.*

And there was. He knew it before he went looking, because he had seen it the night before, in the notebook, in Kambria's slanting hand, on a page he had skimmed past without stopping because it frightened him. He opened the notebook now and turned the pages until he found it. Hebrews twelve. Kambria had copied it out under the heading *Esau*, and drawn a line under it, and written a warning beside it in red pen.

*For ye know how that afterward, when he would have inherited the blessing, he was rejected: for he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears.* (Hebrews 12:17)

He read it again.

*…he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears.*

So that was it, then. That was the answer to the question he had been afraid to ask since he got up off the kitchen floor that morning. Esau had sold his birthright for a bowl of stew because he was hungry and it was there, and later, when he understood what he had thrown away, he wept and begged for it back, and it was too late. The door was shut. Tears did not open it.

Ezra had sold his for less than a bowl of stew. For a red door in Mount Pleasant. For a couch and a television and a bottle. For the pleasure of being angry at God.

He sat at the table and felt the warmth of the night before draining out of his chest until there was almost nothing left of it. He had been so sure. He had stood in this kitchen with his hands in the air and felt something take hold of him, and he had been sure. Now he thought: *Maybe that was only grief. Maybe it was only a man so tired he would believe anything. Maybe it was just what you wanted to feel.*

The phone buzzed on the table beside him. He turned it over.

*Celeste: Ezra? Are you coming? I'm scared. Please.*

He looked at it. He looked at the verse. He thought, *What does it matter now? If the door is shut, it's shut. Why not one more night of being held by somebody? What's left to lose?*

He stood up. He took his keys off the hook by the back door.

And with the keys in his hand, standing by the door, he looked back at the table, at the notebook lying open in the candlelight where he had left it, and he saw that Kambria had not stopped writing where he stopped reading.

He had stopped at the verse, because the verse was what he had gone looking for, and it had told him what he already believed. But there was more. Under the red line, in Kambria's slanting print, there was a note, and under the note there was an arrow pointing to the facing page, where the writing changed. The facing page was Angela's.

He came back to the table with the keys still in his fist and bent over the notebook and read it standing up.

Kambria had written: *Careful. Esau sought the BLESSING with tears, not repentance. He wanted the inheritance back. He never once wanted God. He wept over what he lost, not over what he did. Read Genesis 27:41. The minute he stopped crying he planned to kill his brother.*

And under that, smaller: *The devil loves this verse. He'll hand it to anybody who's actually sorry, to make them quit. Don't let him. Turn the page. K.*

Ezra turned the page.

It was in Angela's round handwriting, in blue ink, and it was dated years ago, a Thursday in March. There was a verse at the top, written large and carefully, the way she wrote the ones she wanted to remember.

*There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it.* (1 Corinthians 10:13)

Under it she had written, in smaller letters:

*For whoever is reading this at night with the car keys in their hand.*

Ezra looked down at his hand. He opened it. The keys lay on his palm, the truck key and the house key and the little brass one for the studio, with the plastic tag Jalen had made in shop class that said DAD in raised letters.

He laughed. It came out of him without warning, one short broken sound, and then he could not stop, and then it was not laughing anymore, and he had to sit down.

Under Angela's verse, in a different ink, Kambria had added one more line, and it was the one she always added. He had heard it from her mouth a hundred times on his own porch, and he had shut the door on it a hundred times.

*And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered…* (Joel 2:32)

*Whosoever,* Kambria had underlined. Twice.

He sat with the keys in his hand and read the two pages, the one that said it was too late and the one that said it was not, side by side under the candle. The first page was true. Esau was real. A man could harden his heart until he could no longer turn, and Scripture said so plainly. But the voice in the truck had not read him the whole page. It had read him one verse, cut away from everything around it, and hidden the rest. It had done what his father did at the deacons' table and what the false shepherds had done in Jerusalem: it had quoted the Word to keep him from God.

He was not Esau. Esau had wanted the blessing back. Ezra did not want anything back. He did not want his old life back. He did not want the couch or the bottle or the red door. He wanted God.

He stood up and picked the phone up off the table.

He opened Celeste's last message. *Please.*

He sent no reply. There was nothing he could write to her that would be true and kind at the same time, not tonight. He would pray for her. He would pray for her as long as he lived, and he would mean it. But he could not be the one to go to her. He went into her contact and pressed the red word at the bottom, *Delete Contact*, and the phone asked him if he was sure, and he pressed it again.

Then he put the phone in the drawer under the counter, with the matches and the birthday candles, and shut the drawer.

It was not a victory. Nothing in him felt brave. He felt like Joseph running out of the house with his garment in somebody else's hand, ashamed and shaking, sure he was doing the only thing left to do. *Flee also youthful lusts…* Paul had written to Timothy (2 Timothy 2:22). Not fight them. Not reason with them. Flee.

He went back to the table and sat down. He hung the keys on the back of the chair.

\* \* \*

He did not sleep. He did not try.

He sat at the dinner table through the middle of the night with the candle and the two Bibles, Angela's soft black one and the big old family one his mother had given them on their wedding day, and he read. He read the fifty-first Psalm three times through, out loud, in a whisper, because there was no one in the house to hear him. *Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions.* (Psalm 51:1) David had written it after Bathsheba, after the prophet came and told him a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and David had been angry about the rich man, and the prophet had said, *Thou art the man.* (2 Samuel 12:7) Ezra knew how that felt now. He had been angry at his father for thirty-five years. He was the man.

*Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.* (Psalm 51:10)

He found Job, because Angela had a note in the margin that sent him there. *I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?* (Job 31:1) He did not know you could make a covenant with your own eyes. He made one, at the dinner table, at two in the morning, out loud, and felt foolish, and meant it.

Around four the candle guttered, and he lit another from its stub.

At some point near dawn he stopped reading. There was nothing left now but to do the thing the verses were all pointing at, the thing Kambria had begged him to do on his porch that fall, the thing Angela had done at this table every Thursday night for years with her hand held out to him across the tablecloth.

The night before, he had cried out to Jesus in grief. He had been dragged there. The song had broken him open, and the name had come out of him before he could stop it, and he had been heard. He believed that now. He believed it more than he had believed it last night, because the voice had tried so hard to take it away from him.

But he had not yet laid his life down on purpose, with nothing pushing him. With no song playing. With no tears to carry him. Cold, and awake, and choosing it. This was not a second beginning, for he was already His. It was surrender.

He pushed the Bibles aside. He laid both hands on the table, palms up, where Angela's hand used to lie at every grace, waiting for his.

"Lord Jesus," he said.

His voice sounded strange in the empty kitchen. He kept going.

"I'm calling on Your name. Kambria said whosoever. Angela said whosoever. I'm a whosoever. I'm not asking because I'm scared, though I am. I'm not asking because they're gone, though they are. I'm asking because You're God, and I'm not, and I've spent my whole life pretending I didn't need You. I need You. I'm not going back. Whatever it costs. Whatever's coming. I'm Yours. I'm not going back."

He said it again, slower. *I'm not going back.* And as he said it the second time, he felt the strength go out of him.

It went out of him all at once. His arms went heavy on the table. His head went heavy. The room tilted, and the candle flame stretched long and gold, and he thought, distinctly, *This is what happened to Daniel.* He had read it an hour before, in Angela's Bible, without understanding it: the prophet by the river, seeing the vision of the man clothed in linen, and *there remained no strength in me: for my comeliness was turned in me into corruption, and I retained no strength.* (Daniel 10:8) He understood it now. It was not fear. It was weight. It was what happened to a man made of dust when something that was not dust came close to him.

He tried to stand, and his legs did not hold him.

He went down sideways out of the chair. On the way down, his left arm swung out and his wrist struck the edge of the oak table, hard, and he heard the crack of his father's watch against the wood, a sharp, flat sound in the quiet. Then the floor came up, cold linoleum against his cheek, and the kitchen went away.

\* \* \*

He was not asleep. He would always be sure of that.

He was lying on his face on the floor, and he could not move, and then a hand touched him.

It touched him between the shoulders, and it set him up on his knees and on the palms of his hands, trembling, as it had set Daniel. He could not have looked up. He saw only the floor, and the floor was full of light, a light that was not the candle and not the dawn, that came from no window and threw no shadow, and was warm on the back of his neck.

A voice spoke above him. It was not loud. It did not need to be loud. It was the voice he had heard in the dream of the red sky over the rooftops, the night he stood at his front window and said *No*, the voice that had been calling him through the studio wall every night for all those nights under his son's cello. He knew it the way a child knows his father's step in the hall at night. He had always known it.

*Fear not.*

He was shaking all over. He could not stop.

*Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee.* (Ezekiel 2:1)

And he stood. He did not do it himself. Something stood him up, as his own hands had stood the first wall of the studio upright with his son pushing beside him, and held him there until he was steady.

*I have made thee a watchman.*

He heard it as clearly as he had ever heard anything in his life.

*…I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel: therefore hear the word at my mouth, and give them warning from me.* (Ezekiel 3:17)

The light grew until there was nothing else.

\* \* \*

When he opened his eyes, the kitchen was full of ordinary morning.

The sun was coming in low through the window over the sink, laying a long gold bar across the linoleum and over his outstretched hand. The candle had burned down to nothing and gone out. Somewhere outside, a single bird was singing, the first bird he had heard since Thursday. He was lying on his side on the kitchen floor beside the chair, with a bruise rising on his wrist where it had hit the table.

And the kitchen was ticking.

It took him a moment to understand. It was small and close and steady, a sound he had never heard from that watch on his own wrist. He lifted his left arm off the floor and looked at it.

The old crack across the crystal had split into a star. Under it, the second hand was moving.

He watched it go around once, a full minute, jerking from mark to mark, and held his breath. It had come home from Saudi Arabia at the bottom of the carton, stopped. Watchmakers had looked at it. Angela had talked him into taking it off once, the only time, for a man on Georgia Avenue, for his birthday, and the man had opened the back and said the movement was sound, there was nothing broken, he could not say why it would not run. Ezra had worn it dead on his wrist every day of his life since. Now it was running.

Then he looked at where the hands had stood all those years, and he went still.

He had looked at it every day since boyhood. 3:17. He had never once thought about the number. It was simply the time his father's watch had stopped, the time something had happened in the desert that no one would ever explain to him.

*I have made thee a watchman.*

He got up off the floor so fast he knocked the chair over. He grabbed Angela's Bible off the table with shaking hands and flipped toward the back, past Psalms, past Isaiah and Jeremiah and Lamentations, the thin pages sticking together under his thumb, until he found Ezekiel, and then chapter three, and then the verse, and he read it aloud, standing in the sunlight, and his voice cracked in the middle of it.

*Son of man, I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel: therefore hear the word at my mouth, and give them warning from me.* (Ezekiel 3:17)

Ezekiel three. Verse seventeen.

Three seventeen.

He dropped to the floor with the Bible open on his knees and the watch ticking on his wrist.

His father had been wearing it when it stopped. His father, who had said the words of God at the deacons' table and never lived a line of them. The watch had stopped at the watchman's verse on the night his father died, and it had waited all those years on the wrist of a boy who hated God, and it had started again on the morning that boy said *I'm not going back*.

Angela had underlined the verse. Of course she had. In the margin beside it, in blue ink, she had written one word, and then, at some later time, in different ink, Kambria had written another under it. Angela had written: *Ezra?* With the question mark. And Kambria had written: *Yes.*

He read the rest of the chapter on the floor, and it was not gentle. *When I say unto the wicked, Thou shalt surely die; and thou givest him not warning, nor speakest to warn the wicked from his wicked way, to save his life; the same wicked man shall die in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at thine hand.* (Ezekiel 3:18) Kambria had been one. She had stood on his porch with the clipping in her hand, and he had shut the door on her, and she had gone home and kept watch anyway, with her lamp burning in the window, until the morning the Bridegroom came.

Now her post was empty, and the city was dark, and God had handed the lamp to the last man in Washington who deserved it.

\* \* \*

Before he did anything else, he went back to the notebook.

He turned past the pages he had read, past the statue and the Council and the leopard and the Organization, past every sign the two women had written down before it happened, to the last pages, where the entries stopped. He read what came after the Rapture in the Scripture they had copied out, because now it was his to know. The seals. The trumpets. The beast that would rise and make war with the saints. The mark on the right hand and the forehead. And near the very back, on a page by itself, in Angela's hand, Revelation twenty, verse four.

*…and I saw the souls of them that were beheaded for the witness of Jesus, and for the word of God, and which had not worshipped the beast, neither his image, neither had received his mark upon their foreheads, or in their hands; *and they lived and reigned with Christ a thousand years.* (Revelation 20:4)*

Angela had written nothing beside it, only a small box around it, as she boxed the things she did not want to forget, and under the box she had left the rest of the page blank. Across from it, in Kambria's slanting hand, was a single line: *This is the page with your name on it.*

Ezra read it standing at the counter in the morning light, and he understood it. The ones who had been taken on Thursday were the ones who had believed before. Angela. His children. Kambria. His mother. They had believed without seeing, and they had been kept from the hour of trial. That door was shut, and it was right that it was shut. But the book was not shut. God was still calling names. The multitude in white in Revelation seven, Kambria had written beside it, was the Church already home: *…have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.* (Revelation 7:14) What waited for the ones left behind was the other picture, the souls under the altar in Revelation six, who held to the testimony of Jesus, and the beheaded on the page before him, who would not take the mark.

They would reach that place through it, though. Not around it. Through it.

For the ones left behind, it would not be enough to believe in Jesus the way he had believed in the Washington Nationals, or in the weather. It would not be enough to raise a hand once in a dark kitchen. Something was coming that would demand everything, the right hand and the forehead and the knee, and the only people who would stand were people who loved Him more than they loved being alive.

He picked up a pen from the jar on the counter, one of Angela's blue ones, and he wrote in the blank space under her box, in his own square carpenter's capitals, pressing hard enough to dent the page.

*NOT ONLY BELIEVE. LOVE HIM ENOUGH TO DIE.*

Then he capped the pen and put it in his shirt pocket.

He was still turning pages, Revelation forward and Daniel back, hunting for he did not know what, when he came to a line in Angela's blue ink at the foot of a page, with no verse beside it: *Calvin's box. Pray Ezra opens it someday.* He read it twice. Then he thought of the box.

The cardboard carton Calvin had packed himself and the Army had sent home, his slanting capitals still across the lid, the brown tape gone brittle. It had come to him from his mother's hall closet and sat ever since on the shelf above his workbench in the garage, behind the paint cans, where he did not have to see it. He had not touched it in years. He could not have said why he wanted it now. The watch was running. Whatever the box had to give him, he thought, it had already given.

He went out to the garage and brought it in anyway and set it on the kitchen counter in the light.

It was lighter than he remembered. The folded uniform, the boots, the shaving kit, the packet of letters to the deacons, the black Bible with the church program tucked inside the cover. He lifted them out one at a time and laid them on the counter. At the bottom lay a folded handkerchief, and under the handkerchief, the floor of the box.

He ran his thumb along it. It was not right. He had spent his life inside other men's walls and knew when a thing was thicker than it should be. The bottom of the carton stood a quarter inch higher on the inside than it should have, and what lay there was not cardboard. It was a plate of thin plywood, cut to fit, with a hairline seam where it met the walls of the box, a seam that did not belong. A man with a pocketknife and patience had cut it and glued it in.

He took a butter knife from the drawer and worked the point of it into the seam. The old glue gave with a dry little crack. He pried, and the plate lifted away, and in the flat dark space beneath it lay an envelope.

It was long and white, gone the color of weak tea, and sealed. Across the front, in a big, slanting, old-fashioned hand that Ezra had not seen in thirty-five years but would have known anywhere in the world, was a single word.

*Ezra.*

He sat down at the table with it and turned it over in his hands before he opened it. The flap had been sealed with cellophane tape gone yellow and brittle. He slid the butter knife under the tape and lifted it, and drew out three sheets of thin airmail paper, folded in thirds, covered on both sides in his father's slanting hand.

The first line said: *Somewhere in Saudi Arabia. February 23, 1991. Night.*

His father had died on the twenty-fourth.

\* \* \*

*Son,*

*They tell us we go in before first light. I do not know if I will come back, so I am going to write you what I could not say to your face, and if I come home, I will burn this, and if I do not, I have hidden this under the bottom plate of my box, where nobody will look for it but you, and I pray it finds you when you are a man.*

*I have been in the desert for weeks. There is nothing out here but sand and sky and men waiting, and a man has a lot of time to think when he is waiting. I have done more thinking out here than in all the years before. I did not like most of what I thought.*

*I think about your mother's rolls. Of everything, that is what comes back to me out here, the smell of that kitchen on a Sunday morning. I ate them with my head down because I could not look at her and take them both. I never once told her they were the best thing in that house. If you get the chance, tell her for me.*

*I want you to know first that I am sorry. For your mother. For the nights. For the bottle and the belt and the hand. For the gun. I will never forget your face when I put that gun to your head, Ezra. You did not cry. You did not look down. You looked at me like you were already a man and I was the boy. I knew then I had lost you, and I knew I deserved to lose you. I ran away from that look. That is the truth. I told the church I was going to war for my country. I was running from my own front door.*

*Now here is the worst thing, and I am going to say it plain, because there is nobody out here to perform for.*

*I do not know God.*

*I have known about Him all my life. My mama took me to church before I could walk. I could quote you half the Bible before I was twenty. I have led devotion at the deacons' table for nine years and lined out every hymn in the book and lifted my hands at the closing prayer every Sunday so the whole church could see me do it. I have counted the Lord's money and put some of it in my own pocket and gone up front the next Sunday and prayed over the offering. I quoted "Wives, submit" to your mother and left out the next verse. I quoted "Children, obey" to you and left out "Fathers, provoke not." I used His Word like a strap.*

*And I never once knew Him. Not one day. I quoted a God I never knew.*

*There is a chaplain out here, a young white boy from Tennessee, couldn't be more than twenty-six. He held a service on Sunday in a tent, and he read Matthew seven, and when he got to the part where Jesus says, "Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name," and He says, "I never knew you," I had to get up and walk out of that tent. I walked out into the sand in the dark and I threw up. Because that is me. That is the whole of me, son. I prophesied in His name. I did many wonderful works, in front of people. And He never knew me, because I never let Him.*

*I have been trying to pray since Sunday. Real prayer, not the kind I do at the table. It is hard. I do not know how. I am forty-one years old, and a deacon, and I do not know how to talk to God when nobody is listening. But I am trying. I said to Him last night, Lord, I do not know You, but I want to. If You will have a man like me. And I think, I am not sure, but I think something heard me.*

*Here is what I want you to know, and this is the reason I am writing this at all.*

*The God I showed you is not God. The man you saw at the deacons' table on Sunday and in the hallway on Friday night is not what God is like. I was a liar, son. Do not believe my lie. Your mother knows Him. Watch her. She is the real thing. When she prays at night with her door shut, that is God. When she puts her hand on your face after I hit you, that is God. Not me. Never me.*

*Do not be like me. Do not learn your God from your daddy. Find Him yourself. And if you ever have a boy of your own, do not let him grow up afraid of you. Build something with him. Anything. A birdhouse. A wall. Just do it with your hands next to his. I never did that with you, and I would give anything tonight to have done it once.*

*I have to go. They are calling us out.*

*I love you. I do not think I ever said it. I love you, Ezra. I have loved you since the day you were born and I did not know how to show it, and that is on me, not on you. You were a good boy. You are a good boy.*

*If I do not come back, I hope God will let me see you again someday. I do not know if He will. But I hope.*

*Your father,*

*Calvin*

*Matthew 7:21–23. Read it, son. Then read the rest of the book, and do not stop where I did.*

Ezra stopped. He had read those same verses in the truck only hours before, and had not known they were already waiting for him in his father's hand.

\* \* \*

Ezra read it three times.

The first time he read it fast, skipping ahead, looking for the blow. The second time he read it slowly, every word, with his finger under the lines. The third time he could not see the words at all, and he read it anyway, because he knew them by then.

*Build something with him. Anything. A wall.*

He looked across the kitchen, out the window, at the white clapboard studio across the yard in the morning sun. He thought of an eleven-year-old boy with his father's black marker. *ME AND DAD. FIRST WALL.* He thought of himself on that first night in the studio, sitting in one of the folding chairs, crying, and Jalen silent beside him, and Ezra saying, *Because my dad never built anything with me.*

He put the letter down on the counter and covered his face with both hands. Under his sleeve, the watch kept time. His father had written, a night before the end, that he thought something had heard him. Ezra could not know. He hoped, and for now that was all he was given.

\* \* \*


His father had told him not to stop where he had stopped. The rest of what Kambria knew was still in her house, and he did not mean to leave it there. Early that Sunday morning he drove to her door.

It was a small brick duplex on a narrow street in Brookland, twelve blocks from his own, with a chain-link fence and a concrete stoop. The spare key still lay under the third brick from the left along the walk. He did not need it.

The door stood an inch open. The frame around the lock had been split from top to bottom, white wood showing through the paint, the deadbolt hanging by a screw. A strip of yellow tape had been pulled across the jamb and torn through, and one end fluttered in the cold.

Ezra stood on the stoop with his hand flat against the door.

Someone had come for her. Not the dark. Not the trumpet. Someone with a boot.

He pushed it open.

The house was freezing and it was wrecked. Drawers pulled out and upended, cushions slit, books flung off their shelves and lying where they fell with their spines cracked. The front room had been stripped to the floorboards. And in the spare room, where Walt said the wall had been, there was no wall.

There was a plaster ruin. Every headline and photograph, every filing and map, was gone, and in their place hundreds of little holes, the pinholes where the paper had hung, scattered across three walls like a night sky. Scraps of red yarn dangled from some of them. The desk had been swept bare. Square clean rectangles in the dust showed where a stack of notebooks had sat for years, and a ring where a mug had been. On the floor under the window lay the poster board, torn in half. He picked up the top piece. *WHO P*, and a rip.

He stood in the middle of the room and understood that this was an arrest, and not a rapture.

He had to hold the thought in both hands, because it was so much worse and so much better than what he had believed. Not gone, then. Not yet. Taken. By men, with a warrant or without one, in the night, from her own kitchen.

*If you're hearing this and I don't call you back, it already happened.*

Maybe it had. Maybe the trumpet had found her in a cell. But the men had come first, before dawn on Thursday, and he did not know whether she had been rescued or only moved.

On the chair by the desk, untouched, lay her Bible. Nobody had thought it worth taking. He picked it up, and it fell open in his hands to the place it had been opened most. The left-hand page held one verse, underlined in red ink so many times the line had worn through the paper.

*For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first…* (1 Thessalonians 4:16)

He held it for a long time. Then he took a laundry basket from the hall, emptied out like everything else, and laid the Bible in it with great care, on top of nothing.

He went to the kitchen.

The lamp was there, on the sill over the sink, where someone had set it down. It had burned dry. A black ring of soot crowned the chimney and the wick had gone to ash. Whoever had broken her door and torn her wall had walked past a brass lamp in a window and left it burning. He didn't understand that. He understood the dish beside it, a pan of macaroni and cheese gone dull under its foil, and the envelope propped against it in her slanting hand. *For the Pattersons. Thursday. Back by eight. K.* He read it twice and put it in his shirt pocket. He left the dish where she had set it. It was hers to give.

But he wasn't done. Walt had told him: *She said to make you read the notebook first.* Kambria wrote everything down. A woman who dated every line did not leave her last pages for strangers to find.

He stood in the kitchen, in the cold, and made himself think like her.

The window seat. A cushion on the sill, a wooden bench beneath it, the sort a woman would put in a small kitchen to keep her feet out of the draft. Someone had thrown the cushion off and kicked the bench out of line, but they hadn't pulled up the boards. He crouched. On the third board from the wall, the edge of the wood was worn pale along one side by a thumbnail, again and again, until it shone.

He pressed. The board lifted.

Under it, in the dark, wrapped in a dish towel, lay a single notebook. Black cloth cover. Elastic band. The ninth.

He opened it where a ribbon marked the last page. *November 25, 2026. 11:20 p.m.* The last entry in her slanting hand. Above it, the day before, and the day before that, line after line, dated and signed, covering pages.

Before he went, he saw to the lamp. He filled it from the can under the sink, steadying the base so he would not spill, and trimmed the burned end of the wick with the kitchen scissors, and wiped the soot from the chimney with a dish towel until the glass was clear. He struck a match. The flame came up small and blue and then gold, and steadied, and he lowered the chimney over it.

He did not leave it in her window. Whoever had broken that door had left it burning, and they would be back.

He did not read the notebook there. He had heard the sound of a car outside, slow, in the street.

He set the lamp's chimney straight, the notebook inside his jacket against his ribs, and went through the front room to the stoop. At the end of the block, half hidden by a bare tree, sat a gray Chevrolet Malibu with its engine running and its windows fogged. A man's shape behind the wheel held a newspaper up before his face, and did not turn the page.

Ezra turned his back on it and went down the steps with the basket on one hip and the lamp in his other hand, the flame low behind its chimney, as if he had every right to be there. He wedged the lamp on the floor of the cab between the basket and the seat. He took the long way home, through Brookland and Fort Totten and back across Rhode Island Avenue, and watched his mirrors, and the Malibu did not follow.

That did not mean it was not there.

On the drive, the city went by in gray. Burned-out cars on New York Avenue. A line of people outside a pharmacy, six blocks long. Soldiers at the intersections. A church on Rhode Island Avenue with its doors standing open and its lot full of cars that had not moved since Thursday, their windshields white with frost.

At the curb in front of his own house, he looked across the street at Walt's porch. The old man's curtains were drawn, but there was a light behind them.

Ezra carried the lamp up his own front steps and set it in his front window, beside the switch where Mia used to reach up on her toes in her nightgown to turn on the porch light, so that Daddy could find the door.

The notebook he put on the dinner table, under the candle. Then he went across the street, and knocked.
