## Chapter 10: Ten to One

> *…be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up.* — Daniel 3:18

**Word study: *serve* — פְּלַח** Aramaic *pelach* (Strong's H6399). To serve, to worship, to pay reverence. In Daniel's Aramaic, serving a god and worshipping him are one word. The three young men knew that bowing once, just to get along, was already service.

King Nebuchadnezzar set up his image of gold on the plain of Dura, and he sent for every prince and governor and captain and judge and treasurer in the empire to come to its dedication (Daniel 3:1-2). They came from every province and stood on the plain in their finest robes (Daniel 3:3). Then a herald cried aloud, "To you it is commanded, O people, nations, and languages, That at what time ye hear the sound of the cornet, flute, harp, sackbut, psaltery, dulcimer, and all kinds of musick, ye fall down and worship the golden image" (Daniel 3:4-5). Whoever would not bow would be cast that same hour into the midst of a burning fiery furnace (Daniel 3:6). The music played, and the whole plain went down on its face (Daniel 3:7). Only three young men were left standing: Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego (Daniel 3:12). They were brought before the king in his fury (Daniel 3:13), and they offered no plea and no argument. They said only that their God was able to deliver them: "But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods" (Daniel 3:17-18).

\* \* \*

On the Sunday after the statue, Kambria sat in the fourth pew on the left at Mount Zion Christian Center, Angela's church on Rhode Island Avenue, in the seat beside the one that had been Angela's.

Nobody sat in Angela's seat. It had stayed empty since the funeral, going on a year now, and the women who came early to save their places walked past it with their eyes averted. Kambria laid her Bible on the cushion beside her every Sunday, as she used to when Angela was running late, and picked it up again after the closing prayer.

Mia sat on her other side this morning, in a purple dress with her hair in two puffs, swinging her patent leather shoes. Loretta had driven her in from Cedar Hollow because Ezra was "working." Loretta sat at the end of the pew in her Sunday hat and said nothing about where her son was, and Kambria didn't ask.

Before the service started, Mia reached across Kambria's lap and patted the empty cushion beside the Bible with her small hand. "Mommy's saving it," she whispered. "For Daddy. For when he wakes up."

Kambria had to look down at her hands after that.

Pastor Grant came to the pulpit at eleven-thirty. Pastor Xavier Grant was sixty-one years old, a gentle, gray-haired man with reading glasses he was always pushing back up his nose. He carried a pocket calendar and a fountain pen, and he answered every question he was asked plainly and honestly, even the hard ones. He had been Angela's pastor for fifteen years. He had come to the house every morning in her last weeks, and she had prayed over him instead of the other way around, and he had preached her funeral and wept through the last half of it.

"Turn with me," he said, "to the twelfth chapter of Genesis."

His sermons always ran long, and this one was no different. He preached for forty-five minutes on God's promise to Abraham: *And I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed. (Genesis 12:3)* He preached on Romans 11, where Paul asks whether God has cast away His people Israel, and answers, *God forbid. (Romans 11:1)* He preached that the God who keeps His promises to Israel is the same God who keeps His promises to the church, and that a Christian who loves the Bible ought to love the people God chose to carry it.

He preached it well. He preached it with his whole heart, and Kambria believed every word of it. She had read those same chapters with Angela at the dinner table. She wrote *Genesis 12:3* and *Romans 11:1–2* in her notebook and underlined them both.

Then, near the end, he took off his glasses and folded them and held them in his hand.

"Church, some of you have seen the news this week," he said. "There's going to be a great gathering in Jerusalem the first week of November. Leaders from all over the world, all the nations together, coming to talk about peace and about a common moral law for all mankind." He smiled. "Now, I don't know what will come of it. But I know the Bible tells us to *Pray for the peace of Jerusalem: they shall prosper that love thee. (Psalm 122:6)* So when you go home today, I want you to pray for that gathering. Pray that God will use it."

"Amen," said the church.

Kambria didn't say amen. Her pen hung over her notebook and did not come down.

\* \* \*

She caught him in the fellowship hall afterward, by the coffee urns, where the coffee was burnt, as it always was.

"That was a good word, Pastor," she said.

"Well, thank you, Sister Kambria." He took her hand in both of his. "You doing all right? I know this time of year is hard. It's almost a year now."

"It's almost a year." She held on to his hand a moment. "Pastor, can I ask you something about the end of the sermon? About the gathering in Jerusalem?"

"Of course."

"You said pray that God will use it." She set the question down plainly. "But peace on whose terms? A common law for all mankind, written by who? Because I've been reading about it. And I don't think it's going to say Jesus is Lord."

Pastor Grant considered her over the top of his coffee cup. Then he set the cup down and drew her aside, away from the urns, to the window by the coat rack where nobody was standing.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

"You know that?"

"I've read Daniel too, Sister. And Second Thessalonians, and the thirteenth of Revelation. I was reading them before you were born." He polished a lens on his tie. "A law for all mankind with His name left off it. A peace that comes in through the side door. I know what that looks like."

"Then why did you tell four hundred people to pray God would use it?"

"Because He will. He uses everything." He looked out the window at the parking lot, where families were buckling children into car seats. "Half the people in that room are hanging on by a thread. Some of them don't know the Lord yet, and some of them think they do. If I stand up there and preach fire every Sunday, the ones who most need to hear it will stop coming, and they'll never hear the rest." He turned back to her. "Paul told Timothy to pray for kings and for all that are in authority, because God 'will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth.' All men. That's who I preach to. I'm not called to call the judgment down on anybody. I'm called to turn the light on and let God show them what's in the dark."

"And the ones who don't see it?"

"The ones who are watching will."

In the margin of her notebook, under *Genesis 12:3*, she wrote: *1 Tim. 2:4. He sees. He chose mercy.*

\* \* \*

That night she ate supper alone, the television muttering in the corner.

She lived in a small brick duplex in Brookland, with a spare room she used for her notebooks and a kitchen just big enough for one table and two chairs. She had bought the second chair for Angela. It was the chair Angela used to sit in when they met here instead of at Angela's house, on the Thursdays when Ezra had the TV too loud.

The evening news came on at six. The Euphrates was at its lowest level in recorded history, the anchor said. Dams upstream in Turkey held back what was left, and farmers in Iraq were walking across a riverbed where boats used to go. In one place the falling water had uncovered the ruins of a city that had lain under the river for three thousand years.

Kambria stopped chewing.

Then Jerusalem. A hotel high on the Mount of Olives called the Olivet Crown, long and curved, all pale stone and dark glass, with workers on scaffolding hanging banners. *The Council for Universal Moral Law: Opening Session, Jerusalem,* the caption said. *November 2–6.* Two hundred and fifty delegates from more than a hundred and forty countries were coming to agree on a short list of moral principles that, in the words of one organizer, "every person on earth can live by, whatever they believe."

"The principles are based on the Seven Laws of Noah," the anchor said, and a law professor in a bow tie came on to say that five of the seven would pass without a fight. "The difficult conversations will be around the other two: blasphemy and idolatry. How do you define idolatry in a document meant for the whole world? Some traditions would consider the worship of a human being as God to fall under that heading. So the drafters have some delicate work to do."

The anchor moved on to the weather. Kambria didn't hear it.

*The worship of a human being as God.* She knew exactly who he meant. Every person at Mount Zion that morning had sung to Him. Angela had died with His name on her lips.

She reached across the table for her notebook. Her hand was shaking, and she made it stop.

Then, because the hotel on the mountain would not leave her alone, she picked up her phone and found the Council's own website. Somebody was paying for all of it: the hotel, the flights, the banners. She scrolled to the bottom of the page. There was a long list of sponsors: universities, foundations, two governments. The last name on the list was in the smallest type of all. *The Meridian Trust, Dubai.* No logo. No link. When she searched for it, she found a post-office box in Dubai and nothing else. She wrote it down.

\* \* \*

She dated a clean page. *October 25, 2026.* Then she wrote down the broadcast as exactly as she logged a sample at the lab. *Council for Universal Moral Law. Jerusalem. Nov 2–6. Olivet Crown hotel, Mount of Olives. 140+ nations. 250 DELEGATES. Seven Laws of Noah. Idolatry: "the worship of a human being as God."* And at the bottom, on a line by itself: *Euphrates. Lowest in history. Ruins showing in the riverbed.* She stayed on that line until the words stopped moving. Then she wrote two references beside it, *Revelation 9:14* and *Revelation 16:12*, and under them, because she would not put words in God's mouth: *Not yet. Watch.*

Then she opened her Bible to Genesis 9 and read it through twice, every word God spoke to Noah when he came out of the ark. Be fruitful and multiply. Every moving thing shall be meat for you. Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed. And the bow in the cloud, the token of a promise God made to never again destroy the earth by water. She counted. There was no list of seven anywhere in it. There were no courts, no law against blasphemy, no law against idolatry. The Seven Laws of Noah did not come from Genesis. They came from the Talmud, from rabbis writing more than a thousand years after Moses, who drew them out of the text and gave them Noah's name. Under the broadcast notes she wrote, and underlined it: *NOT IN GENESIS. Rabbinic tradition (Talmud). They call it Noah's. God never wrote it.*

She went back over what she had written, line by line. Then she went into the spare room and came back with two of her older notebooks, the third and the sixth, and laid them open on the table side by side.

In the third notebook, years back, in Angela's round handwriting, there was a newspaper clipping about the Seven Laws of Noah and a group that was working to spread them among non-Jews. Angela had written underneath it: *Good laws. But watch what happens to the seventh. Watch who defines "idolatry."* Angela had drawn a box around that line, as she drew a box around one line on every page, the one she most wanted to remember. And under that, in Kambria's own hand: *Revelation 13:15. Worship the image or die.*

In the sixth notebook, there was a verse she had copied out some while later, after she had read more about the Noahide movement, because she wanted to remember what the real promise said.

*In those days it shall come to pass, that ten men shall take hold out of all languages of the nations, even shall take hold of the skirt of him that is a Jew, saying, We will go with you: for we have heard that God is with you. (Zechariah 8:23)*

She read it aloud in the quiet kitchen. Ten men out of all the nations.

She knew the verse. It was from Zechariah, and it was a beautiful promise. It was about the day the true King would reign in Jerusalem, when the nations would stream to Him and ask to learn His ways. It was about Jesus. She and Angela had been sure of that.

And now, in the week before that promise, ten men from every nation were packing their suitcases for Jerusalem, to a hotel built on the mountain where His feet were going to stand, to write a law that might call worshiping Him a crime.

"It's the counterfeit," she whispered. "It comes first. It always comes first."

She wrote it down. *The counterfeit comes before the real thing. Ten men from every nation. Right promise, wrong king.*

Then she read it over once more and weighed it, for Angela had always weighed what she wrote.

Pastor Grant had been right that morning. God had not cast away His people. The Jewish people were still the apple of His eye, the land was still the land He had promised to Abraham, and every word of that promise still stood: *I will bless them that bless thee.* Nothing she had seen on the news changed one letter of it. The families who would pray at the Western Wall that week had done nothing to her. Neither had the grandmothers of the World Diocese lighting candles for their grandchildren, or the Muslim cab drivers she saw kneeling on their prayer rugs in the hospital parking lot at dawn. They were sheep having no shepherd, and she had been one too. Most of them were only following the people they had been taught to trust, and she could not find it in her heart to blame them for it.

It was the shepherds she was watching. The men in the robes and the dark suits at the front of the room, who had read the same Scriptures she had and knew better, and who were leading the whole flock toward a table with no Christ at it. They would write a law for the whole world and call it God's old covenant with Noah, made new, though a covenant God had already made was not theirs to remake. God had said what He thought of shepherds like that through the prophet Ezekiel: *Woe be to the shepherds of Israel that do feed themselves! (Ezekiel 34:2)* The flock would not answer for its shepherds. The men at the front of that hall would answer for what they did with the truth they were given. So would she.

\* \* \*

The phone rang that evening. It was Walt. Walt never called twice in one week.

"You seeing this?" he said.

"I'm seeing it. Every channel."

"I set type for the *Star* the night they signed the Babylonian Bylaws," Walt said. His voice was rough. "I set the headline myself. Seventy-two-point Bodoni, all caps. PEACE. I was proud of that headline, Kambria. I went home and told my wife I'd set a piece of history." He cleared his throat. "Now I don't know what I set."

"Walt, you set the news. You didn't write it."

"A law for the whole world. Every nation at one table. And every religion on earth at the same table, smiling for the cameras." She heard newspaper rustling on the other end, and then something that might have been a cough. "I'm an old man. I've read that book my whole life. I always thought I'd understand it better than this when it finally happened."

"I don't think anybody understands it better than this," Kambria said. "I think we just watch."

"Watch for what?"

Her eyes returned to Daniel 3, open on the table in front of her. "Nebuchadnezzar set up his image, and then he waited for the music. When the horns played, everybody bowed."

"And the three who didn't."

"Went into the fire." She laid her hand flat on the page. "So I'm watching for the music."

\* \* \*

After he hung up, she sat with her Bible open and did not read it, and then did.

She read the whole third chapter of Daniel out loud to the empty chair across the table. She read about the herald and the music and the plain full of people going down on their faces. She read about the three who stayed standing. She read their answer to the king, and she read it twice, because it was the part she had always loved the most.

*If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up. (Daniel 3:17-18)*

*But if not.*

They hadn't known whether God would save them from the fire. They had only known that He could, and that they would not bow either way.

Then she read what happened next. The king heated the furnace seven times hotter than it had ever been heated, so hot that the soldiers who threw the three young men in died at its mouth. And then the king leaped up from his throne in astonishment.

*Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God. (Daniel 3:25)*

Kambria touched the page.

She thought of Angela on the sixth floor, with the drip taped to her arm, reading Psalm 23 aloud to a room full of strangers. *Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me… (Psalm 23:4)* Angela had walked into the fire. And she had not walked in alone.

She thought of Ezra across the city, in a house with the curtains closed, working on a Sunday that nobody believed was work.

She bowed her head at the dinner table and prayed for him by name, as Angela used to, every Thursday night for all those years. She prayed for Mia in her purple dress and for Jalen in his dorm downtown. She prayed for Walt alone with his newspapers, and for Pastor Grant, who saw all of it and chose mercy. She prayed for Loretta. And then, because she had promised Angela she would, she prayed for herself.

*Lord, when the music plays, keep me standing.*

When she lifted her head, it was dark outside the window. She turned to the last page she had written on and added one more line beneath, in deliberate letters.

*But if not.*
