## Chapter 14: The Door

> *Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me.* — Jesus, Revelation 3:20

**Word study: *knock* — κρούω** Greek *krouō* (Strong's G2925). To knock, to rap on a door with the knuckles. In Revelation 3:20 it is in the present tense: I stand, and I am knocking, and I keep on knocking. He does not knock once and go away.

In the Song of Solomon, a young bride lies asleep at night, though her heart is awake, and she hears her beloved knocking at the door (Song of Solomon 5:2). He asks her to open to him, for his head is filled with dew (Song of Solomon 5:2). She does not rise; she has put off her coat and washed her feet, and she asks how she can dress again and soil them (Song of Solomon 5:3). He puts his hand in by the hole of the door, and her heart is moved for him (Song of Solomon 5:4). At last she rises to open, her hands dripping with myrrh upon the handles of the lock (Song of Solomon 5:5). But her beloved has withdrawn himself and is gone. She seeks him and cannot find him; she calls him, and he gives her no answer (Song of Solomon 5:6).

\* \* \*

Kambria parked across the street from the Pattersons' house a little before six on Thursday evening, and sat in her car with the engine off.

She had not slept much. She had spent most of the afternoon at her dinner table, writing down what she wanted to say, crossing it out and writing it again. The page was in her coat pocket now. She already knew she would not read from it. She had brought it anyway, as she brought a list of questions to her own doctor and then forgot all of them the moment he walked in.

It was nearly dark. The streetlights had come on along the block, and the air smelled of wet leaves and someone's fireplace. Across the street, the porch light was already burning over the Pattersons' front steps. Mia turned it on herself every evening at dusk, up on her toes by the door. Everyone on the street knew that. Nobody switched it off before morning.

In the brick house directly behind her, a curtain moved. Kambria looked over her shoulder and saw Walt standing at his kitchen window in his brown cardigan, with a coffee mug in his hand. He regarded her through the glass. Then he raised the mug, just slightly, and let the curtain fall.

She had told him on Wednesday that she was going. He had called it neither a good idea nor a bad one. He had only said, "Thursday's his night out. Go early."

She locked the car and crossed the street.

\* \* \*

Mia opened the door before she could ring the bell.

"Miss Kambria!"

Mia threw both arms around Kambria's waist and held on. She was in pajama pants and a sweatshirt with a cartoon fox on it, and her hair was in two puffs, and she smelled like crayons and macaroni and cheese. A brown teddy bear with one ear worn shiny hung from her hand by its paw.

"Hey, baby girl." Kambria knelt on the porch and hugged her properly. "Hey. Look how tall you got."

"I'm drawing," Mia announced. "Come see. Grandma went home already. Daddy's in the shower. He's going out."

She took Kambria by the hand and pulled her inside, through the front hall and into the kitchen, before Kambria could decide whether she was allowed in.

The kitchen looked as it always had. The same yellow curtains Angela had sewn herself. The same scratched table. The same chair at the end, Angela's chair, with nobody in it. A flyer from the spring was still pinned to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a strawberry: *Senior Recital. Thursday, April 16.*

Mia climbed onto her chair and picked up a red crayon. Before her on the table lay a sheet of paper with the beginnings of a picture: their house, the porch light drawn crooked, and a black sky colored in from edge to edge.

"That's our house," Mia said. "At night."

"I see," Kambria said. "It's beautiful."

Mia bent over the paper. In the middle of the black sky she began to draw a circle, and then to fill it in with the red crayon, pressing hard, going around and around. Kambria watched the circle darken and take a shine from the wax.

"What's that?" Kambria asked. Her voice came out thin.

"The moon," Mia said, without looking up. "It's going to be red. I'm making it for Daddy. I'm going to put it on the fridge so he sees it." She pressed harder. "He doesn't look at my pictures anymore. But he'll see this one, because it's on the fridge."

Kambria pressed her knuckles to her lips.

She knew the verse. She had copied it into her notebook a dozen times. *The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come. (Joel 2:31)* She had copied the one after it a dozen times too, and she said it to herself now, silently, sitting at Angela's dinner table with Angela's daughter coloring beside her. *And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered… (Joel 2:32)*

Upstairs, the shower turned off.

\* \* \*

Ezra appeared on the stairs a few minutes later in a clean, pressed shirt that was new to her, with his hair still damp. He smelled of cologne. He stopped in the kitchen doorway when he saw her, and for a second his face showed nothing but surprise.

Then it closed.

"Kambria," he said.

"Hi, Ezra."

"It's Thursday."

His phone lay on the counter by the bread box. It lit while he stood there and buzzed once against the laminate, and Kambria read a single line on the screen before his hand came down and flipped it face down. *C: Left the light on for you.*

No one she knew went by a single letter. She did not let herself wonder.

"I know. I'm sorry. I won't stay long." She stood up. "Can I talk to you for a minute? On the porch?"

He looked at Mia, who was coloring and pretending not to listen. He looked at the clock on the stove. Then he walked past Kambria without a word, through the front hall, and opened the front door, and held it for her.

She went out. He came out after her and pulled the door almost shut behind him, so that it stood open a few inches. The porch light was bright over their heads. The air was cold.

"What is it?" he said.

She had the page in her pocket. She did not take it out.

"I'm going to say it plainly," she said. "And then I'll go. I promise." She took a breath. "I think it's very close, Ezra. Everything Angela and I wrote down for years. The statue on the Mall. The Council in Jerusalem. The committee it left sitting. Daniel. Ezekiel. All of it. It's not coming someday. It's coming now." She heard her own voice shake, and she made it stop. "I think the Lord is coming back for His people, very soon. And I don't think you're ready."

Ezra's hand closed on the doorframe. He didn't say anything.

"She left you a notebook," Kambria said. "Angela. It's in the hall closet. I know you know it's there. Every page of it is for you. She wrote it for you in that bed when she could barely hold the pen. She wrote *When you're ready* on the cover." Her eyes were stinging. "Ezra, I don't think there's time to wait until you're ready. Please. Open it. Tonight. Before you go wherever you're going."

"Don't," he said, very low. "Don't tell me what Angela wanted."

"I'm not telling you. She told me. She told me herself, at that table, the week before she died. 'When he's ready, Kam, make sure he finds it.'"

"I know where it is."

"Then open it."

"Time for what?" His voice rose. "That's what I want to know. You said there isn't time. Time for what, Kambria? The end of the world?" He laughed, a short, hard sound. "You and her. All those years. Every Thursday night at my dinner table, with your Bibles and your clippings and your colored pens. Every week, it was coming. Every week, it was the end. I came home tired from work every Thursday, and my kitchen was a prayer meeting." He stopped. His voice dropped. "And she died anyway. All that praying. All those notebooks. She died anyway."

"She didn't die anyway," she said. "She died ready."

He stared at her.

"She died holding your hand," Kambria said. "She died with the name of Jesus in her mouth. She wasn't afraid. You were there. You saw it. That wasn't her losing, Ezra. That was her going home. And she wanted you to be able to come too. That's all that notebook is. It's her holding her hand out to you one more time."

She thought she had reached him. His eyes filled, his mouth tightened, his hand on the doorframe began to shake. He looked down at the porch boards, and she saw the man who had stood in the doorway of that room with his arms folded, trying not to fall apart.

Then he looked up, and the anger was back.

"Get off my porch," he said.

"Ezra..."

"Get off my porch. Get off my property. I mean it, Kambria." His voice was rough and low. "Don't come back here with this. Don't bring it to my door again. Not for Mia, not for Jalen, not for anybody."

"Please," she said.

He stepped back inside, and the door closed in her face.

She heard the lock turn.

\* \* \*

She stood on the porch.

She lost count of the seconds. The porch light buzzed over her head. A car crept by on the wet street. She lifted her hand to knock again, and held it there, inches from the wood.

She did not knock.

She stood with her hand raised, and she thought of a verse she and Angela had read together at the dinner table on the other side of that door, more times than she could count. *Behold, I stand at the door, and knock… (Revelation 3:20)* She had always pictured that door as a church door, or the door of a stranger's heart. She had never pictured it as a front door on a street in Northeast Washington, with a porch light that a six-year-old switched on every night. She had never pictured Jesus standing there knocking with His hand raised, as hers was raised now, after the lock had turned.

He did not leave. That was what the verse said. He stood there, and He knocked, and He kept on knocking.

On Ezra's porch she understood a little of how God felt. She had thought she knew what it was to love someone who would not open the door. She had not known anything.

She lowered her hand, and sat down on the top step, and put her face in her hands, and cried.

After a while, she heard a tapping behind her. She looked up. Mia was standing at the big front window in her fox sweatshirt, with the teddy bear tucked under one arm. She had pushed the curtain aside. She was looking out at Kambria very seriously, and she pressed her free palm to the glass.

Kambria got up and went to the window, and pressed her own hand against the other side.

They stayed that way, palm to palm through the cold glass. Then a voice called Mia's name from inside the house, low and tired, and the curtain fell back, and she was gone.

\* \* \*

When she turned around, Walt was coming across the street.

He came one step at a time, his cane in one hand, the other held out for balance, and he did not look both ways. He was wearing his slippers. Eighteen days out of Brandick, he had no business on anybody's steps. He came up the walk to the bottom of the porch steps and stopped there, winded, and looked up at her.

"Well," he said.

"I knocked," she said. Her voice broke on it.

"I saw." He held out a folded handkerchief, white and pressed. "Here. It's clean."

She came down the steps and took it, and settled on the bottom step, and he lowered himself down beside her one inch at a time, grumbling, until he was settled on the cold brick with the cane propped against his shoulder. Behind them, the porch light buzzed.

"Jeremiah was put in a pit for less," Walt said after a while. "They let him down in the mud with ropes. All he did was tell them what was coming."

Kambria laughed in spite of herself, a wet, broken laugh, and wiped her eyes with his handkerchief.

"Did he get out?"

"An Ethiopian pulled him out. Rags under his arms so the ropes wouldn't cut him." Walt looked out at the street. "Nobody in Jerusalem lifted a finger. It took a foreigner."

They sat without talking. The cold came up through the brick.

"Walt," she said. "Will you pray with me?"

He was quiet for so long that she thought he had not heard.

"You pray," he said finally. "I'll sit here."

So she prayed. She prayed out loud, on Ezra's front steps, with her hands folded around Walt's handkerchief, and Walt sat beside her with his eyes open, looking at the street. "Father, it's Kambria, on his steps again. Open that closet door. Whatever it takes, Lord, open it. Hold Mia at that window. Keep Jalen in his dorm. Keep Loretta, who has prayed for her boy every morning for years. And there's a woman he's going to see tonight. I don't know her name. You do. Keep her, too." Her voice was almost gone. "And Lord, the old man sitting beside me. You've been knocking at his door longer than I have. Walter Simmons. Amen."

Walt kept his eyes open. But when she said his name, she felt him go rigid beside her, and he stayed that way until she said amen.

A little before eight the front door opened behind them.

They both stood up. Ezra came out with his keys in his hand and his coat over his arm, and he stopped when he saw them on his steps. No one spoke. Then he walked down past them, close enough that Kambria could have touched his sleeve, and he looked at neither of them. He got into his truck, and backed out of the driveway, and drove away down the street toward Sixteenth.

Walt watched the taillights go.

"I'll walk you to your car," he said.

On the drive home, on Rhode Island Avenue, she noticed the headlights behind her. A gray Chevrolet Malibu, a few car lengths back. She would not have thought anything of it, except that it had been parked in front of Ezra's house when she came out, with the engine running, and now it was here. She turned off onto a side street without signaling. The headlights turned too. She pulled into a gas station and sat at the pump with the engine running, and the sedan rolled past without stopping, its windows too dark to see in. She watched its taillights until they were gone. Then she wrote the plate number on the back of her hand in pen, because she did not trust herself to remember it, and drove home the long way.

\* \* \*

Jalen called her at ten.

She was at the kitchen table with the lamp lit and the notebook shut. She had not been able to open it. When the phone rang and she saw his name, she was so afraid of what he might say that she almost let it go to voicemail.

"Miss Kambria?" His voice was low; she could hear a roommate's music through the wall behind him. "Mia FaceTimed me. She said you were crying on our porch."

Kambria closed her eyes. "I'm sorry, sweetheart. I didn't mean for her to see that."

"What happened?"

So she told him. She did not tell him everything his father had said. She told him that she had asked his father to open his mother's notebook, and that he had said no, and that she had sat on the steps for a while afterward with Mr. Simmons. Jalen listened without saying anything. When she was done, the line went so silent that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"He'll open it," Jalen said.

"Jalen..."

"He will. Mom said. She told me, at the end. She said he'd be ready one day, and I had to be patient with him, because he was a slow builder." He laughed a little, and it caught in his throat. "She said that. A slow builder. But he finishes everything he starts. Everything. I never saw him leave anything half done."

Kambria wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"I'm coming home Tuesday night," Jalen said. "For Thanksgiving. Grandma's doing the dressing, and Mia's making place cards." He hesitated. "Will you come Thursday? For dinner? I know how he's been. But it's my house too. Mia wants you, and I want you to come."

She could not answer.

"I'll come," she said. "I'll bring the macaroni and cheese."

"The baked one? With the crust?"

"The baked one."

"Okay." She could hear him smiling. "Okay. Thursday."

After he hung up, she sat at the table until the kettle clicked off. Then she opened the ninth notebook to the page from Tuesday night, the one with Ezra's name underlined twice at the bottom. She drew a line through *Thursday*. Underneath it she wrote Jesus' words, in pen, all the way through.

*Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me. (Revelation 3:20)*

She numbered the page in the corner and wrote the reference small in the margin, 3 and 20, the way she marked anything she would want to find again at three in the morning. And under the verse, one more line.

*I knocked once. He is still knocking.*

Below it, smaller, she wrote the things she did not want to lose by morning. *Thurs. 11/19, 8:04 p.m. Ezra left for "C." Text on his counter: "Left the light on for you." Gray Malibu on Walt's street. Then behind me. Same car. Plate is on my hand.*

She left the lamp burning in the window and went to bed.

In the small hours she woke up, and did not know why.

The house was quiet. The furnace ticked. She lay listening, then went to the front room in her bare feet and stood beside the curtain without touching it.

Across the street, under the streetlight that had been out since summer, a car was parked with its lights off. A gray sedan. Inside it, low, she could see the small blue glow of a phone screen, and then it went dark.

The numbers she had written on the back of her hand that evening had faded, but she could still read them. She held her hand up to the edge of the window, where the glow from the porch light next door came in.

They matched.

She stood in the dark beside the curtain, making no more sound than the curtain did, and prayed without a sound while the car sat there and nothing happened. Toward four its engine started. It pulled away from the curb without turning on its headlights, and went down the street, and turned the corner, and was gone.

She did not go back to bed. She kept her seat until it was light, with her back to the wall and the lamp burning in the window, and her Bible open in front of her, and the front door where she could see it.
