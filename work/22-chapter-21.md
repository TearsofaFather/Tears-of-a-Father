## Chapter 21: Perplexity

> *And there shall be signs in the sun, and in the moon, and in the stars; and upon the earth distress of nations, with perplexity; the sea and the waves roaring; Men's hearts failing them for fear, and for looking after those things which are coming on the earth: for the powers of heaven shall be shaken.* — Luke 21:25–26

**Word study: *perplexity* — ἀπορία** Greek *aporia* (Strong's G640). Literally, *no way through*: the place where the road runs out. A sailor used it for a strait he could not pass. Jesus used it for the nations on the day the world loses its way and every expert in it stands at the edge of the road with nowhere left to go.

*And Moses stretched forth his hand toward heaven; and there was a thick darkness in all the land of Egypt three days: They saw not one another, neither rose any from his place for three days: but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.* (Exodus 10:22–23)

\* \* \*

Nina woke at noon with the phone still on the pillow beside her head and the recital still playing.

It had looped all night. She lay on her side in the airport dress, which she had never taken off, and listened to the last high note ring out and stop, and then to the boy's voice begin again from the beginning, small and shaking. *I'm dedicating this to someone who isn't here right now.* She reached over and turned it off. Her eyes were swollen. Her mouth tasted of nothing but a fear that had stayed with her through her sleep.

She lay and looked at the ceiling. Outside the glass there was nothing but sky.

*MENE. MENE. TEKEL. UPHARSIN.*

In the white daylight it seemed impossible. A trick of steam and tired eyes. She had almost convinced herself of it by the time she got up and showered and packed her bag. Her flight to New York left at four-forty. Kroll had texted at nine: *Car at 1:30. Think about it on the plane. S.*

She did not reply.

\* \* \*

Dubai International at two-thirty on a Thursday afternoon was a city of glass and white light and moving walkways, and every one of the two hundred thousand people inside it seemed to be in a hurry. Nina cleared security in four minutes, because Kroll's office had put her in the lane for diplomats and first class. She bought a bottle of water for the price of a lunch in Manhattan. She walked down the long concourse toward Gate A9 under ceilings so high there were trees growing inside, real ones, olive trees in marble planters, with the desert light falling through the glass roof onto their leaves.

Trent Walker was sitting at the gate in the borrowed tuxedo trousers and a wrinkled shirt, bent forward with his head in his hands.

She almost walked past him. Then he looked up, and his face was gray, and she stopped.

"You're on the New York flight?"

"Connecting to Dulles." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I couldn't sleep. You ever have a night where you can't sleep and you don't know why?"

"Last night."

He looked at her more carefully. "Bad dream?"

She said nothing, and sat down two seats away from him with her bag on her knees. She could still see the gold letters crawling across the dark glass of the tower. She had told no one. She was not sure that saying it aloud would not make it true.

"I wasn't dreaming," Trent said. "That's the strange part. I sat on my balcony on the fortieth floor till the sky went gray, smoking, and I quit sixteen years ago. The whole night felt like a room where someone was standing behind me, waiting for me to turn around." He laughed, and it came out broken. "I called my mother before dawn, her time. She didn't pick up. She always picks up."

Nina looked at the departures board. The New York flight showed *ON TIME*. Beyond the glass wall of the concourse, on the apron, a big double-decker jet was turning toward the runway, its tail painted with the Emirates flag. The afternoon was brilliant and hot and white. The sky over the desert was the palest blue she had ever seen.

It was two-fifty-five.

"Nina," Trent said. "Whatever it was that kept you up. I think I need somebody else to say it out loud first. Will you tell me?"

She turned toward him. She opened her mouth.

She never got the chance.

\* \* \*

The glass wall began to hum.

She felt it before she heard it: a deep vibration in the soles of her feet and in the bones of her legs. The bottle of water in her hand began to shiver. On the seat beside her a paper coffee cup walked across the vinyl and fell off the edge. All along the concourse people looked up from their phones, frowning.

Then the sound came.

The sky flashed white.

Not gold, not yet. White: lightning with no cloud to carry it, so bright that it printed the whole concourse onto the inside of her eyes. In the same instant the sound arrived.

She would never be able to describe it to anyone who had not heard it, and she would never meet anyone who had not. It came from above and below at once, out of the ground and down out of the sky. It was thunder fused to a trumpet blast, one enormous crack that fell on the terminal like a hammer and went through it like a sonic boom. The glass roof over the olive trees burst. Every pane along the concourse cracked end to end, and then the whole long wall came in, sheets of it, a hail of glass that rang off the marble and the carpet and the seats. It was so loud that it beat inside her ribs, and so deep that the floor jumped under her feet.

People screamed. Somewhere a child was shrieking. A man in a business suit a few seats away fell to his knees with his hands over his ears and his mouth wide open. Nina could neither scream nor move. She sat with the bottle of water in both hands, and a fine glittering rain of glass settled in her hair, and she looked out at the apron through the place where the wall had been, because out there the sky was turning gold.

Not the sun. The sun was where it had been, white and small in the west. The gold came from everywhere else: from the whole dome of the sky at once, pouring down over the runways and the hangars and the palms along the airport road, a light so pure and fierce that the shadows under the parked jets went faint and then disappeared.

Beside her, Trent had stood up. He had gone to the glass with both palms flat against it, looking up, and he was weeping, openly. The pane under his hands blew out when the sound hit. He pitched back with a cry, and when he turned over his hands, the palms were full of glittering splinters and running red.

"Mama," he said, staring at them. "Oh, Mama. No. No."

Out on the apron, the big double-decker jet that had been turning toward the runway kept turning. It turned past the runway and onto the grass and kept rolling across the sand beside the taxiway, until its nose wheel sank and it stopped at an angle, tipped forward, its engines still screaming. No one climbed out.

And on the runway, an airliner on final approach, wheels down, a hundred feet off the concrete, simply stopped being flown. Its nose dropped. It struck the runway on the left wing and cartwheeled, and the fuel went up in one long orange flower that rolled over the taxiway and lit the faces of everyone at the broken glass. The shock of it came through the floor a heartbeat later. Black smoke stood up over the runway in a pillar.

And then the sound stopped, and the glass went on falling.

\* \* \*

The silence lasted perhaps three seconds.

Then the screaming began in earnest, and it went on and on.

And then the earth shook.

It came up out of the desert in a long roll, then a hard shudder that threw the standing off their feet. The floor of the terminal split along its seams. Ceiling panels fell. Far off, the control tower leaned and held, and the runway lights bent like grass in a wind. In a few hours the instruments would show it had run through the whole world, in Tokyo and Lima and Lisbon, through the old fault lines and the new cities that had been built across them. Walt would find the verse before the week was out: *like as ye fled from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah.* (Zechariah 14:5)

Nina would remember the next hour in pieces, out of order, as a series of pictures with no sound. A woman running down the concourse in one shoe, calling a name. A flight attendant in the Emirates uniform standing in the middle of the moving walkway, letting it carry her past the olive trees, with an empty pram in front of her. A gate agent at A11 sitting rigid at her podium with the phone to her ear, repeating the same sentence over and over in Arabic and then in English. *The captain does not answer. The captain does not answer.* A Filipino cleaner kneeling on the marble floor beside a pile of clothes, a man's suit and tie and shoes, folded where they had fallen as neatly as if he had laid them out for the morning, with a watch on top, still ticking.

She would remember Trent sitting on the carpet with his back against the glass and his phone to his ear, calling his mother again and again, and listening to it ring.

She would remember that she did not call her grandmother. She could not make her fingers do it. She sat with the phone in her lap and looked at her grandmother's name on the screen, and she knew what would happen if she pressed it. She did not press it. As long as she did not press it, it had not happened.

The departures board went blank. Then the lights in the terminal flickered and went out, all at once, and came back a minute later, dimmer, on the emergency generators. Out on the airport road, through the glass, a line of cars had stopped dead in all six lanes, some of them with their doors open and no one inside, and the cars behind them were blowing their horns. Far off, toward the city, a column of black smoke was rising straight up into the gold sky. Then another one, to the south. Then, much farther off, so far she could barely see it, a third.

She thought of the tower she had slept in. She thought of the gold letters crawling across it. *ONE WORLD. ONE ACCOUNT. NO MORE WANT.*

*In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain.*

She picked up the phone and pressed her grandmother's name.

It rang. And rang. And rang. After the eighth ring, the voicemail picked up, and her grandmother's voice filled her ear, recorded on a church-picnic afternoon years ago, cheerful and a little too loud, the way she always spoke to machines.

*"Hey, now, this is Bernice Cole! I can't come to the phone because I'm out doing the Lord's work, or I'm taking a nap. One of the two. Leave me a message, and God bless you. And Nina, if that's you, baby, eat something!"*

A laugh at the end of it. Somebody off to the side calling, *Bernice, the potato salad!* and her grandmother turning from the phone, still laughing, to answer.

Nina's hand went to her mouth.

She did not leave a message. She pressed the name again. Again. Trent, against the glass, had stopped calling his own mother; he sat with his eyes closed, listening to hers ring and ring and come back to the same laugh, the same potato salad, the same *eat something, baby*. On the twelfth try she held the phone against her ear and did not hang up. She let it play to the end, so that for ten seconds, in the middle of the screaming, somebody was laughing in her ear.

Then she put the phone face down on the carpet and folded over on herself in the plastic seat at Gate A9 with her arms around her knees, and for the first time since she was a little girl in her grandmother's kitchen, she made a sound that was not words.

\* \* \*

The world did not end that day. It only broke.

There was no fire falling from heaven. But the sound had gone around the whole earth in one stroke, and it had found the glass on every continent.

Then the wind. Then the ground. Then the sea.

Along every coast, the water went out first. Fishermen in Chittagong and Valparaíso stood on dry harbor mud among their tipped boats and watched it go, and then watched it come back as a wall. In Dubai it came up over the corniche and into the lobbies of the towers. In Rio it took the beach and the avenue behind it. The tide gauges on four continents recorded readings nobody had a column for, and then stopped recording.

Planes fell, the ones whose pilots were gone, and some whose pilots were not, because the boom had blown out windscreens at thirty thousand feet and a wind with no direction had thrown them like paper. No one would ever know how many. The systems that counted them had failed, and some of the people who ran those systems were gone. Trains ran on through red signals until something stopped them. Cars rolled through intersections with no one at the wheel, in Mumbai and Lima and Los Angeles, into other cars, into storefronts, into crowds. In hospitals on every continent, surgeons looked up from the table to find the anesthetist gone and the patient still breathing.

And in every city, people died with no wound on them at all. Men in good suits sat down on the curb and did not stand up again. A cardiologist in Chicago would later count the cardiac arrests in the first six hours and stop counting. Their hearts had simply failed.

Ezra would find the words for it months later, reading to the barns by candlelight, and his voice would shake on them.

*And there shall be signs in the sun, and in the moon, and in the stars; and upon the earth distress of nations, with perplexity; the sea and the waves roaring; Men's hearts failing them for fear, and for looking after those things which are coming on the earth: for the powers of heaven shall be shaken.* (Luke 21:25–26)

Nurses stepped into maternity wards that had been full an hour before and found the cribs empty, every one, row after row of little blankets folded over nothing.

All the children were gone. That was the thing no one could say out loud for the first two days, and then the thing no one could stop saying. Not some of the children. All of them, everywhere, of every nation and every faith and none, down to the youngest, the ones not yet born. Women who had been pregnant that morning walked into emergency rooms in the afternoon with their hands on their flat stomachs, unable to speak.

And the believers were gone. Not all who had called themselves Christians. Nina would learn that later, too. But the ones who had known Him. Her grandmother. Trent's mother. Grandmothers and garbage men and surgeons and a scientist in a locked federal cell, and a young cellist who had played a song with no name for someone who was not there.

By dark on Thursday the power grid of the United Arab Emirates was running at forty percent. By Friday morning the internet in most of the world was a trickle, routed through the satellites that were still in the sky. By Friday night half of those were gone too. The ones that stayed up were Halvard's. They had been built to ride out a solar storm, and they talked to the ground through stations under the Greenland ice, and by Saturday they were nearly the only voice left in the sky.

They came down over three nights, in long white streaks across the stars, whole constellations of them, so many that people in the desert outside Dubai, where the city lights had failed, said it looked as though the sky itself was falling. Nina watched it from the roof of the airport hotel, where the stranded had been herded on the first night, sitting on a folded blanket beside Trent, who had not said ten words since the gate. They watched the satellites fall for an hour without speaking. Then Trent said, barely above a breath, looking up:

"The stars are falling." He looked away from the sky. "My mother used to read me a verse about this. I thought it was the dumbest thing I'd ever heard."

"Trent," Nina said. "What happened to them?"

He kept his eyes on the sky.

"He came for them," he said. "Just like she said. Just like everybody said, for two thousand years, and nobody believed it." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "And we're the ones who are left."

\* \* \*

By Saturday the world had its explanations.

The only channel that still came in clean was the Organization for World Peace, beamed from Geneva on Halvard's satellites, in nine languages, around the clock. In the hotel lobby, a hundred stranded travelers watched a panel at a long white table: a physicist from Zurich, a former head of an American space agency, a London psychologist who specialized in what she called mass trauma. Electromagnetic phenomena, the physicist said. Unexplained signals over the poles, said the space man. Then the psychologist leaned in, choosing her words as carefully as a woman stepping onto ice.

"The international community has to consider that these people were removed. By an intelligence that is not of this world."

Trent laughed. It cracked out of him at the back of the crowd, loud enough that heads turned. "Not of this world," he said to Nina, low. "First true thing anybody's said on TV all week." And he walked out.

\* \* \*

Then Adrian Crowe came on.

He was not in Dubai. He had left the tower on Thursday night by helicopter, and no one knew where he was. He spoke from a plain room with a gray wall behind him and no flags, tired and grave and calm, and Nina, watching in the hotel lobby among three hundred people standing silent, saw every one of them lean toward the screen.

"I will not tell you that I understand what happened on Thursday," he said. "No one understands it. Anyone who tells you they do is lying to you, or selling you something." He paused. "But I will tell you what I know. I know that the world did not end. I know that you are alive, and so am I. And I know that we are going to need each other now more than we have ever needed anyone."

He leaned toward the camera.

"The governments you elected cannot help you. Many of them have lost their leaders. All of them have lost their systems. The International Collective met last night in emergency session, and I will tell you plainly what happened: they argued for six hours and voted on nothing." He waited. "The Organization for World Peace was built for this. We built a ledger for every person on earth so that no one would ever go hungry because a bank failed or a currency collapsed. We built networks that can see where the food is and where the medicine is and where the people are who need it. As of this morning, at the request of the High Council of the Technate and with the consent of forty-one nations, the Organization has assumed emergency authority over the distribution of food, fuel and medicine in the regions that have asked for our help."

A murmur went through the lobby. The man beside Nina let out his breath and crossed himself.

"I am asking for one thing," Crowe said. "Order. Not obedience. Order. Stay in your homes. Register at the nearest distribution point when it opens. Your hand is your account, and your account will feed you. We will get through this together." His voice softened. "Peace. Order. Safety. That is my promise to you. I intend to keep it."

The screen went to the Organization's emblem, a gold circle on white.

Around Nina, three hundred stranded strangers began to applaud, a few hands first, then the whole room.

She stood among them, silent. At the left edge of the picture, in the last second before the screen changed, she saw a hand on the back of Crowe's chair, a thin hand in a gray sleeve, with a red string at the wrist.

\* \* \*

On the far side of the world, in a basement north of Baltimore, a man sat in the dark with a dead boy's tablet in his hands.

Michael had not slept in his own bed since it happened. Upstairs, nothing moved. His father's chair stood empty at the head of the basement stairs, with the old man's reading glasses folded on the arm; he had been gone since the twenty-sixth, like the rest. Daniel's bicycle still lay in the front yard, because Michael could not put it in the shed and could not stand to look at it. On the third morning he had walked out at dawn and watched the front wheel, which had finally stopped, and put out one finger and set it spinning again. It turned for twenty-three seconds. Then he had gone inside and put his fist through a cabinet door.

His knuckles split on the hinge. He wrapped them in a dish towel and left the cabinet hanging.

At the far end of the basement, under a sheet, sat the mixing board his father had built the winter Michael turned nine. Daniel had put his own hands on it at five. Michael pulled the sheet off. The old speakers waited, and a milk crate of discs he had cut in this room over thirty years. He did not touch the crate. He did not trust what it would do to him.

He picked up the tablet instead. It had been in the boy's backpack, on the hook, the pencil case still unzipped. He woke the screen with a thumb, and it opened where Daniel had left it: a video, paused. A kid in a white suit, a cello, a high school stage.

*Untitled.*

Michael plugged the tablet into the board, because it was the only way he knew to listen, and brought up the faders, and pressed play.

The cello came into the cinder-block room.

It was a boy's playing, and Michael heard that at once. A line that climbed and held and fell, and sounded like someone trying to say a thing he could not say. Twelve years old, and Daniel had kept this open in the dark of his room and never said one word about it. Michael had never asked what he listened to. Most nights he came home after the boy was asleep.

Then, because the hands remembered what the head would not, he began to listen the other way. The room tone, soft and warm. The foam on the walls. A single mic, placed a hand too close to the F-hole, pulled back at the climb so the low notes would not bloom. Someone had set that mic with care. Someone who knew that room and knew that kid and had stood at the back during sound check, adjusting it by a finger's width, because it mattered to him how his son was heard.

Somebody loved that boy, Michael thought.

The video ended. In the pause before the next one, he heard his own breath, loud and ragged, and found that his face was wet.

He wrote *Untitled* on a scrap in his father's square capitals and folded it into his wallet. Then he went upstairs and, for the first time since the twenty-sixth, lay down in his own bed.

\* \* \*

On Monday a car came for her.

She was sitting on the roof again at dawn, where she had slept for two nights because the hotel rooms were full of the injured, when a man in a dark suit came up the stairs and said her name. Mr. Kroll had arranged a plane. Mr. Vale had approved it. The airspace over the Atlantic had been opened for Organization traffic only, and there were two seats left on the flight to New York.

"Two," she said.

The man looked at his phone. "For you and Mr. Walker."

It was a private jet, long and white, with the gold circle of the Organization painted on its tail. There were fourteen people on it. Kroll was not one of them; he had flown home on Friday. Trent sat by himself in the back with his hands folded between his knees and looked out the window the whole way. Nina sat across from a German finance minister who had been at the gala and now did not seem to remember her face.

Over the middle of the ocean, the thin man in the gray suit came down the aisle from the front cabin and took the empty seat across the table from her.

He did not introduce himself. Up close, Alexander Vale was younger than she had thought, perhaps in his middle forties, with a narrow, pale, intelligent face and eyes so dark she could not see where the pupils ended. He set a glass of water on the table in front of him, as he had at the gala, and did not drink it. On his left wrist, under the cuff, the red string was knotted three times.

"Dr. Cole," he said. His voice was low and exact. "I am sorry for your loss."

She stared at him. "How do you know I lost anyone?"

"Everyone lost someone," Vale said. "Some of us more than others." He turned the glass a quarter turn on the table. "Sebastian tells me he made you an offer on Wednesday night. I would like to make it again, because the circumstances have changed. Before Thursday it was a project. Now it is a necessity." He looked at her steadily. "The world is frightened, Dr. Cole. Frightened people need a voice they can trust. A voice that does not panic and does not lie and does not die. You know better than anyone alive what can be built now. I would like you to build it."

"Whose voice?"

Vale smiled.

"You saw the broadcast on Saturday," he said. "You saw how they listened." He stood up and buttoned his jacket. "Take what time you need. A week. I will be in New York." He paused. "Your grandmother's building in Baltimore is still standing. I made sure of it. You should go and see her apartment before you decide anything."

He went back to the front cabin.

Nina sat with her hands on the table. She had no memory of giving anyone her grandmother's address. She had never told Kroll, or anyone at the company, where her grandmother lived.

She turned and looked toward the back of the plane. Trent was watching her. He had seen the whole thing. He said nothing. He shook his head once.

\* \* \*

She took the train to Baltimore on Wednesday, because the trains were running again on the Northeast Corridor, at a crawl, with soldiers in Organization armbands standing at every station.

The city was quiet. Too quiet, with half the traffic lights dark and a smell of smoke over everything and hand-lettered signs taped to lampposts on every block: *HAVE YOU SEEN*, and a photograph, and a name. Hundreds of them. She stopped reading them after the first three. She walked from Penn Station to her grandmother's building on Druid Hill Avenue because there were no cabs, and she climbed the four flights because the elevator had stopped, and she let herself into the apartment with the key she had carried on her ring since college and never used.

The apartment smelled of burnt sugar.

On the stove, in the big dented pot, the sweet potatoes had boiled dry and scorched black. Someone, a neighbor, had turned off the burner. The kitchen window was open a crack. On the table, her grandmother's old black Bible lay open beside a cup of coffee gone to scum, and beside the Bible were her reading glasses, and a pair of house slippers sat under the chair, side by side, where her feet had been, and her cardigan lay over the seat, still holding the shape of her shoulders.

Nina stood in the doorway.

Then she went to the bookshelf in the living room. The coffee-can robot was there, as her grandmother had said: a Maxwell House can with two bottle-cap eyes and pipe-cleaner arms and a battery pack held on with electrical tape, standing next to a photograph of her father in his Army uniform. She picked it up. It weighed almost nothing. She held it against her chest.

She sat down at her grandmother's dinner table, in her grandmother's chair, and looked at the open Bible.

It was open to Joel. One verse was underlined twice in blue ink, with a date written beside it in her grandmother's shaky hand: *For Nina. 11/25.* The day before. The day Nina had called her from Dubai.

*And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered.*

She sat there until the light went out of the window.

\* \* \*

It was dark when she finally took out her phone. There was one bar of signal, and the battery was at nine percent. She opened the video because it was the last thing she had heard before the horn, and she wanted a sound from before.

The counter under it said *2,114,806,331 views*.

She read the number twice. Two billion. In six days. She scrolled down. There were comments in Spanish and Korean and Arabic and Portuguese and languages she could not read, thousands of them, millions, scrolling faster than the screen could load. The few in English said the same things over and over. *He's gone. The cellist is gone. They said on the news he was taken.* And: *I played this the night it happened and I couldn't stop.* And: *I don't know why this is the only thing that helps.* And, again and again, in every language, one question.

*Who was it for?*

*Who was the someone who isn't here?*

On the screen, the boy in the white suit leaned toward the microphone in a high school auditorium in Washington, D.C., and the camera drifted, unsteady, down to the third row, to a seat on the aisle with a program lying on it. Nina had watched the video thirty times. She had never noticed the program. Now she leaned close to the dim little screen and saw, written across the top of it in black marker, three letters.

*DAD.*

She opened Juno, with seven percent battery left, in her dead grandmother's kitchen in the dark.

"Juno," she said. "The cellist in the *Untitled* video. Find out who his father is."

"Searching," said the warm, unhurried voice, and then: "The performer has been identified in press reports as Jalen Patterson, eighteen, of Washington, D.C., reported missing on November twenty-sixth. His mother, Angela Patterson, died a year ago. His father is listed in public records as Ezra Patterson, a carpenter, of Northeast Washington."

Nina went cold.

*I apprenticed under a carpenter in Northeast for six years. Best carpenter in DC. Ezra Patterson. Never heard of him, I bet.*

"Is he alive?" she whispered. "The father. Is Ezra Patterson on any list?"

"He is not listed among the missing," Juno said.

The phone went dark in her hand. The battery was gone.

Nina Cole sat in the dark of her grandmother's kitchen in Baltimore with the coffee-can robot in her lap, and knew that the father the whole world was asking after was still here.
