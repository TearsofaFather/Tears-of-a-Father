## Chapter 1: The Dinner Table

> *By faith Noah, being warned of God of things not seen as yet, moved with fear, prepared an ark to the saving of his house.* — Hebrews 11:7

**Word study: *warned of God* — χρηματίζω** Greek *chrēmatizō* (Strong's G5537). To give a divine answer, to be instructed by God. The word was used for an official reply from a king or a court. Noah's warning was not a hunch or a feeling. It was a word from the throne.

On summer evenings the neighbors brought their wine out to the hill to watch the old man work. Gopher-wood beams as long as a street. Pitch boiling in pots. His sons hauling timber while the town's young men called up to them, laughing. "When's the rain coming, Noah?" Nobody on that hill had ever seen the kind of rain he was building for. Noah wiped his forehead, picked up his hammer, and went back to work. A hundred and twenty years is a long time to be laughed at. (Genesis 6:3, 14–22)

\* \* \*


*Long before the trumpet.*

Ezra Patterson grew up in Cedar Hollow, Maryland, twenty minutes past the District line, where the county road gives up and turns to gravel.

There was no stoplight. There was a general store with one gas pump that quit in 1986 and was never fixed. The movie theater was forty minutes north, and they never went. Eggs came from the coop behind the house on Old Ferry Road. If you wanted a toy, you built it: a go-cart from a lawnmower engine and a pallet, a raft from oil drums, a tree fort with a trapdoor and a rope. They didn't have much money. By the time Ezra was ten he could frame a doorway plumb with a bob he made himself from a creek stone and a length of twine.

Calvin Patterson threw switches at the rail yard in Hollis Junction six days a week. On the seventh he wore a gray suit to Pilgrim Rest and stood at the deacons' table with one hand on the offering plate. He shook every hand in the vestibule. He knew the names of everybody's grandchildren. When it rained he carried old Mrs. Tolliver's casserole dish out to her car, and the church women told his mother, Loretta, she was a blessed woman.

At home he took the suit off and hung it on the back of the bedroom door, and the house went quiet. Supper was at six, and nobody talked. When his boots hit the hall floor, the television went off. After Loretta went to bed, the bottle came down from the cabinet over the refrigerator, and some nights it never went back up. Nobody at Pilgrim Rest ever saw the hole in the hallway wall, because Loretta hung a calendar over it.

Ezra learned two things in that house. He learned that God was a voice a man used in public. And he learned to sit very still, so the voice wouldn't find him.

His mother kept going to Pilgrim Rest after his father was gone. Ezra stopped the day he was old enough to drive himself somewhere else.

At eighteen he caught a ride into the city with a framing crew and didn't go back. He was good. He was fast and careful and didn't talk much, and foremen liked all three. Elijah Brooks, from the next house down Old Ferry Road, the loudest boy in Cedar Hollow, followed him in a year later and couldn't hang a door straight if you held the level for him. Ezra got him hired anyway. They had been friends since the worst night of Ezra's life, when he was eight years old.

\* \* \*

He met Michelle at twenty-three, on a job in Silver Spring where she was the one telling the contractors they were behind. She came from a town like his, with one gas pump and a church at the crossroads. She didn't talk much either. She worked the way he did: early, quiet, and all the way to the end. The first time she laughed at something he said, he went home and lay awake half the night.

They married at twenty-five in her mother's backyard. Jalen came the next spring, eight pounds with a full head of hair and long fingers that wrapped around Ezra's thumb and would not let go.

Then the firm made her a project manager, and then a regional one. She was good, she wanted more of it, and he was proud of her. She was in Richmond three nights a week, then four. She called at bedtime, then texted, then forgot. They stopped arguing, because arguing took two people in the same room. By the summer Jalen turned three, they were two quiet people who were very good at their jobs, handing a little boy back and forth at the door.

It was Elijah who dragged them both to the house party in Brookland, that last summer, the summer of 2011. A narrow clapboard house full of people and music. A cooler of ice in the bathtub. Michelle had driven up from Richmond that afternoon and spent the party in the kitchen on her phone. Ezra stood by the back door with a cup he wasn't drinking.

That was where he first saw Angela.

She had come with two girlfriends and she was standing in the middle of the living room talking with her hands. She had a pencil skirt and a cardigan, and she held herself like a woman who had not slouched at a dinner table since grade school, and still every man in that room knew exactly where she was. She caught him looking. She looked back. She didn't blink first, and neither did he.

He couldn't go to her. He had a ring on.

So he listened instead. He stood by the back door and listened to her talk to a man about why third graders are the best age to teach. *Because they still believe you, and they're finally old enough to argue.* She was a teacher. She had a master's degree she was still paying off. She talked about a boy in her class who couldn't read yet, and she talked about him the way other people talked about someone they loved. She laughed with her whole face. The smile started on one side and took its time getting to the other.

He drove home with Michelle, who answered work email the whole way and didn't speak to him in the truck. He did not see the woman in the cardigan again. He told himself he had not been looking for her.

By August, Michelle was gone. The firm offered her Atlanta, and she took it. She left a note on the kitchen counter, and she did not take Jalen. *He needs one of us who's home,* the note said. *That was always going to be you.*

\* \* \*

The Montgomery County Fair, in Gaithersburg, the third week of August. Ninety-four degrees. Ezra brought Jalen, who was three, because the boy had stopped asking where Mommy was and started asking nothing at all, and Ezra wanted one day that would take both their minds off it and bring the boy a little happiness.

They rode the carousel four times. They looked at a pig the size of a car. Jalen fell asleep on his shoulder in the poultry barn, and Ezra stood in the dusty aisle between the cages, holding his sleeping son, and thought, *This is it. This is the whole rest of my life. I'll never leave his side.*

When he came out of the barn, she was standing in line at the fried-dough truck.

Same smile. Sundress this time, and a straw hat, and two girlfriends on either side of her, both of them talking at once. She saw him. She saw the sleeping boy. She looked at his left hand, and he watched her see that the ring was gone, and he watched her decide not to ask.

"You were at the party in Brookland," she said. "By the back door. You didn't say one word all night."

"I was listening."

"To what?"

"You. Talking about the boy who couldn't read."

She held his eyes and said nothing. Then she turned back to the window and said, "One more," and when the paper plate came, she put it in his free hand. A slab of fried dough the size of a hubcap, hot, slick, buried under powdered sugar.

"No," he said. "Let me." He fished out his wallet one-handed. Jalen woke up, saw the plate, and went for it with both fists, and sugar went down the front of Ezra's shirt and all over the boy's face. Angela laughed with her whole face.

\* \* \*

She was from Erie, Pennsylvania, up on the lake, where the snow comes in sideways off the water from November to April and nobody thinks anything of it. Her father, Joe, was a retired Navy captain who had commanded a destroyer out of Norfolk and still made his bed every morning with hospital corners. Her mother, Pat, ran the church kitchen and every bake sale within twenty miles. They had raised two children, Angela and her older brother, on schedule, on Scripture, and on the strict understanding that you finished what you started, you said what you meant, and you were home by ten.

They drove down the first Thanksgiving. Joe walked through Ezra's one-bedroom apartment the way he had once walked a ship at inspection, ran his thumbnail along the seam of a bookshelf Ezra had built, and said nothing. Pat brought a plate of chocolate cupcakes with sprinkles and stuck one candle in the middle of them, though it was nobody's birthday. "We do one candle in our house," she said. "One candle, one wish, and you share the rest." She made Jalen blow it out. She made him do it twice.

That night, in the parking lot, Joe stood by his car with his keys in his hand and looked at Ezra for a long, plain moment.

"She prays for you," he said. "You know that."

Ezra found no answer to that, so he gave none. Joe nodded once, as if that told him something, and got in the car.

\* \* \*

They married in June, in Angela's church, a small brick church in Northeast with a basement fellowship hall. Jalen, four, carried the rings on a pillow and dropped it twice. Pat cried through the vows. Joe walked his daughter down the aisle with his jaw set, and when he put her hand in Ezra's, he held on a second longer than he had to.

Ezra's father had been in the ground at Arlington for twenty-one years. Ezra looked out at the pews anyway, once, and hated himself for looking.

That fall a gutted rowhouse came up on Twelfth Street in Brookland: no windows, no wiring, a roof you could see the sky through, and behind it a long, deep lot gone wild with sumac and broken glass. Nobody wanted it. Ezra sold his truck and his good table saw to make the down payment and rode the bus to job sites for a year.

Jesus had a parable for it: *Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto treasure hid in a field; the which when a man hath found, he hideth, and for joy thereof goeth and selleth all that he hath, and buyeth that field.* (Matthew 13:44)

He rebuilt it with his own hands. Every joist, every stair tread, every door. Angela painted. Jalen hammered nails into a scrap of two-by-four in the backyard, crooked, all day, perfectly happy.

One Saturday that first spring, Ezra was in the back lot with a shovel, digging footings for an addition, down in red clay to his knees, filthy and soaked through. Angela came out with two glasses of iced tea and sat on the back step and watched him.

He stopped and leaned on the shovel.

"You know you could have done better," he said. He kept his eyes off her. "Teacher. Master's degree. Joe's daughter. You could have had a lawyer. Somebody who didn't come with all this."

Angela set down the tea and walked out into the mud in her good sneakers and stood in front of him.

"Ezra," she said. "Look where you're standing." He looked down: clay, roots, a broken bottle, a rusted hinge. "That's what a field looks like before you find what's in it. You have to dig through the dirt to get to the treasure." She wiped a streak of clay off his jaw with her thumb. "I'm not going anywhere. Keep digging."

\* \* \*

Those were the good years.

The house came together a room at a time. Jalen grew long and quiet and found a cello at a church rummage sale when he was nine and would not put it down. Ezra poured a slab at the end of the yard and framed him a studio, and the boy played out there every night with the windows open. Angela taught third grade four blocks away. Walt Simmons, across the street, read his newspaper on the porch every morning and never once waved first.

In the summer of 2020 Mia was born, small and loud and furious, with a full head of curls. Pat came down for two weeks and cooked for an army. Joe held his granddaughter on the back step for an hour and didn't say a word. When he handed her back to Ezra, his eyes were wet, and he pretended they weren't.

Saturdays they went to the park on Bunker Hill Road. Ezra pushed Mia on the swings until his arms ached. Angela read on a blanket. Jalen practiced scales under a tree, and strangers stopped to listen. Every August they went back to the fair for one fried dough and split it, and she always took the half with more sugar.

At supper Angela bowed her head over the food and rested her left hand open on the cloth beside his plate. Jalen took her other one. Ezra kept his on his fork. She never said a word about it, and she never stopped leaving it there.

\* \* \*

Angela had always believed. But in the spring Jalen turned ten, something in her went deeper. She started a Thursday-night women's study in the basement fellowship hall of the church where they were married. Fourteen women the first week. She came home from it talking faster than he had heard her talk since college. She bought spiral notebooks in four colors and a Strong's Concordance so thick it needed both hands. She read at the dinner table after Jalen was in bed, a pen in her teeth, drawing boxes around lines.

On the third Thursday, a young biochemist named Kambria came in late, in a lab coat, and sat in the back.

She had known her Bible since she was a girl. She could quote it. But she had never been taught how to *study* it, how to take a single word down to its root, how to keep a notebook so that ten years of reading added up to something. Angela showed her. Kambria went at it like a child let loose in a library. She came back the next week with three pages of questions. The week after, six. By the third spring, when the study had shrunk to the two of them and moved into Angela's kitchen, Kambria was there every Thursday at a quarter to seven, and Angela had a sister she had never had to share a bathroom with.

Jesus had said it plainly: *Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven.* (Matthew 18:3)

Ezra came home on Thursdays to two women at his kitchen table with their heads together over the Hebrew, and something in him went cold and hard.

He couldn't have said why. He started taking more jobs. He started stopping at Rooney's with Elijah on the way home, then staying. He started complaining: about money, about the roof, about the noise from the studio, about pierogies three nights a week. He stopped noticing the clean shirts in his drawer and the plate kept warm in the oven. When Angela turned to him in bed, more and more often he was already asleep, or pretending.

She never stopped praying for him. That was the worst of it. Sometimes he came down for water at two in the morning and found her at the kitchen table with her eyes closed and her lips moving and his name in it.

When she prayed out loud, he heard Pilgrim Rest. That was the thing he could never explain to her. It was not her voice. It was the words. The *Lord, we just* and the *in Jesus' name*, the holy words his father had shouted from the deacons' table on Sunday morning and then carried home in a bottle on Friday night. Every time she bowed her head, Ezra was six years old again in the front pew at Pilgrim Rest, sitting very still, so the voice wouldn't find him.

On Mia's third birthday, Joe and Pat drove down. Pat brought the cupcakes, chocolate, with sprinkles, one candle. The family stood around the kitchen table, and before Mia could blow, Angela touched Ezra's arm.

"Would you bless it?" she said quietly. "Just this once. For her."

The kitchen went still. Joe was watching him. Pat was studying the cupcakes. Mia had her eyes on the candle, which was burning down.

Ezra heard the deacons' table. He heard the bottle. He heard *you're just like him.*

"You do it," he said. "You're the one who's good at it."

He said it harder than he meant to. Angela's face didn't change, but something behind it closed. She bowed her head and said the blessing herself.

Mia blew out the candle.

Joe found Ezra on the back step afterward, in the dark, and stood beside him with his hands in his pockets.

"I had three hundred sailors under me," Joe said. "I know what it looks like when a man's angry at somebody who isn't in the room." He waited. Ezra said nothing. Joe nodded once, as he had that first Thanksgiving in the parking lot, and went inside.

\* \* \*

*Two years before the trumpet.*

Across the city from the Pattersons' house, in a government building with no windows, Angela's closest friend spent her days at a lab bench. Her name was Kambria Whitfield.

The morning the newspaper announced that someone had found Noah's ark, Kambria read the headline in the lab's break room and laughed out loud. Then she read the second paragraph, and she stopped laughing. She cut the page out with the lab's good scissors and carried it in her coat pocket the rest of the day.

Such days were what her work was made of. She was a biochemist with the Centers for Disease Control, and when people got sick and nobody knew why, the samples came to her bench, and she traced the germ back to where it started: the farm, the plant, the water main, the one bad shipment out of ten thousand good ones. That week it was two children in a hospital in Ohio and a bagged-lettuce plant in California, tied together by nothing but a fingerprint of bacterial DNA.

One thing did not fit. A single isolate from Ohio carried a stretch of thirty-odd bases in a region where nothing was supposed to be written at all, a patch of sequence that matched nothing in any database she could reach. It was too orderly to be damage and too short to be anything a germ would keep for itself. The confirmation run had come back from Verity Health Labs in Rockville, the private bench the agency used for its overflow, stamped clean. She logged the patch as probable contamination, because that was the honest category for a thing she could not explain. Then she exported the run to her cloud drive and sent it to Francesca Moretti, who had worked two benches down from her for years before she moved up to New York, and asked for a second opinion.

At a quarter to seven she turned onto Twelfth Street in Brookland, in Northeast Washington, still in her gray slacks and flat shoes, her agency badge flipped backward on its lanyard. Thursday evenings in that neighborhood had a sound of their own. Somewhere on Otis Street a speaker sat on a porch rail, sending a Christian hip-hop song out over the rooftops, and from Rhode Island Avenue the bell at Mount Zion began to ring the hour. Kambria caught her head nodding before she had decided to let it. The old porch-front houses, brick below and painted wood above, stood shoulder to shoulder behind small, fierce gardens, and two doors down from the Pattersons' house Mr. Odell sat in his lawn chair, as he had every warm evening since before Kambria knew the street. He lifted two fingers from the armrest as she parked. She lifted two back.

Angela Patterson's house was the brick one with the crooked porch light and the front step that Ezra had been promising to rebuild for six summers. Nobody knocked there. Kambria went in through the side door, into a kitchen that was all steam and noise: Swedish meatballs simmering in cream gravy on the back burner, loaded potato pierogies browning in butter in the skillet, a small pot of lingonberry jam warming beside them. In the middle of it stood Angela, the closest thing Kambria had to a sister, a dish towel over one shoulder and a wooden spoon in her hand, conducting.

"You look like you haven't eaten since Monday," Angela said, without turning around. "Sit. You will not read the Word on an empty stomach in my house."

"I ate."

"A granola bar in a parking lot is not eating."

At the table, Angela's four-year-old daughter, Mia, knelt on a chair with her hair in two puffs tied up in yellow ribbons, working a purple crayon across a coloring page with the focus of a lab tech plating cultures. "Miss Kambria! I know my verse!"

"Let's hear it."

"Jesus wept." She said it proudly, and at a great volume.

"It's the shortest one in the whole Bible," Angela said, "and she has decided that is the one she's keeping."

The screen door slapped, and Jalen came in from the backyard: sixteen years old, lanky legs, unkempt hair, and glasses, with headphones around his neck and calluses on the fingertips of his left hand from hours with the cello. He kissed his mother on the cheek, stole a pierogi straight out of the skillet, and dodged the spoon.

"Hey, Miss Kam."

"Hey, maestro."

"Is Dad home?" He asked it of the skillet instead of his mother.

"He's finishing a job in Takoma," Angela said. "He said he would try."

"Cool," Jalen said. He carried a plate piled high back out to the little studio at the end of the yard, and the screen door had barely finished its second slap when the cello began to play.

They ate standing at the counter, as Thursdays always went, and Kambria noticed that Angela, who had fixed three plates, only moved the food around on her own. She noticed, too, that Angela's left hand drifted to her side and away again, twice. Kambria filed it where she filed everything. Then Angela asked about the lab, and Kambria told her about the lettuce, and the moment passed.

Kambria had been at Angela's table every Thursday since the study shrank to two.

Now the plates were rinsed and stacked, Mia had been carried up to bed with a cup of water and three promises, and the two women sat across from each other as they had every Thursday night for years: two cups of coffee going cold, two worn King James Bibles, and a dozen spiral notebooks spread out between them.

Each notebook had a color. Blue for Israel. Red, the one she had labeled *Of the Nations*. Green for money. Gold, the thinnest one, for the church.

Kambria dated everything, in small square letters, each one standing apart from the next. Angela kept her own habit. On every page she drew a box around one line, in whatever pen was nearest, the line she would look for first if she lost all the rest.

"Why that one?" Kambria had asked her once, years ago, tapping a box with her pen.

"Because if the house burned down and I got one line back," Angela said, "I want it to be that one."

Between the two Bibles sat Angela's Strong's Concordance, two thousand pages thick, its spine split and mended with packing tape. Every Thursday started the same way. One verse. One word in it. Then down to the root, in the Hebrew or the Greek, until they knew what that word had meant to the people who first heard it.

Tonight the word was *watch*. Angela had her finger on it in Matthew 24, and Kambria was running down the columns in the back of the concordance, past thousands of numbers, to the Greek.

"Here. Eleven twenty-seven." Kambria sounded it out. "*Grēgoreuō. γρηγορεύω.* To keep awake. To be vigilant." She read the next line twice. "It comes from a word that means to rouse up. To wake somebody who's asleep."

Angela sat back. "So it doesn't mean look out the window."

"No. It means don't fall asleep."

Angela wrote it in pen in the margin of her Bible, small and neat, beside the verse: *1127. Stay awake.* Then she glanced through the doorway at the couch in the living room, where her husband fell asleep in front of the television most nights, and said nothing.

"What did you bring?" Angela said.

Kambria pulled a newspaper clipping from the back of the red notebook and smoothed it out with her palm. Four wars on three continents, all of them flaring in the same month. She had clipped one like it on Monday, and another the week before that.

She read it aloud to Angela. Then she laid her pen across the open notebook and sat back, silent. Every week she would find something in the newspaper, and then she would find it in the Bible, and the two would line up so closely that it frightened her. Wars and rumours of wars. Nation against nation, kingdom against kingdom. She had been reading those words her whole life. She had never expected to watch them come up off the page and onto the front of the paper.

"Angie," she said, barely above a whisper. "It's all in here. All of it. Just like He said it would be." She pressed her fingertips against her closed eyes. "Some nights it's more than I can hold."

Angela flipped through her Bible and found Jesus' answer in Matthew 24 quickly. "'For nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom: and there shall be famines, and pestilences, and earthquakes, in divers places.'" She tapped the next line with one finger. "'All these are the beginning of sorrows.'"

"The beginning," Kambria said. "That's what keeps me up. It's not the end yet, but it's definitely intensifying." She slid the clipping into the red notebook and dated it in the corner. "It's like He's turning the pages faster, Angie. Ten years ago I'd find one of these articles a month. Now I see it every day."

Kambria took the ark clipping from her coat pocket. "And one more." She laid it on the table. It was from that morning's paper. It showed a long, boat-shaped hill on a mountainside in eastern Turkey, snow above it. The headline said NOAH'S ARK FOUND, and under it, in smaller type, *Scientists confirm structure in Ararat region is man-made.*

Angela read it twice. Then she turned back to His words in Matthew 24 and ran her finger down the page. "'But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.'" She looked up. "They were eating and drinking, and marrying, right up to the day Noah went into the ark. And they knew not until the flood came and took them all away." She set her hand on the photograph. "He's showing them the boat, Kam. Right before."

Kambria dated the clipping and slid it into the red notebook with the others.

On the windowsill over the sink, Angela's little radio went on talking under the clink of the coffee cups. Kambria half-listened to the announcer: a recall of bagged romaine lettuce in fourteen states, E. coli, two children in the hospital in Ohio. Then a report on the drought in the West, the land gone brown from Nevada to Texas, and Lake Mead lower than it had been since the dam was built. She wrote both of them down in the margin of the red notebook, small, under the date. She was studious, and she remembered to write it down, the way any thorough scientist would. *Famines, and pestilences.* Then she looked through the doorway at the empty couch, the dent in the cushion where a tired man slept through the news most nights, and the word they had just learned came back to her. *Stay awake.*

The back door banged open.

Ezra trudged in with muddy work boots, a dusty tool belt, and drywall dust in his hair. He was a big man, broad in the shoulders, with hands rough as unplaned oak.

He kicked off his boots, glanced past the table where the ladies studied, and started toward the fridge. "Well," he said. "I can see the doomsday club is in session."

"Hi, honey." Angela tipped her face up, and he kissed her forehead, and for one second the teasing went out of his eyes. It always did with her.

Then he looked at Kambria. "Let me guess. World's ending Tuesday."

"Nobody knows the day or the hour," Kambria said, without looking up at him.

"Then why do y'all keep writing this stuff down?"

"Because He told us to watch," Angela said. "So His coming won't catch us by surprise." She never argued with him about it. She just kept inviting him, even though she had stopped expecting him to say yes.

Ezra opened the refrigerator, took out a beer, and let the door swing shut harder than it needed to. From the studio came the long, low groan of a cello: a single note, held until it trembled, then sliding up into what sounded almost like grief.

Through the window over the sink he could see it: the little studio across the grass, its windows glowing amber in the dark. He had poured the slab for it himself five summers ago and framed every wall, with Angela holding the level and an eleven-year-old Jalen at his elbow, handing him nails and asking a hundred questions about construction. He had taught his son to frame a wall, hang a door, and finish the trim on the studio that summer, and they had grown close doing it. When it was finished, they flipped the breaker together and stood in the doorway, grinning while every light came on at once.

Ezra looked at the glowing windows for one second. Then he looked away.

"He's still at that thing?"

"That thing," Angela said, "got him a scholarship audition."

Lionel Hart had been Jalen's teacher since the boy was nine years old. He had played viola with the Capital Philharmonic for thirty-one years, and there was no string instrument he couldn't teach. The first time he heard Jalen sawing away at that rummage-sale cello in the church basement, he walked over, fixed Jalen's bow hold without a word, and told Angela he would teach him for nothing. He had been to every recital since. Ezra had been to two. When Mr. Hart retired from the orchestra, he began coaching the student orchestra at Coolidge High three afternoons a week, unpaid, and Jalen had sat in its cello section ever since.

"Scholarship for what? Sitting in a chair?" He twisted the cap off the bottle. "Boy's sixteen. When I was sixteen I was framing houses."

"When you were sixteen," Angela said, "you didn't have a father who came to anything."

The kitchen went still. Even the cello seemed to pause.

Ezra stood there with the bottle halfway to his mouth. His father's watch, the stopped one, gleamed on his wrist. Kambria had never once seen him without it, and never seen it tell the right time.

"I'm going to shower," he said, and left.

Angela closed her eyes. Neither spoke. They had prayed for this man together for years, and some nights there was nothing left to say but his name.

"Mommy."

Mia stood in the doorway in her pajamas, four years old and solemn, clutching a sheet of paper to her chest. She had been sent to bed an hour ago.

"Baby, what are you doing up?"

"I made a picture for Miss Kambria." She padded across the linoleum and laid it on top of the red notebook, then stepped back and watched their faces, proud of what she had made.

It was a house. Their house; Kambria recognized the crooked porch light. But the sky above it was not blue. Mia had colored it red from edge to edge, pressing so hard the crayon had gone waxy and dark, and in the middle of all that red she had drawn a small yellow circle, with light pouring out of it.

"That's beautiful, sweet pea," Kambria said. "Why is the sky red?"

Mia considered this seriously. "That's what the sky does when Jesus is almost here."

Angela's pen stopped moving.

"Who told you that, baby?"

"Nobody." Mia shrugged, already bored with the question. She pointed to a small rectangle in the front window of the house, with a brown shape inside it. "That's Daddy on the couch. He doesn't see it because he's sleeping."

Kambria looked at Angela. Angela looked at the drawing. Upstairs, the shower came on.

"Bed," Angela said finally, too brightly, and scooped her daughter up.

When she came back down, she sat across from Kambria again and pulled her Bible toward her, and they went on. Angela found her place in Matthew 24, where Jesus tells His disciples to watch, and began to read aloud in her clear, even teacher's voice.

"'*Watch therefore: for ye know not what hour your Lord doth come. But know this, that if the goodman of the house had known in what watch the thief would come, he would have watched…*'" (Matthew 24:42–43)

She stopped.

Not at the end of the verse. In the middle of it. Her finger stayed on the page, and she did not look up.

Then she looked up, and reached across the table, across the red notebook and the blue one and Mia's waxy red drawing, and took both of Kambria's hands in hers.

Angela reached for people's hands all the time. She held them to pray, to comfort, to welcome; it was the most natural thing in the world to her. Yet in all their years of Thursday nights, Kambria had never seen her reach out like this, for herself, to be held. Her hands were cold now, and trembling.

She had seen it in other women, at other times, and she knew what it meant.

"Angie," she whispered. "What is it?"

Angela started to speak and stopped. Tears ran down to her chin, and still Angela didn't let go to wipe them away.

"I found a lump," she said. "Three weeks ago. Right here." She drew Kambria's fingers to her side, just below her left arm, and let it rest there. "I had the mammogram Monday. They called me back today. The radiologist..." She stopped and pressed her lips together. "He thinks it's cancer, Kam. They want to do a biopsy Thursday."

Out in the studio, even the cello had stopped. Kambria didn't know when. Nothing moved in the house.

"Does Ezra know?"

Angela shook her head. "Not yet. I can't. Not until I know for sure." A small, broken laugh escaped her. "You know what he'll say. He'll look at me and say, 'Where's your God now?'"

Kambria came around the table. She knelt beside Angela's chair, and Angela bent forward into her arms, and the two women held each other in the warm yellow kitchen with the notebooks of the end of the world spread out around them. Kambria felt her friend's shoulders shaking and heard a sound she had never heard Angela make in all their years: a low, wordless weeping.

"I'm scared," Angela whispered into her shoulder. "I'm not scared to die. I'm scared to leave them. Mia's four. Jalen's sixteen and he barely talks to his father. And Ezra..." Her voice cracked. "I'm scared to leave him like this. Who's going to pray for him?"

"I will," Kambria said. "Every day. As long as it takes."

They stayed like that while the clock above the stove ticked on.

At last Angela sat back and wiped her face with the heels of her hands and laughed through it, a real laugh this time. "Look at us. Two old prophecy ladies crying all over Matthew." Her eyes went to the Bible, still open where she'd stopped, and fresh tears came, but she was smiling now. "Whatever it is, He already knows. He knew before I did. He knew before the foundation of the world."

They prayed then, holding hands across the notebooks, as on every Thursday. This time Kambria's voice shook.

"Father, it's Kambria. And Angela. You know why we're here. You knew before she did. Hold her, Lord. Whatever is in her body, be bigger than it. And hold Ezra. He's going to need You more than either of us."

"Amen," Angela whispered.

\* \* \*

The next afternoon, between two sequencing runs, Kambria's phone buzzed in the pocket of her lab coat.

*Biopsy is Thursday at 2. Still haven't told Ezra. Pray for me.*

She read it in the lab stairwell, between the third and fourth floors, where no one could see her face. Then she took out the small gold notebook, the one for the church, and opened it to a clean page. She didn't write a headline or a verse. She wrote a name. Then, underneath, she wrote the date.

That same night, across the city, Angela Patterson sat alone at her dinner table after everyone was asleep and opened a brand-new notebook, a plain black one, never used.

On the cover she wrote two words, studied them until the ink had dried, and closed the cover.

*For Ezra.*
