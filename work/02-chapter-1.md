## Chapter 1: The Dinner Table

> *By faith Noah, being warned of God of things not seen as yet, moved with fear, prepared an ark to the saving of his house.* — Hebrews 11:7

**Word study: *warned of God* — χρηματίζω** Greek *chrēmatizō* (Strong's G5537). To give a divine answer, to be instructed by God. The word was used for an official reply from a king or a court. Noah's warning was not a hunch or a feeling. It was a word from the throne.

On summer evenings the neighbors brought their wine out to the hill to watch the old man work. Cypress beams as long as a street. Pitch boiling in pots. His sons hauling timber while the town's young men called up to them, laughing. "When's the rain coming, Noah?" There had never been rain. Not once, not in all the years of the world. Noah wiped his forehead, picked up his hammer, and went back to work. A hundred and twenty years is a long time to be laughed at. (Genesis 6:3, 14–22)

\* \* \*

*A couple of years earlier.*

The morning the newspaper announced that someone had found Noah's ark, Kambria Elizabeth read the headline in the break room of the outbreak lab and laughed out loud. Then she read the second paragraph, and she stopped laughing. She cut the page out with the lab's good scissors and carried it in her coat pocket the rest of the day.

Such days were what her work was made of. She was a forensic scientist with the Centers for Disease Control, and when people got sick and nobody knew why, the samples came to her bench, and she traced the germ back to where it started: the farm, the plant, the water main, the one bad shipment out of ten thousand good ones. That week it was two children in a hospital in Ohio and a bagged-lettuce plant in California, tied together by nothing but a fingerprint of bacterial DNA. Her whole career rested on one stubborn belief, that nothing happens without a trail, and that anyone patient enough can follow it back.

One thing did not fit. A single isolate from Ohio carried a stretch of thirty-odd bases in a region where nothing was supposed to be written at all, a patch of sequence that matched nothing in any database she could reach. It was too orderly to be damage and too short to be anything a germ would keep for itself. The confirmation run had come back from Brandick Health Labs in Rockville, the private bench the agency used for its overflow, stamped clean. She logged the patch as probable contamination, because that was the honest category for a thing she could not explain. Then she saved a copy to a drive that was not the lab's, for a trail was her whole faith, and she did not like one that began in the middle.

At a quarter to seven she turned onto Twelfth Street in Brookland, in Northeast Washington, still in her gray slacks and flat shoes, her CDC badge flipped backward on its lanyard. Thursday evenings in that neighborhood had a sound of their own. Somewhere on Otis Street a speaker sat on a porch rail, sending a Christian hip-hop song out over the rooftops, and up on the hill the monastery bells began to ring the hour. Kambria caught her head nodding before she had decided to let it. The old brick rowhouses stood shoulder to shoulder behind small, fierce gardens, and across from the Pattersons' house Mr. Odell sat in his lawn chair, where he sat that October, as he had every warm evening since before Kambria knew the street. He lifted two fingers from the armrest as she parked. She lifted two back.

Angela Patterson's house was the brick one with the crooked porch light and the front step that her husband, a carpenter, had been promising to rebuild for six summers. Nobody knocked there. Kambria went in through the side door, into a kitchen that was all steam and noise: Swedish meatballs simmering in cream gravy on the back burner, loaded potato pierogies browning in butter in the skillet, a small pot of lingonberry jam warming beside them. In the middle of it stood Angela, a third-grade teacher with a voice that could quiet a cafeteria without ever rising, and the closest thing Kambria had to a sister, a dish towel over one shoulder and a wooden spoon in her hand, conducting.

"You look like you haven't eaten since Monday," Angela said, without turning around. "Sit. You will not read the Word on an empty stomach in my house."

"I ate."

"A granola bar in a parking lot is not eating."

At the table, Angela's four-year-old daughter, Mia, knelt on a chair with her hair in two puffs tied up in yellow ribbons, working a purple crayon across a coloring page with the focus of a lab tech plating cultures. "Miss Kambria! I know my verse!"

"Let's hear it."

"Jesus wept." She said it proudly, and at a great volume.

"It's the shortest one in the whole Bible," Angela said, "and she has decided that is the one she's keeping."

The screen door slapped, and Angela's son came in from the backyard: sixteen years old, all lanky legs, with headphones around his neck and calluses on the fingertips of his left hand from hours with the cello. Jalen kissed his mother on the cheek, stole a pierogi straight out of the skillet, and dodged the spoon.

"Hey, Miss Kam."

"Hey, maestro."

"Is Dad home?" He asked it of the skillet instead of his mother.

"He's finishing a job in Takoma," Angela said. "He said he would try."

"Cool," Jalen said. He carried a plate piled high back out to the little studio at the end of the yard, and the screen door had barely finished its second slap when the cello began to play.

They ate standing at the counter, as Thursdays always went, and Kambria noticed that Angela, who had fixed three plates, only moved the food around on her own. She noticed, too, that Angela's left hand drifted to her side and away again, twice. Kambria filed it where she filed everything. Then Angela asked about the lab, and Kambria told her about the lettuce, and the moment passed.

They had met years earlier, in the basement fellowship hall of Angela's church, at a Thursday women's Bible study that began with fourteen women. Kambria, then a young biochemist working nights in a university lab, had come in on her one free evening because she could not sleep, and sat down in the back. By spring there were eleven women. By the next, six. After old Mother Dorsey went home to the Lord, there were two, and Angela had said, "Well, Thursday is still Thursday," and moved the whole study into her kitchen.

Now the plates were rinsed and stacked, Mia had been carried up to bed with a cup of water and three promises, and the two women sat across from each other as they had every Thursday night for years: two cups of coffee going cold, two worn King James Bibles, and a dozen spiral notebooks spread out between them.

Each notebook had a color. Blue for Israel. Red, the one she had labeled *Of the Nations*. Green for money. Gold, the thinnest one, for the church.

Kambria dated everything, in her neat slanting hand. Angela kept her own habit. On every page she drew a box around one line, in whatever pen was nearest, the line she would look for first if she lost all the rest.

"Why that one?" Kambria had asked her once, years ago, tapping a box with her pen.

"Because if the house burned down and I got one line back," Angela said, "I want it to be that one."

Between the two Bibles sat Angela's Strong's Concordance, two thousand pages thick, its spine split and mended with packing tape. Every Thursday started the same way. One verse. One word in it. Then down to the root, in the Hebrew or the Greek, until they knew what that word had meant to the people who first heard it.

Tonight the word was *watch*. Angela had her finger on it in Matthew 24, and Kambria was running down the columns in the back of the concordance, past thousands of numbers, to the Greek.

"Here. Eleven twenty-seven." Kambria sounded it out. "*Grēgoreuō. γρηγορεύω.* To keep awake. To be vigilant." She read the next line twice. "It comes from a word that means to rouse up. To wake somebody who's asleep."

Angela sat back. "So it doesn't mean look out the window."

"No. It means don't fall asleep."

Angela wrote it in pen in the margin of her Bible, small and neat, beside the verse: *1127. Stay awake.* Then she glanced through the doorway at the couch in the living room, where her husband fell asleep in front of the television most nights, and said nothing.

"Read it again," Angela said.

Kambria pulled a newspaper clipping from the back of the red notebook and smoothed it out with her palm. Four wars on three continents, all of them flaring in the same month. She had clipped one like it on Monday, and another the week before that.

She read it aloud to Angela. Then she laid her pen across the open notebook and sat back, silent. Every week she would find something in the newspaper, and then she would find it in the Bible, and the two would line up so closely that it frightened her. Wars and rumours of wars. Nation against nation, kingdom against kingdom. She had been reading those words her whole life. She had never expected to watch them come up off the page and onto the front of the paper.

"Angie," she said, barely above a whisper. "It's all in here. All of it. Just like He said it would be." She pressed her fingertips against her closed eyes. "Some nights it's more than I can hold."

Angela flipped through her Bible and found Matthew 24 quickly. "'For nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom: and there shall be famines, and pestilences, and earthquakes, in divers places.'" She tapped the next line with one finger. "'All these are the beginning of sorrows.'"

"The beginning," Kambria said. "That's what keeps me up. It's not the end yet, but it's definitely intensifying." She slid the clipping into the red notebook and dated it in her neat, slanting hand. "It's like He's turning the pages faster, Angie. Ten years ago I'd find one of these articles a month. Now I see it every day."

"And one more." She laid it on the table. It was from that morning's paper. It showed a long, boat-shaped hill on a mountainside in eastern Turkey, snow above it. The headline said NOAH'S ARK FOUND, and under it, in smaller type, *Scientists confirm structure in Ararat region is man-made.*

Angela read it twice. Then she turned back to Matthew 24 and ran her finger down the page. "'But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.'" She looked up. "They were eating and drinking, and marrying, right up to the day Noah went into the ark. And they knew not until the flood came and took them all away." She set her hand on the photograph. "He's showing them the boat, Kam. Right before."

Kambria dated the clipping and slid it into the red notebook with the others.

On the windowsill over the sink, Angela's little radio went on talking under the clink of the coffee cups. Kambria half-listened to the announcer: a recall of bagged romaine lettuce in fourteen states, E. coli, two children in the hospital in Ohio. Then a report on the drought in the West, the land gone brown from Nevada to Texas, and Lake Mead lower than it had been since the dam was built. She wrote both of them down in the margin of the red notebook, small, under the date, because she wrote everything down. *Famines, and pestilences.* Then she looked through the doorway at the empty couch, the dent in the cushion where a tired man slept through the news most nights, and the word they had just learned came back to her. *Stay awake.*

The back door banged open.

Ezra, Angela's husband, trudged in with muddy work boots, a dusty tool belt, and drywall dust in his hair. He was a stately man, broad in the shoulders, with hands rough as unplaned oak.

He kicked off his boots, glanced past the table where the ladies studied, and started toward the fridge. "Well," he said. "I can see the doomsday club is in session."

"Hi, honey." Angela tipped her face up, and he kissed her forehead, and for one second the teasing went out of his eyes. It always did with her.

Then he looked at Kambria. "Let me guess. World's ending Tuesday."

"Nobody knows the day or the hour," Kambria said, without looking up at him.

"Then why do y'all keep writing this stuff down?"

"Because He told us to watch." That was Angela. She never argued with him about it. She just kept inviting him, even though she had stopped expecting him to say yes.

Ezra opened the refrigerator, took out a beer, and let the door swing shut harder than it needed to. From the studio came the long, low groan of a cello: a single note, held until it trembled, then sliding up into what sounded almost like grief.

Through the window over the sink he could see it: the little studio across the grass, its windows glowing amber in the dark. He had poured the slab for it himself five summers ago and framed every wall, with Angela holding the level and an eleven-year-old Jalen at his elbow, handing him nails and asking a hundred questions about construction. He had taught his son to frame a wall, hang a door, and finish the trim on the studio that summer, and they had grown close doing it. When it was finished, they flipped the breaker together and stood in the doorway, grinning while every light came on at once.

Ezra looked at the glowing windows for one second. Then he looked away.

"He's still at that thing?"

"That thing," Angela said, "got him a scholarship audition."

Lionel Hart had been Jalen's teacher since the boy was nine years old. He had played viola with the Capital Philharmonic for thirty-one years, and there was no string instrument he couldn't teach. The first time he heard Jalen sawing away at a borrowed cello in the church basement, he walked over, fixed Jalen's bow hold without a word, and told Angela he would teach him for nothing. He had been to every recital since. Ezra had been to two. When Mr. Hart retired from the orchestra, he began coaching the student orchestra at Coolidge High three afternoons a week, unpaid, and Jalen had sat in its cello section ever since.

"Scholarship for what? Sitting in a chair?" He twisted the cap off the bottle. "Boy's sixteen. When I was sixteen I was framing houses."

"When you were sixteen," Angela said, "you didn't have a father who came to anything."

The kitchen went still. Even the cello seemed to pause.

Ezra stood there with the bottle halfway to his mouth. His father's watch, the stopped one, gleamed on his wrist. Kambria had never once seen him without it, and never seen it tell the right time.

"I'm going to shower," he said, and left.

Angela closed her eyes. Neither spoke. They had prayed for this man together for years, and some nights there was nothing left to say but his name.

"Mommy."

Mia stood in the doorway in her pajamas, four years old and solemn, clutching a sheet of paper to her chest. She had been sent to bed an hour ago.

"Baby, what are you doing up?"

"I made a picture for Miss Kambria." She padded across the linoleum and laid it on top of the red notebook, then stepped back and watched their faces, proud of what she had made.

It was a house. Their house; Kambria recognized the crooked porch light. But the sky above it was not blue. Mia had colored it red from edge to edge, pressing so hard the crayon had gone waxy and dark, and in the middle of all that red she had drawn a small yellow circle, with light pouring out of it.

"That's beautiful, sweet pea," Kambria said. "Why is the sky red?"

Mia considered this seriously. "That's what the sky does when Jesus is almost here."

Angela's pen stopped moving.

"Who told you that, baby?"

"Nobody." Mia shrugged, already bored with the question. She pointed to a small rectangle in the front window of the house, with a brown shape inside it. "That's Daddy on the couch. He doesn't see it because he's sleeping."

Kambria looked at Angela. Angela looked at the drawing. Upstairs, the shower came on.

"Bed," Angela said finally, too brightly, and scooped her daughter up.

When she came back down, she sat across from Kambria again and pulled her Bible toward her, and they went on. Angela found her place in Matthew 24 and began to read aloud in her clear, even teacher's voice.

"'*Watch therefore: for ye know not what hour your Lord doth come. But know this, that if the goodman of the house had known in what watch the thief would come, he would have watched…*'" (Matthew 24:42–43)

She stopped.

Not at the end of the verse. In the middle of it. Her finger stayed on the page, and she did not look up.

Then she looked up, and reached across the table, across the red notebook and the blue one and Mia's waxy red drawing, and took both of Kambria's hands in hers.

Angela reached for people's hands all the time. She held them to pray, to comfort, to welcome; it was the most natural thing in the world to her. Yet in all their years of Thursday nights, Kambria had never seen her reach out like this, for herself, to be held. Her hands were cold now, and trembling. Kambria had studied biochemistry before she ever joined the CDC, and she had spent years on the trail of whatever makes people sick. She knew what trembling hands usually meant, in the moment before bad news came.

She knew that tremble.

"Angie," she whispered. "What is it?"

Angela opened her mouth, and nothing came out. Her eyes filled and spilled over, and still Angela didn't let go to wipe them away.

"I found a lump," she said. "Three weeks ago. Right here." She drew one of Kambria's hands to her side, just below her left arm, and let it rest there. "I had the mammogram Monday. They called me back today. The radiologist..." She stopped and swallowed hard. "He thinks it's cancer, Kam. They want to do a biopsy Thursday."

Out in the studio, even the cello had stopped. Kambria didn't know when. Nothing moved in the house.

"Does Ezra know?"

Angela shook her head. "Not yet. I can't. Not until I know for sure." A small, broken laugh escaped her. "You know what he'll say. He'll look at me and say, 'Where's your God now?'"

Kambria came around the table. She knelt beside Angela's chair, and Angela bent forward into her arms, and the two women held each other in the warm yellow kitchen with the notebooks of the end of the world spread out around them. Kambria felt her friend's shoulders shaking and heard a sound she had never heard Angela make in all their years: a low, wordless weeping.

"I'm scared," Angela whispered into her shoulder. "I'm not scared to die. I'm scared to leave them. Mia's four. Jalen's sixteen and he barely talks to his father. And Ezra..." Her voice cracked. "I'm scared to leave him like this. Who's going to pray for him?"

"I will," Kambria said. "Every day. As long as it takes."

They stayed like that while the clock above the stove ticked on.

At last Angela sat back and wiped her face with the heels of her hands and laughed through it, a real laugh this time. "Look at us. Two old prophecy ladies crying all over Matthew." She looked down at the Bible, still open where she'd stopped, and fresh tears came, but she was smiling now. "Whatever it is, He already knows. He knew before I did. He knew before the foundation of the world."

They prayed then, holding hands across the notebooks, as on every Thursday. This time Kambria's voice shook.

"Father, it's Kambria. And Angela. You know why we're here. You knew before she did. Hold her, Lord. Whatever is in her body, be bigger than it. And hold Ezra. He's going to need You more than either of us."

"Amen," Angela whispered.

And as she prayed, with the wars and earthquakes and signs of the end spread across the table, Kambria saw that for the first time in all those years, she was afraid of something that wasn't written in any of them.

\* \* \*

The next afternoon, between two sequencing runs, Kambria's phone buzzed in the pocket of her lab coat.

*Biopsy is Thursday at 2. Still haven't told Ezra. Pray for me.*

She read it in the lab stairwell, between the third and fourth floors, where no one could see her face. Then she took out the small gold notebook, the one for the church, and opened it to a clean page. She didn't write a headline or a verse. She wrote a name. Then, underneath, she wrote the date, because she logged everything that mattered, and this mattered more than anything she had ever logged.

That same night, across the city, Angela Patterson sat alone at her dinner table after everyone was asleep and opened a brand-new notebook, a plain black one, never used.

On the first page she wrote two words, studied them until the ink had dried, and closed the cover.

*For Ezra.*
