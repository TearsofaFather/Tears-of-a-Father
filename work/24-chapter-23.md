## Chapter 23: Tears of a Father

> *And said, O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to thee, my God: for our iniquities are increased over our head, and our trespass is grown up unto the heavens.* — Ezra 9:6

**Word study: *ashamed* — בּוֹשׁ** Hebrew *bosh* (Strong's H954). To be ashamed, to be put to confusion, to be disappointed in what you trusted. It is the word in Genesis for the man and the woman who were naked and *not ashamed*, before the fall. And it is the word in Psalm 34: *They looked unto him, and were lightened: and their faces were not ashamed.* (Psalm 34:5)

*And when I heard this thing, I rent my garment and my mantle, and plucked off the hair of my head and of my beard, and sat down astonied.* (Ezra 9:3)

\* \* \*

The battery icon in the corner of the screen said nineteen percent.

Ezra sat at the dinner table with one candle and Angela's notebook under his hand, in a house that had never been so quiet. The refrigerator had stopped humming. Her black Sunday shoes stood by the front door where he could not bring himself to move them. Nothing in all the dark was alive but the little light of the screen.

There were two files on the drive. Jalen had named them himself.

*For Dad.*

*Untitled.*

He opened the first one with a hand he could not keep still.

The picture was crooked and too bright: the studio, the summer they built it, the bare pine floor and the south window black behind him, a coffee can of nails propping up the camera.

Then a face leaned in close, too close, blocking out everything, and leaned back again.

It was Jalen. Eleven years old, in a Nationals T-shirt two sizes too big, with sawdust in his hair and a scab on his elbow, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the room they had just finished.

Ezra froze with his lungs half full.

"Okay," the boy whispered. "Okay. Is it on? It's on." He looked over his shoulder at the door, then back at the camera. "Hi, Dad. It's me. It's night, and everybody's asleep, so I have to be quiet."

He looked around the empty room, and his whole face lit. "Thank you for building this with me. You were tired every day after work, and you still did it. And you let me hang the door. Mom says you never let anybody hang a door, not even Mr. Elijah, and you let me." He grinned so wide his eyes disappeared. "It's the best room in the whole world."

Ezra's hand had found its way over his mouth. He did not remember putting it there.

The boy came up onto his knees, too happy to sit still. "You're my hero, Dad. For real. I thanked God tonight for giving me the best dad ever. I said it out loud, and I think He heard me."

Then he settled back, and his voice went soft. "And when I saw you cry I knew I had to make a song where you can turn it into joy. You will always see what we built together."

He leaned in toward the camera and lowered his voice. "It's gonna take a long time, because I have to get really good first. But I already know what it's called. I'm not telling anybody. Not even Mom. You're the only one. Okay?"

He took a breath.

"It's called 'Tears of a Father.'"

He sat back, proud of himself, and then embarrassed, and rubbed his nose with the back of his wrist. "Because of you. I love you, Dad. That's all. Bye."

His small hand came toward the camera, reaching, and the screen went black.

Ezra sat in the candlelight with his son's voice still in the room. *Tears of a Father.* Seven years. Jalen had carried that name for seven years, through every slammed door and every fight in the kitchen, through his mother's dying, through the empty seat. He had never told a soul. He had kept it for his father, as he promised, and his father never came to hear it.

He knew what it was to hold out one small thing and wait for someone to look. He had been eight years old once, walking a mile of gravel home with a single free throw like a coal in his chest, wanting his father to look up from the television. Jalen had been standing in front of him for seven years holding out something the same size, and Ezra had looked at his tool belt.

Ezra laid his forehead down on the dinner table beside Angela's notebook, and he could not lift it for many minutes.

Then, because he could not stop now, he opened the second file.

On the screen, his son walked out into the light in a white suit.

He watched his son sit down before a closed red curtain, with the old man in white behind him. He watched him search the seats, and the camera followed his eyes down Row C: Mia with her daisies, his mother in her Sunday hat, Angela's purple scarf lying across a seat. And beside it, on the aisle, a seat with a program on it, and three letters in black marker.

*DAD.*

Jalen leaned toward the microphone. His chin was shaking, and Ezra watched him make it stop.

"I'm dedicating this to someone who isn't here right now."

For one second Ezra thought of Angela. Then he saw the boy on the studio floor again, leaning close to the camera. *You're the only one.*

The cello began.

\* \* \*

It was the low line. He knew it before the second note. He had heard it through the studio wall every night for longer than he cared to count, from the couch, with the television on to cover it.

And he was in the backyard, seven summers ago, with sawdust in the creases of his knuckles and his big hands over his son's small ones on the hammer. *Let the hammer do the work, J. Don't fight it. Just let it fall.* Angela was holding the level and laughing at the bubble. He had been happy. He had known it at the time, and he had not said so to anyone.

He was in the kitchen, and Jalen was fifteen and taller than his mother, and Ezra was saying, *There's no money in music, J. A trade is something nobody can take from you,* louder than he needed to, because the child would not look away. He had meant it as love. He had said it as a verdict.

Her hand on the hospice blanket that last night, opening.

His breath came in hitches. He put his fist against his mouth.

\* \* \*

Ezra watched the camera drift, unsteady in Kambria's crying hands, down to the seat on the aisle.

Empty.

He saw his phone on the counter. *Seats saved. Row C, on the aisle. 7:00. Don't be late.* He saw his own thumb press the small yellow thumbs-up and set the phone face down.

*Where were you? Told you. Work thing.*

On the screen the curtain began to open, and fifty-eight children in white were playing behind it, and had been playing the whole time. And Ezra understood that the camera kept finding that seat because his son's eyes kept finding it.

Then the cello broke upward and held, straight and hard and pressing, calling out over a full hall to one seat on the aisle. To someone who wasn't there.

*It was never only for her.*

*It was for me.*

The bus depot at Hollis Junction. He was eight, and he was holding out his hand, and his father patted him on the head and turned his back and walked out to the bus.

His own back, every night, turned to the studio wall. He had turned away from them, every night, until the day they were gone.

*I did it. I did to him what was done to me.*

The bottle. The raised hand. The couch. The red string. The thumbs-up. The empty seat.

*I knew. I chose it. It was me.*

\* \* \*

He had nothing left to defend. Every excuse had fallen down at once, and he sat in the ruins of them and made no move to pick one up. There was only a man at a dinner table who had been called, and called, and called, and had turned his back every time.

And somewhere out past these walls the trumpet had sounded, and everyone who was ready had heard it and gone. They had not left him in anger. They had simply gone, as you go when the door is open and Someone is calling, and he had slept through the only sound that ever mattered. The candle still burned. Her shoes stood by the door. Everything in the house was still here but them.

*O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to thee…* (Ezra 9:6)

On the screen, the cello kept calling, and Ezra could not sit still under it any longer.

He pushed back his chair so hard it fell over behind him. He stood up in the dark kitchen, and he screamed. It was one word, and it came from a place in him that had been locked since he was eight years old.

"Jesus!"

And he lifted his hands.

He lifted them high, both of them, open, over his head: the hands he had sat on in the front pew when he was eight. There was no church here. There was a kitchen with one candle and a dead refrigerator and his wife's shoes by the door. He lifted them anyway.

"Jesus, it was me," he cried. "All of it. I turned my back on her, and on my boy, and on You. I knew, and I chose it. I'm not sorry because it's late. I'm sorry because it's true. Forgive me. Wash me. I can't make myself clean. Wash me."

He did not leave any of it out.

"The bottle. The couch. The television turned up so I wouldn't hear my own boy. The thumbs-up on a text, and the empty seat on the aisle. Mia's porch light, and I walked past it. Angela's hand on the blanket, and I made her wait for it till the very end. Celeste. I laughed at Kambria on my own porch and shut the door in her face. All of it, Lord. I name it. Every bit."

And as he named them, the hard thing he had carried since he was a boy began to come loose, and then it was gone, lifted out of him. *If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.* (1 John 1:9) He was clean. He had not been clean in his whole life, and he had not known it until now.

On the screen, the orchestra had begun to rise beneath his son, and a drum was ticking, faster and faster. Ezra looked down at his father's watch. It said 3:17. It had said 3:17 for thirty-five years. The ticking ran together into one sound, and stopped. The harp sang the grieving line he had heard through the studio wall every night. It was not grieving anymore.

His knees went, and he was on the floor.

His hands were still open in front of him. He held them out into the dark.

Angela had reached for him for twelve years. At every meal, on the sixth floor, on the hospice blanket the last night of her life, she had reached for him, and he had kept his hands in his lap. Only at the very end had he finally taken hers. Now he reached, with nothing in his hands and nothing left to give, for the hands of God.

And he felt them take hold of his.

It was not a picture, and it was not a voice. It was a warmth that came over his open hands and moved up his arms and into his chest, steady, and it filled the place where the hard thing had been. He knew who it was. Angela had told him, the afternoon she pressed the notebook against his chest. *When you finally reach for Him, honey, He'll send you the Comforter. You won't have to carry any of it by yourself.* Jesus had promised it to His friends the night before He died: *I will not leave you comfortless: I will come to you.* (John 14:18)

The Comforter had come.

And then, at last, the tears came.

They came without any sound at first, and then all at once. He had wept for Angela all that first winter, on his knees in front of the television with her laugh on the screen. This was not that. He wept for his son and for his little girl. He wept for every night he had turned the television up. Jesus had promised it in His sermon: *Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.* (Matthew 5:4) He let it come. For the first time in his life, he did not want it to stop.

The candle burned down an inch. The house was empty and still.

And in that stillness, in the place that had been shut since the bus station, he heard it. Not with his ears. It was the same call that had come to him in the dream of the red sky, the night he stood at the window and said *No.* It had never stopped calling. He had only never been weak enough to hear it.

*Ezra.*

He sat on the kitchen floor with his open hands held out in the dark, and he answered with the last breath he had, in a voice that was barely there.

"I'm here."

\* \* \*

He woke on the kitchen floor at first light, with his cheek against the chill of the linoleum and Angela's notebook under his hand.

He had no memory of falling asleep. The candle had burned down to a puddle of wax on the saucer. The house was gray and cold, and he lay listening to the quiet and found it no longer frightened him.

He got up on aching knees. He righted the fallen chair and set it back at the table, square, where it belonged. Then he stood at the kitchen window and looked out at the backyard.

The studio stood at the back of the lot in the gray light, under the bare branches of the maple: a small building of white clapboard with a green door and a window on the south side, and a short cedar ramp they had built so Jalen could roll his cello case in on its wheels. He had not set foot in it since the night of the recital. For a year he had walked past it every day on the way to the truck, and turned the radio up in the truck so he would not hear what came out of it at night, and told himself it was a hobby.

He went out the back door in his socks, across the frosted grass, and opened the green door.

\* \* \*

The room was immaculate.

That was the first thing he saw, before anything else, before he let himself see the cello stand or the chair. Jalen had kept it clean. The pine floor was swept. The music stand was dusted. The sound panels on the walls, the ones they had glued up together that first summer, were straight and whole. The recording gear on the little desk was coiled and labeled in Jalen's handwriting. The room smelled of rosin and cedar and the faint stale smell of a room no one had been in for days.

On the wall beside the door hung the framed photograph Angela had taken the day they finished: Ezra and an eleven-year-old Jalen standing in front of the green door with their arms around each other's shoulders, both of them covered in sawdust, both of them grinning.

Ezra stood in the doorway and looked at it until he could look at the rest.

Behind the two of them in the picture, just inside the open door, was the casing they had hung together on the last day, and behind the casing, the first stud. Last night, somewhere past two, in Angela's notebook, between a grocery list and a prayer for the audition, he had read a line he did not understand: *Read Ps. 91 at supper. J. asked me twice for the chapter and verse, then wouldn't tell me why. My secret keeper.*

The night before they framed the studio, she had read Psalm 91 at supper. He remembered it now: the spaghetti going cold, and Jalen not eating, watching her mouth as she read.

He took the pry bar from the hook by the door, the one they had left there for the next project. He set it behind the left side of the casing and leaned on it. The finish nails shrieked coming out of the jamb. He had taught Jalen to set those nails. He pulled the casing away a hand's width, then a forearm's, until the trim cracked at the miter, and he did not care.

On the bare stud, in an eleven-year-old's black marker, in letters that ran downhill:

*ME AND DAD. FIRST WALL.*

And under it, small, the way Ezra numbered every stud on every plan he had ever drawn: *91 4*.

He knew the verse before he could have said it. Angela had read it over the spaghetti with her finger on the line. *He shall cover thee with his feathers, and under his wings shalt thou trust: his truth shall be thy shield and buckler.* (Psalm 91:4)

His son had put his father under God's wings on the first day, and told no one, and a week later Ezra had hung drywall over it without a look.

He slid down the doorframe until he was sitting on the cedar ramp in his socks, the pry bar across his knees. He stayed there until the frost on the grass had gone to water.

When he could stand, he pressed the casing back against the jamb. He did not nail it. He left the stud where he could see it.

\* \* \*

On the music stand there were two things.

The first was a letter on heavy cream paper, folded once. He opened it. At the top, in navy blue, was the crest of the Capital Philharmonic Orchestra. It was dated the ninth of November. It offered Jalen Patterson a full-time position in the cello section of the orchestra, beginning in the new year, with a salary and benefits and a line at the bottom that said the orchestra had never before offered a contract to a musician so young. Below the last line, in blue ink, stood Jalen's signature, and beside it the signature of a woman named Evelyn Shaw.

He had signed it seventeen days before he was taken. He had told no one at home, least of all the man who called it a hobby.

Under the letter, on the music stand, was the score.

It was handwritten, in pen, on music paper, a thick stack of it held together with a black binder clip. The first page was folded back over the top of the stack, so that the title was hidden. Jalen had carried it out of the house folded that way, and Ezra had not thought anything of it.

Ezra took the clip off. He unfolded the first page.

Across the top, in large letters, in the careful, laboring handwriting of an eleven-year-old boy, older than the rest of the pages and faded a little, written first, with the music added under it over the years:

*TEARS OF A FATHER*

Under it, in the same boy's hand, a verse:

*…and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes.* (Revelation 7:17)

He knew that verse. He had heard Angela read it. She had read it to Jalen on his bed one night when he was nine, the night after his first recital, when he came crying to her about something Ezra could no longer remember, and Ezra had stood in the hall outside the door and listened. She had told him about the people in white, the great multitude who came out of great tribulation, and how God would wipe away every tear. *Every tear, baby. Yours, and mine, and your daddy's too.*

The white suits. Every player on that stage in white. He had seen it on the screen and not understood. It had been that verse. It had been a promise, standing on a stage in front of an empty seat.

Under the verse, in Jalen's grown handwriting, newer, in black ink:

*For Dad, who built the room where I found it.*

Ezra turned the pages. He could not read music, not really; Jalen had tried to teach him once, and he had lost patience. But he could see the shape of it, the low notes at the start crowded down at the bottom of the staff, and then the long climb, page after page, the notes rising. And in the margin of the rising passage, the one he had heard climbing through the wall every night for all that time and stopping, always stopping, in the same place, there was a note in small black ink, in the boy's hand.

*the night we flipped the breaker and all the lights came on*

He remembered. He remembered the night they finished the wiring, the last night, after dark, and Jalen standing at the panel with his hand on the main breaker and Ezra saying, *Go ahead, it's your building,* and Jalen throwing it, and every light in the studio coming on at once, and Jalen whooping and jumping up and down on the new floor, and Ezra laughing, really laughing, and lifting his son off his feet.

Ezra sat down on the floor of the studio, with the score in his lap and his back against the wall they had framed together, and wept.

\* \* \*

He understood it then, sitting on the floor of the room they had built. Every night through the wall, until the fall, his son had been talking to him. Words had stopped working between them the summer Angela got sick, so Jalen had stopped using them. He had come out here, to the one place his father had loved him without holding anything back, and he had played the low line because that was where they were, and the climb because he remembered the night the lights came on.

His son had not kept playing in spite of him. He had kept playing because of him.

"I heard it, J," he said, out loud, to the empty room. "I finally heard it."

There was no one there to hear him say it.

\* \* \*

He could not stay. The room was full of his son, and his son was only one of the two doors that had stood open on Thursday morning.

He ran. He crossed the frozen grass with the score against his chest and went in at the back door and up the stairs two at a time, as on Thursday, and this time he knew what he would find.

Mia's door was open. The power had taken her nightlight, and the paper stars were gone from the ceiling, and the room was gray and small and plain. Her yellow nightgown with the moons lay on the bed in the shape of a sleeping girl, the blanket still tucked around nothing. On Thursday morning he had touched it, and it had been warm.

It was cold.

He sat on the little bed and held the nightgown to his face. Strawberry, the kind Angela used to buy.

*That's Daddy on the couch. He doesn't see it because he's sleeping.*

She had said it about a crayon house. She was four years old. He had been on the couch.

*Please help Daddy not be sad.* She had prayed it over a plate of macaroni with her eyes shut, and he had not been able to say amen.

A drawing of a horse, and *That's nice, baby*, with his eyes on his phone. A red moon on the refrigerator, the wax pressed so hard it shone, made so that he would finally look at one of her pictures. A porch light she would not let anyone switch off. *So Daddy can find the door.*

On the last night, she had settled against his arm in her mother's shoes and told him the whole thing, in a story, in her clear small voice. The lamp in the window. The King. The shoes by the door. *The door stays open a little while after.* She had told him what was coming and where she would be and how to follow, and he had kissed the top of her head and thought it was a story.

*Leave the light on, Daddy.*

She was six years old. She had spent a year trying to get her father to look at her, with crayons and porch lights and prayers.

*Daddy, I need it to eat.* He had held her hand through the whole blessing, and when she asked for it back he had let it go. He would have held it forever.

He lay down on her bed with his feet hanging off the end, the nightgown against his chest and the score beside him, and he said her name. Not shouting, the way he had in the yard on Thursday. Low, as a father calls a child in at dusk.

"Mia."

No one came. He said it again, and again, until it stopped being a word.

The light in her window went from gray to gold. When he finally got up, he left her door open. A little girl had told him it would stay open a little while after, and she had never once lied to him.
