## Chapter 4: For Ezra, When You're Ready

> *And Israel stretched out his right hand, and laid it upon Ephraim's head, who was the younger, and his left hand upon Manasseh's head, guiding his hands wittingly; for Manasseh was the firstborn.* — Genesis 48:14

**Word study: *wittingly* — שָׂכַל** Hebrew *sakal* (Strong's H7919). To act wisely, with insight, knowing exactly what you are doing. Old Jacob was nearly blind, but he crossed his arms on purpose, and the younger son received the greater blessing.

The old man Jacob was dying, and his eyes were dim with age, so that he could not see. Joseph brought his two sons to the bedside, and the old man reached out for them in the dark. He found their heads with his trembling hands and rested them there, crossing his arms so that his right hand lay on the younger boy, and he blessed them. When he had finished blessing all his children, he gathered up his feet into the bed, and yielded up the ghost, and was gathered unto his people. (Genesis 48:10–16; 49:33)

\* \* \*

For a while that spring, it looked like they had won.

Angela finished her last round of chemotherapy on an overcast Tuesday in February. The nurses on the sixth floor lined the hallway and clapped while she rang the brass bell by the door, three times. Mr. Harold, Angela's friend from the recliner next to hers, cheered loudest. Kambria cried. Ezra stood at the end of the hallway in his work jacket and clapped along with everyone else, and when Angela turned and looked for him, he gave her a small, proud nod. It was the closest he had let her see him come to the infusion room in all those months.

She had surgery in March. Radiation in April. And on the first warm day of May, her oncologist, Dr. Sandoval, came into the exam room smiling and said the words they had been praying for, or that Angela had been praying for: *no evidence of disease.*

In the parking garage afterward, Ezra sat behind the wheel of his truck with both hands on it and didn't start the engine. Angela watched him and waited.

"Honey?"

"Give me a second," he said. His voice was rough. He kept his face turned toward the window so she wouldn't see his eyes, and she let him have that.

That summer was the best they'd had in years. Angela's hair came back soft and curly, a shade darker than before. They took the kids to the Outer Banks for a week, and Ezra taught five-year-old Mia to jump the waves while Angela watched from under an umbrella, laughing. Jalen, seventeen now and nearly as tall as his father, brought his cello and practiced on the porch of the rental house every evening at sunset. The unfinished melody had grown longer. It climbed higher now before it fell back. Ezra caught himself listening to it from inside the kitchen, and once, when Jalen hit a high note and held it, Ezra almost went out to tell him it was good. But he didn't.

On their last afternoon a storm came in off the ocean. They waited it out on the porch, all four of them, and watched the rain blow sideways across the dunes. When it passed, the sun came out low behind the house, and Mia jumped off the bench and pointed.

A rainbow stood over the water, the whole arch of it, from one end of the beach to the other. Above it was a second one, fainter.

"Mama! Two of them!"

Angela took Mia's hand and walked her down the wet boardwalk to the sand. Ezra followed with his coffee. Jalen stayed on the porch, but he set the cello down to look.

"You know what that is?" Angela knelt so her face was level with Mia's. "That's God keeping a promise. After the flood, He put His bow in the clouds and told Noah, every time you see this, I remember."

"Remember what?"

"That He keeps His word, baby. Every single time."

Mia thought about that. Then a butterfly came across the dune grass, dark, with a purple shine on its wings when the light caught them, and it landed on Angela's knee. Mia held her breath.

"Purple," she whispered. "That's your color."

"It sure is."

"Where did it come from?"

So Angela told her. She told her about the caterpillar that eats and eats, and then hangs itself up and wraps itself in a little case and goes still. "It looks like it died," she said. "Anybody walking by would think so. But it didn't. It was waiting. And when the time came, it came out new, with wings."

Mia looked at her mother's hair, short and dark and curly, and then back at the butterfly.

"Like you," she said. "You came out new."

Angela laughed, and then she stopped laughing, and her eyes filled up. She pulled Mia into her lap. The butterfly opened and closed its wings twice, then lifted off and went out over the water toward the rainbow, and Mia waved at it until she couldn't see it anymore.

Ezra stood a few steps behind them in the wet sand. He was happy. Then he saw the back of Angela's neck, thin under the new curls, and he was afraid. He said nothing.

\* \* \*

In August, Angela's back began to hurt.

She blamed the beach chairs at first, and then the new mattress, and then her age. She told no one for three weeks. When she finally told Kambria, Kambria drove her to the hospital that same afternoon for scans.

The cancer was back. It was in her spine, her hips and her liver. Dr. Sandoval sat down across from them in the small consultation room, which was how Ezra knew before the doctor even spoke. Doctors only sat down for the worst news.

"Stage four," Dr. Sandoval said, her voice low and even. "We can treat it. We can buy time. But I want to be honest with you both about what we're treating toward."

Angela nodded. She was calm. She reached across the space between the chairs and took the doctor's hand. "Thank you for not dressing it up, Doctor," Angela said. She held the doctor's hand in both of hers. "I would rather know. Now tell me how we spend the time."

Ezra stood up, walked out of the room, and went down six flights of stairs to the parking garage. He sat in his truck for an hour and hammered the steering wheel with his fist until his knuckles were bruised purple. He didn't cry. He couldn't. Something in him had locked shut a long time ago, the day an Army carton came home to Old Ferry Road with his father's things in it, and his father's watch among them, stopped at 3:17. The watch had been on his wrist since he was sixteen. The carton sat on the shelf over his workbench, behind the paint cans, where he did not have to look at it.

When he finally went back upstairs, Angela was sitting in the waiting room with her purse on her lap, waiting for him. She didn't ask where he'd been. She just stood up, slipped her hand through his arm, and said, "Let's go home, honey. Let's go home and tell the kids."

\* \* \*

By October, the hospice nurses had set up a hospital bed in the living room, by the big back window that looked out on the yard and the studio. Angela had asked for that spot. She wanted to see the leaves turn, she said. She wanted to hear Jalen practice.

And the people came.

They came every day, all day, until Ezra lost count. Pastor Grant came every morning with his worn Bible and his pocket calendar, and told the family plainly what each day was likely to bring. Teachers from Angela's school came with casseroles. Her third graders sent a box of handmade cards so heavy the mail carrier had to bring it to the door. Rosa from the infusion center came with a knitted blanket. Lionel Hart, Jalen's teacher, came every evening after school and went straight out to the studio, and some nights Ezra could hear the two of them out there until late, the cello and the viola, playing for her through the open window. Kambria came every single evening after work and stayed until Angela fell asleep.

And every time someone came, Ezra watched his wife do the same thing.

She prayed for them.

She was weaker every week. By the end of October she could barely lift her head off the pillow. But when a visitor sat down beside the bed, Angela would ask them one question, "What can I pray about for you?", and then she would close her eyes and do it, out loud, by name, while they sat there with their coats still on.

She prayed for Pastor Grant's arthritis and his son in the Navy, while she was the one dying. She prayed a young teacher through a crying jag about a classroom she couldn't control. Elijah, Ezra's oldest friend, the man who joked his way through every hard thing in his life, sat down beside the bed with his cap in his lap, and Angela asked him her question, and Elijah opened his mouth to make a joke, and nothing came out. She prayed for him anyway. Ezra watched from the kitchen doorway as Elijah bowed his head and wept into his cap. She prayed over Lionel Hart, low and urgent, and the old man closed his eyes and nodded, and nodded again.

One morning, Pastor Grant found Ezra in the kitchen afterward, pouring coffee he wasn't going to drink. The old minister leaned against the counter and shook his head, a bemused smile on his face.

"You know what the Book of James says?" he asked, his voice low. "'Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord…' (James 5:14)" He nodded toward the living room. "That's why I come every morning, son. I'm the elder. I'm supposed to be praying over her." He let out a soft laugh. "And every single morning, she prays over me."

Ezra didn't answer. He stared down into his coffee.

"Thirty-five years I've been a pastor," Pastor Grant went on. "I've sat at a lot of deathbeds. I've never seen anybody less sick than that woman in there. Her body is failing. But her soul is the healthiest thing in this house." He rested a hand on Ezra's shoulder. "'…though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.' (2 Corinthians 4:16) That's her. She's not the sick one here."

He didn't say who was. He didn't have to. He squeezed Ezra's shoulder once and went out to his car, and Ezra stood alone in the kitchen.

He was the one with the strong back and the clean blood test at his last physical. And standing there with his coffee gone cold, listening to his dying wife pray over a crying stranger in the next room, he thought of his father at Pilgrim Rest, raising holy hands on Sunday morning that he would raise against his mother by Friday night. And the woman who was dying had been praying for his healing every day of their life together.

He remembered the dinner table: her head bowed over every meal for years, her left hand resting open on the cloth beside his plate. He had never once covered it. He'd told himself it was a church thing. His father's thing. And she had never once taken it back.

\* \* \*

On a Sunday in early November, Ezra's mother came.

Loretta was seventy now, with a voice that had sung alto in church choirs for fifty years. She had prayed for her son every morning, and especially the year his father left for the war and never came back. Ezra loved her, and he let most of her phone calls go to voicemail, and they both knew why.

She asked him to wait in the kitchen. He was surprised, but he went.

Through the doorway he watched his mother sit down beside Angela's bed and lean close to the pillow. Then she spoke too low for Ezra to hear.

Angela listened. Tears ran down her face. She nodded, and then she did something that raised the hair on the back of Ezra's neck. She picked the black notebook up off her lap, the one she wrote in every night, and pressed it against her chest with both arms.

When his mother came into the kitchen, Ezra asked her what it was.

Loretta looked at him. Grief was in her face, and an older thing under the grief. "Something between me and her," she said. "And between you and God, when you're ready."

She put her hand on his cheek. She hadn't done that since he was a boy. Then she picked up her handbag and went home.

\* \* \*

Two days later, on a cold, clear Tuesday afternoon, Angela asked for him.

The kids were at school. The hospice nurse had stepped out to the porch. The house was quiet except for the furnace and the slow tick of the kitchen clock. Ezra pulled a chair up beside the bed and sat down, and Angela turned her head on the pillow and studied him in silence. She was so thin now. Her eyes looked enormous in her face.

Then she reached under the blanket and drew out the black notebook and held it out to him with both hands.

It was heavy. The cover swung back an inch as he took its weight, and he saw a page of her round hand with a single word printed small in the margin, Selah, and beside it a Strong's number, and a box drawn around one line. He shut it. He had seen her writing in it for a year, late at night at the dinner table, but he had never asked what was inside. On the front cover, under the two words she had written there the night she bought it, she had added three more:

*For Ezra, when you're ready.*

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Everything I want you to know." Her voice was barely above a whisper. "Everything Kambria and I found. Everything I prayed for you."

"I'll read it tonight."

"No." She shook her head. "Not tonight. Not because I asked you to." She pressed it against his chest and held it there until he closed his arms around it. "When you're ready, you'll know. And it will be there. And when you finally reach for Him, honey, He'll send you the Comforter. You won't have to carry any of it by yourself."

He wanted to say something. He wanted to tell her he would be ready, that he would read every word, that he would become whoever she'd been praying he would become. But the words stuck behind the locked door, and all he could do was nod.

That night, after the kids were asleep, he stood in the front hallway turning the notebook over and over in his hands. Then he opened the hall closet, reached up to the top shelf, and pushed it all the way to the back, behind the winter coats and a box of Christmas lights, where he wouldn't have to see it.

He closed the closet door. He stood with his hand on the doorknob. He knew he was putting away the last thing she had ever asked of him.

\* \* \*

Angela began to leave them the week before Thanksgiving.

Kambria saw it first. She had come straight from the lab, and she stood at the foot of the bed in her coat, listening to Angela's breathing. Years in hospital wards had taught her that sound, and Ezra watched her face change. She said nothing for a moment. Then she came and put her hand on his shoulder.

"It's going to be tonight," she said. "Maybe early morning. You should call your mother. And wake the kids if you want them here."

They sat with her through the night. Loretta came and sat by the window, praying under her breath with her eyes closed. Mia, five years old, fell asleep curled up at the foot of her mother's bed. Jalen sat in the corner with his cello and played barely louder than a whisper: an old setting of the hundred and twenty-first psalm at first, and then, near two in the morning, the melody that was his own. It climbed, searching. It fell back. It climbed again, higher than ever before, and held there, trembling, and stopped. It still didn't have an ending.

Toward morning, Kambria touched Ezra's arm and nodded toward the bed. Then she gathered up Loretta and Jalen and led them into the kitchen, and the room was empty except for Ezra and his wife and his sleeping daughter.

Angela's breathing had grown slow and shallow, with long pauses in between. Ezra sat down in the chair where a hundred visitors had sat, and kept his hands on his knees.

Then her hand moved on the blanket.

So weak he thought he'd imagined it. The fingers stirred, and uncurled, and lay open on the wool, turned toward him.

Ezra looked at it, and every grace he had ever sat through came down on him at once: the bowed heads, the open hand beside his plate, his own hands kept in his lap like evidence.

He took her hand in both of his.

Her fingers were cool and light as paper, and they closed. With whatever she had left, Angela held on.

He had cried once since he was eight years old, on the floor of a studio he built with his son, and never in front of her. It broke out of him now, all of it, and he bent over their hands with his shoulders shaking.

"I'm here," he whispered. "Angie. I'm right here."

He didn't know how to pray. But from somewhere under thirty years of silence, from the front pew at Pilgrim Rest, the old words came up on their own, and he heard his own voice say them out loud, cracked down the middle, to the God he had refused at that table every night of their marriage.

"'*The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters. He restoreth my soul…*'" (Psalm 23:1–3)

He lost the next line. He could not see.

Her lips moved. Her eyes stayed closed and her voice was only breath, but he bent close and heard her finish it for him.

"*…he leadeth me in the paths of righteousness for his name's sake. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me…*" (Psalm 23:3–4)

She drew one more breath and spent it on His name. "Jesus."

Then her eyes opened, once, and found him, and the smile started on one side, slow, the way it had at the fair with sugar on her fingers, and took its time getting to the other. It cost her everything. She gave it to him anyway.

They were the last words she said, and the last thing she gave him.

Her breathing slowed. It paused. He waited for the next breath, and waited, and it didn't come. Outside the big back window, the sky over the studio was beginning to pale. Mia slept on at the foot of the bed. In the kitchen, his mother was weeping into a dish towel.

Her fingers were still closed around his. He didn't move. He sat there with her hand in both of his until the window went from gray to white, as if, for as long as he held on, there was still something to hold.

\* \* \*

They buried her that week, on a cold, bright morning, beside the church where she had taught Sunday school for fifteen years.

The day began with Mia's hair.

Mia came downstairs in her black velvet dress with a comb in one hand and two yellow ribbons in the other and held them up to her father. Two puffs. Angela had done them every morning of her life: part down the middle, a dab of the coconut cream, two puffs high and even, a yellow ribbon on each. Ezra had watched it a thousand times from the kitchen doorway. He had never once done it.

He tried. His big carpenter's hands, that could set a door plumb by eye, could not make the parts come straight. The left puff came out lopsided and low. Mia looked at herself in the hall mirror and her chin began to shake, and she said, very quietly, "That's not how Mommy does it," and Ezra stood behind her with the comb in his hand and had nothing to say to that.

Kambria redid them in the church bathroom, kneeling on the tile in her good stockings, while Mia held perfectly still and watched her in the mirror to make sure she got it right.

The church was full. Angela's third graders sat in the front rows on the left, in their winter coats, every one of them holding a card. A boy in the second row waved at the casket when they wheeled it in, a small, hopeful wave, and his teacher put her hand on his arm and he put his hand down.

Joe and Pat had driven down from Erie through the night. Joe sat on the aisle in a dark suit and a pressed checked shirt, his silver hair combed straight back, and he did not move once during the whole service. He looked at the casket the way he had looked at every joint Ezra ever cut in that house: as if he expected it to be true. Pat sat beside him in her small gold cross, and every few minutes she leaned her shoulder against his, and he let her.

Pastor Grant preached from Proverbs 31. He read it slowly. When he came to the twenty-eighth verse he stopped and looked up from the pulpit at the second pew.

"'*Her children arise up, and call her blessed.*'" (Proverbs 31:28)

Mia stood up.

She stood up on the pew in her velvet dress and her two yellow ribbons, because she was five, and the pastor had said *arise*, and she had always done what the Bible said. She stood there in front of three hundred people, waiting to be told what to say.

The whole church came apart. Somebody in the back let out a sound that wasn't a word. Pat put both hands over her mouth. Pastor Grant held on to the pulpit and couldn't go on, and for a long time the only sound in that sanctuary was grown people trying not to be heard.

Jalen reached up and took his sister around the waist and lifted her down into his lap, and held her there, with his chin on top of her head and the ribbons against his face, and stared straight ahead at the casket.

Ezra sat on the other side of them and did not move.

At the grave, Jalen played "Amazing Grace." He made it through the first verse and the second. On the third, *Through many dangers, toils, and snares*, the bow caught on the string and stopped, and he stood there in the cold with the cello against his knee and his face working, and could not find the note. Behind him, Mr. Hart lifted his viola and played the line, quietly, one bar, until Jalen found it again and took it back from him. He finished it alone.

When they lowered the casket, Mia pulled on Kambria's coat.

"It's cold down there," she said. "Mommy doesn't have her blanket."

Nobody answered her. Kambria knelt down on the frozen grass and held her, and Mia let herself be held, and kept watching the hole.

Ezra didn't cry at the grave. Elijah stood at the back with his cap crushed in his fist and not a joke in him, watching his friend's face, and later he told his own mother it was the worst thing he had ever seen: a man at his wife's funeral with a face like a house after the power's been cut.

\* \* \*

That night the house was full of food nobody had cooked and silence nobody could fill.

Mia would not go to bed. Ezra carried her up at nine, still in the velvet dress, the yellow ribbons crushed flat from the car seat, and she climbed out of his arms and knelt down on the rug beside her bed, the way she had every night of her life, and folded her hands on the quilt.

Then she looked up at him and waited.

Every night it had been Angela down there on the rug beside her. Two sets of folded hands. *Now I lay me down to sleep*, and then the long list: Daddy, Jalen, Grandma Loretta, Grandma Pat and Pop Joe, Miss Kambria, Mr. Walt, the boy at school who cries, the dog on Otis Street. And last, every night, Angela laid her hand on top of Mia's head and blessed her with the old words from Numbers, the priest's blessing, and kissed her where the two puffs met and said, *Goodnight, my love.* Ezra had heard it a thousand times through the wall. He had never once come in.

"Mommy always started it," Mia said. "So it's your turn."

He knelt. His knees cracked on the floorboards. He looked at her small brown hands pressed together, the knuckles still sticky from the funeral cake, and he opened his mouth.

Nothing.

He tried again. The words were right there, *Now I lay me*, five words, a child could say them, his child said them, and they would not come out of him. They sat in his throat like a stone in a pipe.

Mia waited. She was so patient. She had learned that from her mother too.

Ezra leaned forward and kissed her on the forehead, right at the part where the two puffs met, and when he pulled back his face was wet and he couldn't stop it.

"Maybe you can do a prayer for me instead, baby," he whispered.

Mia looked at him for a moment, very seriously. Then she got up off her knees.

He was still kneeling, so for once they were the same height. She stepped in close and reached up and laid her small hand flat on top of his head, the way her mother had laid hers on Mia's every night of her life. Her palm was warm and a little sticky. He felt it there, and he could not breathe.

"*The LORD bless thee, and keep thee,*" Mia said.

She said it in Angela's rhythm. Not her own sing-song: her mother's. The same pauses in the same places, the same little lift on *keep*.

"*The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.*" (Numbers 6:24–26)

Then she leaned forward and kissed him on the top of the head, right where her hand had been, and said, in her mother's voice, "Goodnight, my love."

Something gave way in his chest like a floor.

He got to his feet somehow. He tucked her in with hands that would not hold still. He walked out. He pulled her door shut behind him, somberly, his heart aching for his children, and stood in the dark hallway with his hand on the knob.

Through the wood he heard her start her list, alone, slower than usual. Jalen. Grandma Loretta. Grandma Pat and Pop Joe. Then a long pause.

"And God, please help my daddy. He forgot the words."

He let go of the knob.

He went down the stairs in the dark. The hospice company had come for the bed while they were at the cemetery, and where it had stood for seven weeks there was a rectangle pressed into the living-room carpet, four deep dents from the wheels, the pile flattened pale in the shape of where she had been. He stood at the edge of it and could not step in.

He took out his phone and found it without looking: a video, nine seconds long, that Angela had sent him from the sixth floor during her fourth round of chemo. He had been sitting in the waiting room at the time, in the gray vinyl chairs. She had known exactly where he was, and exactly why he couldn't come in. He had watched it once, felt something catch in his throat, and put the phone face down on his knee. He had never answered her.

He cast it to the big television across from the rectangle in the carpet, turned off every lamp in the house, knelt on the floor in front of the screen, and pressed play.

Her face filled the dark room, close and a little blurry, lit gold by the lamp beside the infusion recliner. Her glasses had slid down her nose. She was wearing the soft purple scarf. Behind her, faintly, Mr. Harold grumbled at his crossword.

"Hey, honey," she said. "It's me. I know you're out there worrying. Stop it. I'm okay." She tipped her head and gave him the look over her glasses she gave her third graders when they were being silly. "God's got me. And I've got you." Then the smile broke, wide and bright and completely unafraid, starting on one side and taking its time getting to the other, and she laughed, and her hand came toward the camera, and the video ended on her face in the middle of that laugh.

Nine seconds.

He played it again. Again. On the fourth time through, the thing in his chest that had held since the hospice bed let go all at once. When her hand came toward the camera he leaned forward and pressed his face against the glass, and for one blurred second her hand was on his cheek, and Ezra Patterson broke open like a dropped jar.

He cried for her smile. He cried for every August and every paper plate of fried dough. He cried for every grace he had sat through with his hands in his lap, for the one prayer he had managed at the very end when there was nothing left to give her, and for the five words he could not give his daughter tonight. He cried with his face against the cold screen until the glass was wet beneath his cheek and there was nothing left in him at all.

*Can you hear me, Angie?*

*I keep looking for you. I know you're gone. I stood at your grave this morning and watched them lower you down, and I still look for you. I hear a car door and think it's you home from school. I smell coffee and think you made it. I reach across the bed for a hand that isn't there.*

*Since you left, I've been traveling somewhere I've never been before. I don't know what to call it. It's a shadowed country, and the roads all go down. There are signs along the way, and I've read them all. Grief. Then anger. Then a long road called Nothing, where I can't feel anything at all. The dark there has a weight to it, a pull, like gravity. It holds me down in the mornings. It won't let me get up. I keep walking, looking for you, but you're not here, Angie. I've looked everywhere. This isn't where you went.*

*I remember your hand on the blanket that last night. Opening. Reaching for me. I finally took it, and you held on. But I keep asking the same thing, over and over, in the dark.*

*Did you reach for my hand as I reached for yours? Or were you reaching for Someone else, Someone I couldn't see, standing on the other side of the bed?*

*And if you were, why won't He reach for me?*

A floorboard creaked behind him.

Mia stood at the foot of the stairs in her yellow nightgown with her teddy bear hanging from one hand, the ribbons gone, her hair loose. He could not tell how long she had been there. He made no move to wipe his face. She crossed the dark room in her bare feet, stepped right into the rectangle in the carpet without stopping, stood beside him, looked at the frozen picture of her mother, and said, "Play the part where she laughs."

He played it. Mia watched with her mouth a little open, and when Angela's hand came toward the camera, Mia lifted her own hand and laid it flat against the glass, over her mother's, beside his wet cheek. Neither of them spoke. When it ended she climbed into his lap, bear and all, and was asleep before it could start again, and he sat on the floor in the dark with her until his legs went numb.

At the top of the stairs, Jalen stood in his socks and watched them both. He didn't come down. After a while he went back to his room and closed the door very quietly, so his father would never know he had seen.

In the morning, before anyone was up, Ezra wiped his face off the television with his sleeve. A small handprint stayed low on the glass. He could not make himself clean it.

He carried his coffee down the front hallway and stopped in front of the closet. Behind it, on the top shelf, behind the winter coats, was her notebook, with his name on the cover in her hand. He did not open the door.

He would not reach to that shelf again for a year. And by the time he did, his children would be gone.
