## Chapter 3: The Sixth Floor

> *The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit.* — Psalm 34:18

**Word study: *contrite* — דַּכָּא** Hebrew *dakka'* (Strong's H1793). Crushed, broken to pieces, ground into powder. Its root is used for crushing something into dust. God is near to the one who has been crushed so fine there is nothing left of him to hold together.

When Job's three friends heard of all the evil that had come upon him, they came from their own places to mourn with him. They did not know him at first; he was sitting in the ashes, scraping his sores with a broken piece of pottery. So they tore their robes and threw dust upon their heads, and sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights. And none spoke a word unto him, for they saw that his grief was very great. (Job 2:8, 11–13)

\* \* \*

The word, when it came, was a long one. Invasive ductal carcinoma. Stage three. Two lymph nodes.

Dr. Sandoval folded her hands on the desk.

"Invasive ductal carcinoma," Angela said slowly. "Would you spell that for me? I want to write it down." Kambria watched her print each letter in the black notebook in her neat, round handwriting. Angela wanted to know the name of what she was fighting.

"Chemotherapy every other Tuesday for months," the doctor said. "Then surgery, then radiation." The oncologist said the word *aggressive* four times, once about the cancer and three times about the treatment. She said *hopeful* once.

In the car afterward, Angela sat with the notebook closed on her lap and watched the October trees go by.

"Months," she said. "That's the whole school year." Then, after a while: "I'm going to need a really good substitute."

Kambria heard the tremble in those words and kept driving.

\* \* \*

The infusion center was on the sixth floor of the hospital whose lab sent the Centers for Disease Control its samples, where Kambria knew half the staff by name. It sat at the end of a long hallway that smelled of hand sanitizer and burnt coffee: a wide, bright room ringed with recliners, each one with its own IV pole and its own small window onto the city. On Tuesdays it filled with the same quiet company: a retired bus driver named Mr. Harold who did crossword puzzles in pen; a young mother named Rosa who knitted hats for her twins; a teenage boy with headphones who never spoke.

Kambria took every other Tuesday off from the lab, out of leave she had been saving for years. Nobody asked her to. Nobody had to.

The Tuesday volunteer on the sixth floor was named Celeste Moore.

She had started on the sixth floor that September as a volunteer in the hospital's Patient Companions program, Tuesday mornings only, and by October the whole floor was half in love with her. When anyone asked what she did the rest of the week, she said she worked in public health, mostly paperwork, and changed the subject. She remembered every patient's birthday, and their children's names, and which of the techs took their coffee with oat milk. She had red hair that she pinned up for her visits and let down the minute they ended, and she knew exactly what it did to a room. She laughed at their jokes and touched their arms and called the older patients "sweetheart," and all the while her eyes kept count. At her throat she wore a small gold pendant shaped like a tree: ten tiny circles joined by lines, climbing from the roots to the crown. On her left wrist she wore a thin red string, knotted three times. When one of the residents asked her about it, she told him it was Rahab's scarlet cord, from the Book of Joshua: the outsider who was brought inside when the walls came down. "We're all inside now," she said, and smiled. She had two hundred thousand followers online, where she posted photographs of herself in the morning light at her front window, in a robe slipping off one shoulder or a top cut low, because the more she showed, the more they answered. She posted soft-spoken videos about self-love and letting go of guilt, filmed in front of a framed drawing of the same tree. The Tree of Life, she called it. *Nobody has to die for you,* she told her followers. *You climb. Your joy is holy. God wants you to have it. All of it.*

Kambria had been prepared not to like her. A biochemistry degree and years of tracing outbreaks through hospitals had left her suspicious of anyone who smiled that much at seven in the morning.

But Celeste was good at it. Kambria watched her warm Angela's blanket in the cart dryer before she tucked it around her knees, and remember which tea Angela could keep down, and bring a second blanket without being asked. Kambria could not find anything wrong with her, and she tried. The trouble was that every kindness arrived a moment too well timed, like a result that comes back too clean.

By the third session, the two women had brought the Thursday-night study to the sixth floor. Angela would settle into her recliner with a warm blanket over her knees and the bag of red medicine dripping into the port below her collarbone, and Kambria would pull up a plastic chair and open the notebooks on the rolling tray table between them. They read Psalms mostly, now. The prophecies could wait.

"'Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death,'" Angela read one morning, "'I will fear no evil.'" She paused and looked around the room at the IV poles standing all around them. "Well. Here's the valley."

Mr. Harold looked up from his crossword. "Keep going," he said gruffly. "I like that one."

At the next session, Loretta came.

Ezra's mother stood at the glass doors at half past nine in her church coat, small and straight-backed, with a foil-covered pan held level in both hands like something that might spill. Before the doors had finished closing behind her, the smell reached the recliners: warm flour and butter, a smell that had no business on a floor that ran on hand sanitizer and burnt coffee. Heads turned. Rosa lowered her knitting. Even the teenage boy slid his headphones down around his neck.

"I know you can't eat much, baby," Loretta told Angela, folding back the foil. "But you can smell them. And you can try one."

They were dinner rolls, golden on top and shining with butter, packed shoulder to shoulder in the pan. Loretta had baked them every Sunday of Ezra's life from a recipe she had never written down and never given away, not to the church mothers who had begged her for it for forty years, and not even to Angela, who had asked more than once. *Some things you keep,* she always said, and smiled, and changed the subject. Angela pulled one apart, and it came away in soft, flaky layers with the steam still rising from the middle, buttery and smooth on her tongue. It was the first thing in two days that had not turned her stomach.

"Mama Loretta," she said, and for a moment that was all she could say.

Loretta did not stay by that chair. She went around the room with the pan, chair to chair, the way she had gone down the pews with the offering plate for fifty years. Mr. Harold took two and set down his pen to eat them. "Lord have mercy," he said, with his mouth full, and then, to no one, "My mother used to make these." Rosa wrapped one in a napkin for her twins. The teenage boy ate three without a word and then said, very quietly, "Thank you, ma'am," and it was the first thing anyone on that floor had heard him say. The nurses came by in twos on their way past the desk and left with their hands full.

Celeste took one, with a warm smile and a murmur of thanks. Kambria noticed afterward that she had held it the whole morning and never taken a bite.

So Angela kept reading. At the next session Rosa asked if they could hear the psalm again. At the one after that, the boy with the headphones took them off.

One Tuesday that fall, Kambria brought a question instead of a psalm.

She had been up since four o'clock reading Genesis 5, the long list of fathers and sons, and every name on it ended the same way. *And he died. And he died. And he died.* Hundreds of years, and then a funeral. Except for one man in the middle of the list.

"'And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him.'" She turned her Bible around on the tray table so Angela could see it. "Everybody else in this chapter dies, Angie. Everybody. Except him."

Angela read it with the blanket pulled up to her chest. The red medicine went on dripping. "Hebrews tells you why," she said. She always knew where everything was. "Chapter eleven."

Kambria found it. "'By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death... for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God.'" She looked up. "He walked with God, and he pleased Him, and God just took him. He never saw death."

"Keep going," Angela said. "Jude."

Jude was one page long, tucked in right before Revelation, and Kambria had read it maybe twice in her life. Near the bottom she found him again. "'*And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all…*'" (Jude 14–15) She read it again to herself. Then she flipped back to Genesis 5 and ran her finger down every line of it.

She sat back. "He saw it," she said. "Before the flood. Before Noah ever picked up a hammer, Enoch saw the Lord coming back with His saints."

"And then God took him," Angela said. "Before the flood ever came." She smiled, tired. "Noah went through the water, Kam. Enoch never saw a drop of it."

Kambria wrote it in the margin of her notebook, in ink, in her precise hand: *Enoch. Taken before. Hebrews 11:5. Jude 14–15.* That was all the Bible said about him, and it was enough. She dated the entry in the corner and numbered the page, as she numbered every page. Angela's pages had no numbers. "The Lord keeps count," she said, when Kambria offered her the pen, and she went on turning her own pages by the corners.

Later that afternoon, before she closed the notebook, she turned back one more time to the man in Genesis 5 who walked with God and was not, and she looked across the tray table at Angela, asleep in the recliner with the line in her chest. She did not write down what she prayed. She said it under her breath, so low it hardly moved the air.

"Father, if You are going to take her, take her the way You took Enoch. Walking. Not through the water. Please."

\* \* \*

On the second Tuesday in November, Celeste came by Angela's recliner on her lunch break with a thermos of golden turmeric tea and a little book wrapped in tissue paper. It was a paperback of daily affirmations, with the gold tree on the cover.

"It's what got me through my divorce," she said, unfolding the tissue. "You don't need anybody to save you, Angela. You just need to believe in your own light."

Angela laid her hand over Celeste's. "Honey, that is the sweetest thing," she said. "But I already have Somebody who saved me." She folded the book back into its tissue paper and closed Celeste's fingers around it, and she held on to Celeste's hand a little longer. "Everything I need was finished on a cross. You keep this. But I'm going to pray for you. Is that all right?"

Celeste laughed, surprised, and tucked the little book into the pocket of her cardigan. "Of course it is."

Celeste perched on the arm of the empty recliner and looked at the open Bible on the tray table with real tenderness. "Oh, I love that you have your practice," she said. "I think it's all the same light, really. All paths up the same mountain. Whatever brings you peace."

Kambria felt the words before she said them. "Jesus said, '*I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.*'" (John 14:6)

Celeste turned and gave her a smile of such untroubled sweetness that Kambria felt, absurdly, that she had been unkind.

"That's beautiful," Celeste said. "Well, that's your path." Then, lightly: "Angela says you work in a lab. What do you actually test, if you don't mind my asking?"

"Outbreaks," Kambria said. "Mostly lettuce."

Celeste's eyes went to the government badge clipped to her coat. She read the name on it, then the department under the name, and only then looked up.

Celeste laughed and asked nothing more. It was only afterward that Kambria noticed she herself had answered exactly the question and not one word past it.

On her way out, she paused at the double glass doors and glanced down the hall toward the waiting room. "Is that him?" she murmured to Kambria. "The husband? In the work jacket?"

Kambria hesitated, then nodded.

"He looks so alone," Celeste said, and let the doors swing shut behind her.

That night, in the parking garage, Kambria opened her laptop on the passenger seat and looked again at the thirty bases from Ohio, and found she had no wish to mention them to anyone, including her own supervisor. She kept hearing it, too: *Is that him?* As if Celeste had been expecting someone.

\* \* \*

Angela's hair began to fall out in November.

She found the first handful on her pillow on a Sunday morning and sat up in bed with it cupped in her palm until the room grew light. Then she walked into the kitchen, handed Ezra the clippers from the bathroom drawer, and said, "Get it over with."

He stood behind her chair holding them. Kambria, who had come over with groceries, watched from the doorway as his hands shook. Then he steadied them.

Mia came in to them. She climbed into her mother's lap, studied her face seriously, and announced, "Mommy's going to look like a baby bird." And Angela laughed, and Ezra laughed, a hoarse, startled bark, and the clippers buzzed to life.

When it was done, Jalen came in from the studio, took one look at his mother, took the clippers out of his father's hand, and shaved his own head right there at the kitchen sink without a word.

Ezra stared at his son. Kambria thought he might say something. Then he turned and went out the back door, and through the window she watched him walk to the end of the yard and stand in front of the little studio he had built, with his palm against its wall, until his arm grew tired.

\* \* \*

Ezra drove Angela to every session.

He took the whole day off every other Tuesday. He had not missed a day of work in nineteen years. He carried her bag, signed her in, kissed her forehead at the double glass doors, and then he went back out to the waiting room and sat there for five hours. He never went in. He told himself it was because they only allowed one visitor, and Kambria was already there.

The truth was that he had tried, once, on the first Tuesday. He had made it as far as the doorway, seen his wife in the recliner with the line running into her chest, felt his legs nearly give out, and walked back out before she saw his face.

So he sat in the gray vinyl chairs in his work jacket, scrolling through his phone without seeing it, while the television bolted above the vending machines played the news with the sound off. The captions trailed a few seconds behind the pictures and misspelled half the names. He read them because it was easier than thinking. An earthquake in Turkey, the second in a month. A salmonella outbreak traced to a farm in Iowa, and a hundred million eggs pulled off the shelves. A woman across the room shook her head at the screen. "Lord, what is this world coming to," she said, and the room let it pass.

Everyone asked about Angela. His foreman asked. His mother called every night and asked. Pastor Grant from Angela's church left voicemails every Sunday, and Ezra deleted them without listening. *How's Angela? How's she holding up? Tell her we're praying.* Nobody asked how he was. He would have hated it if they had.

On the last Tuesday in November, a woman in a navy cardigan with a volunteer badge clipped to it sat down two chairs away from him with a coffee in each hand.

"You're the carpenter," she said.

He looked up. Red hair pinned up in a loose knot, with a few strands falling around her face. A face he'd seen somewhere.

"Rooney's," she said. "Back in October. My friend's birthday. You were at the bar with the funny one." She smiled. "I'm Celeste. I volunteer on Angela's floor." She held out one of the coffees. "I didn't know how you take it, so I guessed black. You look like a black-coffee guy."

He was. He took it.

She didn't ask about Angela. She looked him in the eye. No one had done that in weeks. She said, "How are *you* doing?"

He opened his mouth to say *fine*. What came out was, "I don't know."

She nodded. "That's allowed."

That was all. She quoted no scripture and said nothing of God's plan. Angela's church friends treated him like a project, and Kambria treated him like a lost cause. Celeste did neither. She stayed ten minutes, talked about nothing, laughed at his one bad joke, and returned to the floor.

It wasn't until she was gone that he realized he had told her about his father. He never told anyone about his father.

Two weeks later, she came again. And at the session after that.

On the third visit, she told him she had looked up his name. "Ezra means help," she said. "Did you know that? You were named to be a help. But who helps you?" Ezra snorted. But she only smiled and laid two fingers on his wrist, right over his father's stopped watch, and left them there longer than she needed to.

"Everything that breaks is waiting to be repaired," she said. "That's the whole secret. Nobody has to die for you. You climb. You just have to let yourself want something again."

He didn't believe in any of it. He'd grown up under a man who quoted the Bible at the deacons' table every Sunday and drank it all away every Friday night, and some old reflex in him recoiled from the talk of light and the little gold tree at her throat. But sitting there in the gray vinyl chair, with her fingers resting on his wrist, he did not move his arm.

What he could not shake was the question. *Who helps you?* He had asked it of himself once, in a cold back stairwell at Coretta Elementary, with his knees pulled up, and worked out the answer alone. No one. He had gone on giving the same answer for thirty years, and it had cost him a great deal, and he had yet to hear it spoken aloud.

He started noticing, without meaning to, when the elevator doors opened.

He told himself it was nothing. A kind woman being kind. That was all.

\* \* \*

On the first Saturday in December, the infusion center held its holiday open house, and Jalen brought his cello. Mr. Hart, the retired violist who had taught him since he was nine, came with him and brought his viola, and played a quiet harmony underneath him.

Jalen was sixteen, thin as a bow stick, and painfully shy, and he set up in the corner by the window without looking at anyone. The nurses had hung paper snowflakes from the ceiling. Mr. Harold wore a reindeer sweater. Angela, in her soft purple scarf, was partway through her chemotherapy. Purple had always been her favorite color.

Jalen drew the bow across the strings, and every conversation in the room stopped mid-word.

He played "Amazing Grace." He played it unhurried, exactly as it was written, and the deep, warm voice of the cello filled the bright room and seemed to fill the hallway beyond it, because nurses began drifting to the doorway one by one and stopping there. Rosa put down her knitting. Mr. Harold took off his reading glasses and pressed his thumb and finger against his eyes.

And from her chair, thin and bright in a borrowed sweater, Angela began to sing.

*Amazing grace, how sweet the sound, that saved a wretch like me...*

Her voice was thin from the chemo. It cracked on the high notes. She sang anyway. By the third verse, half the room was singing with her, and Kambria couldn't sing at all.

When the song was over, nobody clapped. It was not a song to clap after. Jalen sat with his bow resting on his knee, and then, almost to himself, he began to play another piece.

Kambria had never heard it before. It began low, on the cello's deepest string, slow and grieving. It climbed a little, searching, then fell back. Climbed again, a few notes higher this time, held there, trembling.

Then it stopped. Not ended; stopped. In the middle of a phrase, unfinished.

"What was that?" Angela asked, hushed.

Jalen flushed to the tips of his ears. "Nothing. Something I'm working on."

"It's beautiful. What's it called?"

"It's got a name. I'm just not telling anybody yet." He hesitated. "It doesn't have an ending yet either. I can't figure out where it goes."

Angela looked at her son, and Kambria kept that look with her ever after.

"It'll come," she said. "When it does, play it for your father."

Across the room, Mr. Hart sat with his viola across his knee. He caught Jalen's eye and nodded once, as he did at lessons whenever the boy played true.

Later, while the nurses were taking down the paper snowflakes, Kambria saw Angela beckon him over to her recliner. The old man bent low to hear her. Angela asked him a question, and he smiled and nodded.

"He blows a ram's horn at his church every Easter, at the sunrise service," Angela told her when he had gone. "A shofar. He brought it home from Jerusalem when the orchestra played there." She looked across the room at Jalen, who was loosening his bow with his head down. "I asked him to take us this Easter, if I'm still able to go. I want my boy to hear what it's going to sound like."

Kambria didn't have to ask what she meant. They had read it together at the dinner table more times than either of them could count. *…for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.* (1 Corinthians 15:52)

\* \* \*

On the second Tuesday of December, Kambria went out to the vending machines and stopped short in the doorway of the waiting room.

Celeste was sitting beside him again. She had been stopping by the waiting room on her breaks for weeks now; Kambria had seen her once or twice and told herself it was kindness.

She had two coffees, one in each hand, and she was holding one out to him, and Ezra was taking it. She was murmuring, low and warm, and leaning in a little. She leaned in toward everyone. And Ezra was laughing.

Kambria hadn't heard him laugh since the morning of the clippers. Before that, she couldn't remember. It was a tired, surprised laugh.

Celeste laid her hand on his forearm, lightly, just for a moment. She did that with everyone.

Then she took it away.

Kambria stopped at the threshold with her coins in her hand and felt the same dread she got at her bench when a sample she expected to be clean came back dirty.

She went back inside without her soda and said nothing. There was nothing to say yet. A volunteer had brought a tired man a cup of coffee. That was all.

But Celeste was not looking at Ezra as she looked at the patients. There had been hunger in her face. Kambria had seen it before, on other women in other waiting rooms.

But when she sat back down beside Angela's recliner, Angela was looking past her, through the glass doors and down the hallway, to where the corner of the waiting room was just visible. Her face was calm. Her hands were folded on the blanket over her knees, and her lips were moving. She was praying, and Kambria knew without asking that she was praying for both of them.

Angela said nothing either.

That night, after Ezra and the children were asleep, Angela sat alone at the dinner table in her purple scarf and opened the black notebook. She turned past the pages she had already filled, past the signs and the dates and the verses she and Kambria had copied out together, to a fresh page.

At the top she wrote: *Proverbs 7.*

Beneath it, after a pause, she wrote her husband's name.

Then she bowed her head over the page.

"Father," she whispered, "he's tired and he's hurting, and she knows it. Don't let him walk through that door. Protect his mind and his ear gates, Lord, and lead him in the paths of righteousness, whatever it costs me." She was quiet a long moment. "And her too. Somebody sent her. Let her be found."
