## Chapter 24: I Never Knew You

> *And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.* — Jesus, Matthew 7:23

**Word study: *knew* — ἔγνων** Greek *egnon*, from *ginōskō* (Strong's G1097). To know by experience, the way a husband knows a wife and a friend knows a friend. Not to know about. The men in this verse knew a great deal about God. They had preached in His name. What they never had was Him.

*Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the LORD.* (1 Samuel 2:12)

\* \* \*

Ezra sat at the dinner table in the morning light with Angela's notebook under his hands, and his hands had nothing to do.

He had barely slept, and he was not tired. He had expected to be. Last night he had gotten down on this kitchen floor and said a thing he had never said in his life, and he had half expected that by morning it would have gone sour in him, as every good feeling always had. It had not. Nothing had come to take it back.

For thirty-five years he had believed that God was his father. Not the Father Angela talked about, not the One in her prayers. His father. Calvin Patterson. A big voice in public and a cold one at home and a back walking out the door to a bus. He had believed it so deeply that he had never once put it into words, even to himself. *God is loud on Sunday and cruel on Friday, and when you need Him, He leaves.* It had been the floor under every other thing he believed. It was why he sat on his hands in the front pew at eight. It was why he had sworn never to lift them. It was why, when Angela died with her hands open, he had decided that God leaves too.

And last night, when he had been as low as a man can get, God had not been loud, and He had not been cruel, and He had not left.

The floor was gone.

He sat with nothing under him, and he was not falling. That was the strangest part. He had thought, if he ever let that floor go, he would fall forever. He was not falling. Something was holding him up, and he knew what it was, because it had taken hold of his hands on this same kitchen floor the night before.

He studied his hands on the notebook.

There was a Bible on the table, Angela's, the soft black one with the cracked spine and the ribbons. He opened it to the back, to Ezekiel, because he remembered a verse his mother used to say when he was a teenager and furious at his father, a verse he had always thought was about someone else. It took him a while to find it. His hands were clumsy with the thin pages. Then he found it, in chapter eighteen, and someone had already underlined it, in blue ink, in Angela's hand.

*…The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.* (Ezekiel 18:20)

In the margin beside it, in Angela's round letters: *For Ezra. You are not him.*

And under that, smaller, in a different pen, added later: *And neither is God.*

\* \* \*

He drove to Arlington in the afternoon.

He never exactly decided to. He needed out of the house, and he got in the truck and found himself driving south across the city toward the river, and he let himself keep driving, because he understood where he was going and why. He had not been to his father's grave since he was nineteen, when he had gone once, alone, to shout at it.

The city was strange in the daylight. Half the traffic lights were dark, and at every intersection the drivers crept through one at a time, looking at each other, waving each other on, with a courtesy he had never seen in Washington in his life. There were soldiers at the bridges now, in Organization armbands, with clipboards, stopping every car. A young one with a tired face looked at his license and asked where he was going. Behind him, a man in a gray overcoat, with a small silver pin of the Technate on his lapel, was watching the truck instead of the paperwork. Ezra had last seen that face in the long mirror at Rooney's, over a glass of club soda.

"Arlington," Ezra said. "My father's grave."

The soldier looked him over. Then he handed the license back and waved him on without writing anything down. "Lot of people going out there," he said. "Lot of people."

Ezra understood what he meant when he came over the rise on Memorial Drive and saw the cemetery.

The white headstones ran away over the green hills in their rows, as they always had, thousands upon thousands of them, straight and even, all the way to the trees. And among the rows, everywhere he looked, scattered over the hillsides, were the holes.

Open graves. Hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. The earth heaped neatly beside each one, the white stones still standing at their heads. Here one, there one, and between them a whole row untouched, and then three together, and then a long stretch with none. People were walking among them. Families, mostly, in coats, moving up and down the rows with maps and phones, stopping at a stone and bending to read it and standing there. Some of them were crying. Some of them were kneeling at the edges of the open graves with their hands over their mouths. A man in an Army uniform, an old one, with medals on the chest, was sitting on the grass beside an open grave near the road with his legs stretched out in front of him and his cap in his lap, staring into it.

Ezra parked by the road and sat with the engine off.

Then he got out and walked.

\* \* \*

It took him an hour to find it. He had the section number from the funeral home papers his mother had kept in a shoebox, and he had to ask a groundskeeper, a young woman in a green jacket who walked with him part of the way and told him, without being asked, that she had been here on Thursday morning, early, and had seen it happen, and that she had not slept since.

"They just came up," she said. "Out of the ground. Into the light. I watched them go up." She stopped at the end of a row and pointed. "Your section's down there. Third row from the end." She looked at him. "I hope he went up, sir. I hope yours went up."

Ezra walked down the row.

The stone was like all the others: plain white marble, rounded at the top, with the cross carved in a circle above the lettering. He read it standing at the foot of the grave.

*CALVIN JAMES PATTERSON. SFC. US ARMY. PERSIAN GULF. JUN 4 1949. FEB 24 1991.*

The grass in front of it was whole.

On either side of it, two graves down on the left and one on the right, the earth was open. He could see the broken lids at the bottom. But in front of his father's stone the grass was green and unbroken and grown over, as it had been all those years.

Ezra stood and looked at it.

He had come out here, he understood now, hoping. He had gotten in the truck and driven across the river hoping that he would find the ground open. That in the last hour, in the dark before the shells came, his father had cried out to God, and been heard, and gone up on Thursday morning with the rest.

The grass was whole.

The grass would not say. That was the thing he would hold on to, later, on the nights he thought about it. He did not know. The Bible said the dead in Christ would rise first. What his father had done in the last hours, what God had heard out in the sand or in the dark before the guns, he could not say. He knew only that his father had known about God all his life, and that no one living could say whether he had ever let Him in.

He was not the judge of that. He had never been the judge of it, though he had judged his father every day since.

*…Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?* (Genesis 18:25)

He knelt down on the grass in front of the stone.

He had not knelt for his father in his life. He knelt now. The ground was damp and icy through the knees of his jeans, and he put one hand on the top of the white marble, as you would put your hand on a man's shoulder, and held it there.

"Daddy," he said.

It was the word he had not used since the bus station. It came out of him with a crack in it.

"I don't know if you can hear me." He swallowed. "I was scared of you my whole life, and then I hated you. I figured God was just you with a bigger voice. Not now." He looked at the carved letters. *SFC.* Sergeant First Class. He had been proud of that, once, when he was small, before he understood. "I built a room with my boy. Let him hang the door. You never once handed me a hammer." He laughed, and it hurt. "I'm still just trying to be what you weren't."

The wind moved over the hill. Somewhere down the row, a woman was crying without a sound, over another open grave.

"I hated you," Ezra said. "I need you to know that. I hated you every day. I hated you at my wedding when you weren't there, and when Jalen was born, and every time I raised my voice at him and heard yours come out." His hand tightened on the stone. "And I did it anyway. All of it. I did to my boy what you did to me. Not the gun. Not the belt. But I turned my back on him every night for all that time, and he played that cello to my back, and I let him." His voice went. He waited until he could talk again. "So I don't get to hate you anymore. I'm not better than you. I'm just a man who got a second chance you didn't."

He bowed his head until his forehead rested against the cold marble.

"I forgive you," he said. "I forgive you, Daddy. I don't know where you are. That's between you and Him. But I forgive you. I'm not carrying it anymore."

He stayed there, forehead on the stone and hand on top of it. When he finally sat back on his heels, the sun had gone down behind the trees on the far hill, and the long rows of white stones were turning gold and then gray, and the people were walking back to their cars.

He did not get up. He looked down the long rows, at the open graves and the whole ones, and words of Jesus came to him that he had heard a hundred times in Pastor Grant's pulpit and never once heard.

*Enter ye in at the strait gate: for wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat: Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it.* (Matthew 7:13–14)

Many there be which go in thereat. He had gone in at it himself. For thirty years he had walked the broad way with his mother's religion at his back and his hands busy and a good name in the town, and he had called it the narrow one because it was well kept and he was not alone on it. His father had walked it too. They had walked it side by side, and neither had once looked up to see where it went.

Few there be that find it. He had not found it. He understood that now, with his knees in the wet grass. It had found him, in a cold kitchen and a dark house, when he had nothing left in his hands to carry it with.

He bowed his head again.

"Lord Jesus," he said. "I don't have anything to bring You. I came out here thinking I might have something. A forgiveness. A good deed. I don't." He shut his eyes. "*All our righteousnesses are as filthy rags.* (Isaiah 64:6) I've read it all my life, and I thought it was about other men." His breath smoked in the cold. "It's me. I was dirty when I went to church and dirty when I stayed home. Nothing I did out here today made me clean. You did that, on the cross, before I was born, and You did it for me on my kitchen floor last night. I didn't earn one hour of it."

The wind moved the dry grass. He did not look up.

"So I'm not asking for what I've earned. I'm asking for mercy. That's the only thing I've got to ask for." His voice broke and he let it. "*God be merciful to me a sinner.* (Luke 18:13) That's the man in Your story, Lord, the one who stood off by himself and wouldn't lift his eyes. That's me. Be merciful to me. Be merciful to my boy, and my girl, and the ones I can't find. And if You want to use me, use me as I am. I'm weak. You know I'm weak. You told Paul, *My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.* (2 Corinthians 12:9) Then let it be made perfect in me. I'm Yours, Jesus. All of me. I've got nothing else."

He stayed bowed a long while. When he finally lifted his face, he was no longer afraid, and he could not have said when the fear had left him. Whatever the Lord asked of him, he thought, he had already laid it down on this hill.

He took his father's watch off his wrist.

He had worn it every day since he was sixteen. It had come back from Saudi Arabia at the bottom of a cardboard carton, stopped at 3:17, and he had slept with it under his pillow from the day it came, until his wrist was big enough to hold it, and it had hardly been off his wrist since. He held it in his palm now and looked at the stopped hands. 3:17. The box his father's things came in was still in his garage, on the shelf behind the paint cans, where he did not have to look at it.

He thought about leaving it on the stone.

Then he didn't. He put it back on his wrist and buckled the worn leather strap.

"I'll come back," he told the stone. "I don't know when. But I'll come back."

He stood up, and his knees cracked, and he walked back up the row in the dusk, past the open graves, toward the truck.

\* \* \*

He did not drive home right away. He sat in the truck at the edge of the cemetery with the engine running for the heat and his phone plugged into the dashboard to charge, because it was the only place left he could charge it, and he read Jesus' sermon in Matthew seven in Angela's Bible by the dome light.

*Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. *Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name? and in thy name have cast out devils? and in thy name done many wonderful works? And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.* (Matthew 7:21–23)*

He read it slowly, as Angela had asked him to read everything. Then he read on, past the place where the loud men stop reading. He read about the wise man who built his house upon a rock, and the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew and beat upon that house, and it fell not, because it was founded upon a rock. And the foolish man who built his house upon the sand.

He thought of the row house on Kennedy Street he had shored up two winters back, the back wall leaning off a footing poured on fill.

He sat in the truck with the Bible lying on the wheel, as Kambria's had, and he thought: *I have built my whole life on sand. On my father. On my grief. On my own two hands.* And then he thought, with a feeling so new it had no name: *It is not too late to pour a footing.*

The phone lit up in its cradle on the dashboard.

It had found a signal, the first in two days. One by one, the messages came in, a dozen of them, then twenty, buzzing against the plastic: Elijah, four times. *Ez. Ez, call me. Please. I'm losing it.* The school. The police station, an automated message, *no new information at this time.* Mrs. Okafor. A number he did not know.

And at the bottom, the newest, sent four minutes ago, a name he had not let himself think about since Thursday.

*Celeste: I'm so scared, Ezra. Everyone's gone. I can't be alone tonight. Please come. I need you. I'll leave the door open.*

Ezra sat in the truck at the edge of Arlington National Cemetery, with the open graves going dark on the hills behind him and his wife's Bible open on the wheel, and looked at the screen.

He knew exactly how to get to Mount Pleasant from here. He had driven it a hundred times. Twenty minutes, at night, with no traffic.

His thumb moved toward the screen.
