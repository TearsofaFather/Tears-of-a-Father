## Chapter 17: The Mountain

> *And they gathered themselves together against Moses and against Aaron, and said unto them, Ye take too much upon you, seeing all the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the LORD is among them: wherefore then lift ye up yourselves above the congregation of the LORD?* — Numbers 16:3

**Word study: *holy* — קָדוֹשׁ** Hebrew *qadosh* (Strong's H6918). Set apart, separated, sacred. Its root means to be cut off from the common and given to God alone. A thing is holy because God has chosen it and set it apart for Himself. When Korah said that everyone was holy, he was not lifting everyone up. He was saying that no one had been set apart at all.

*And they rose up before Moses, with certain of the children of Israel, two hundred and fifty princes of the assembly, famous in the congregation, men of renown… And they took every man his censer, and put fire in them, and laid incense thereon, and stood in the door of the tabernacle of the congregation with Moses and Aaron… and the glory of the LORD appeared unto all the congregation.* (Numbers 16:2, 18–19)


\* \* \*

On Tuesday morning, Walt called at nine and told her to come over.

He gave no reason. He had never once, in all the years she had known him, asked anyone to come over. She had told him everything on Monday night, on the phone, and when she finished he had said only, "Tomorrow. I'll make the coffee."

It was a sullen, windless morning, two days before Thanksgiving. At the Pattersons' the porch light was off and the driveway was empty. In the big front window hung a paper turkey made from a traced hand, the fingers colored red and orange and brown, and across the bottom Mia had printed, in careful capitals, *I AM THANKFUL FOR MY FAMILY*.

Walt had two cups poured and the small television turned to face the table. He was three weeks out of open-heart surgery at Brandick Memorial, his breastbone wired shut, and he had poured the coffee anyway, one-handed, against orders.

"Have a seat," he said.

She sat in the chair across from his.

"I should have told you." He said it to his cup. "The woman. I saw her car outside his house in October, and then again. More than once. I figured it wasn't my business. I figured you'd find out, and be hurt, and I didn't want to be the one who did it." He rubbed his mouth. "That was cowardly. I'm sorry, Kambria."

She laid her hand over his old, spotted one. "It's all right, Walt."

"It's not. But thank you." He took the hand back and nodded at the screen. "Jerusalem. They've been on it all morning. I wanted you to see it with me."

\* \* \*

The picture was the Mount of Olives in the late afternoon: the long ridge east of the Old City, the olive trees, the white tombs stepping down toward the Kidron Valley. Across the valley the Temple Mount burned gold. At the top of the ridge stood a long, low building of pale stone and dark glass, its name in brass over the doors: *THE OLIVET CROWN*.

*JERUSALEM: COUNCIL FOR UNIVERSAL MORAL LAW CONVENES.*

Two hundred and fifty delegates. The Council, back in session, seated now as a permanent body. Buses crawling up a narrow road, men in suits and robes and clerical collars stepping down into the cameras. Then the Council's president, Rabbi Matteo Arcangeli, tall and silver-haired at the top of the hotel steps.

"For too long," he said, "the children of one God have stood apart, each claiming to hold the only door. Today we take down the walls. Every tradition here is holy. Every one of them. No tradition may stand above the others and call itself the only way."

Applause. The camera drifted over the third row and stopped on a finely cut charcoal suit. Bishop Roland Sterling, clapping with the rest, wiping his eyes. Then Senator-elect Thorne on the steps, hand in hand with the rabbi, smiling for the lenses. The first resolution, adopted unanimously: religious claims to exclusive access to God were "a root cause of division and violence in the modern world."

Walt thumbed the sound off.

"Every tradition here is holy," he said. "Every one of them. I know when a man is quoting something, Kambria."

"I don't think he knows he's quoting it."

"He knows."

She had already turned the pages. She knew the place; she had headed the page in her notebook in the spring, *THE MOUNTAIN*. "Walt. Listen to where they're standing. This is the hill where the disciples asked Him, *What shall be the sign of thy coming?* This is where He went up, and where the angels said He'd come back the same way. *And his feet shall stand in that day upon the mount of Olives.* (Zechariah 14:4) Right there, on those steps." She scrolled with her thumb. "I looked up the hotel. They cut the access road straight through the oldest Jewish cemetery in the world. Three thousand years of graves, facing the Temple, waiting to be first up when the Messiah comes. The buses drove over the broken stones this afternoon."

Walt took off his glasses.

*Behold, O my people, I will open your graves.* "They built a road over the resurrection," he said.

"Two hundred and fifty men signing a law for the whole world about who's holy, on the exact mountain where—"

"Kambria."

Something in his voice stopped her.

He had not moved his head. His eyes had gone to the front window, over her shoulder, and his hand had closed around the handle of his cane.

"Don't turn around fast," Walt said.

\* \* \*

She turned around slowly.

Across the street, at the curb in front of the Pattersons' empty driveway, a gray Chevrolet Malibu idled with its headlights off. The windows were dark. She knew the dent in its rear bumper. She knew the plate; it was written on the back of her hand again, in pen, because she had stopped trusting paper.

Behind it, a small silver car pulled to the curb. A thin red string swung from the rearview mirror.

The driver's door opened. Celeste stepped out in a camel coat, red hair loose, carrying a covered pot in both hands with a dish towel folded over the lid. She bent to the window of the Malibu and said something. The man inside never turned his head. Then she straightened and looked across the street and up at Walt's front window, and smiled, as if she could see straight through the glass.

"She's coming," Kambria whispered.

"Kitchen." Walt was already on his feet. He did not lean on the cane; he lifted it, like a man who meant to use it. "Back in the kitchen. Shut the door. Don't come out till I say."

She went. The kitchen door was an old hollow-core thing with a glass knob. She pulled it nearly closed and stood against the refrigerator with her hand over her own mouth, and listened through the gap.

The bell rang once. Walt let it ring again before he crossed the room.

The chain rattled. A crack of cold air.

"Mr. Simmons!" Celeste's voice was a light, warm bell. "Good morning. I'm so sorry to bother you. I saw Kambria's car out front and I thought, well, she mustn't have eaten. I brought soup."

"Her car's out front because she parked it there," Walt said. "That's all a car tells you."

"I only want to see her. Five minutes."

"She isn't receiving."

"Mr. Simmons." The warmth did not leave her voice. It thinned. "I know she's in there. I'm not here to fight. I'm here because I'm worried. She isn't well, and she's a long way down a road with no one to pull her back. You're a sensible man. You've had your own troubles." A pause, and a softer note. "The heart. At your age, with a winter like this coming. Such a hard thing, an old man alone."

In the kitchen Kambria's nails went into her palm.

When Walt answered, it was in the voice he used on proofreaders.

"Ma'am. I've been threatened by better. Take your soup."

"It's not a threat. It's a kindness." Something clinked: a pot set down on the porch boards. "Tell her it's still warm. Tell her I'll be thinking of her Thursday." The voice came closer to the crack. "And tell her Francesca sends her love."

The door shut. The chain slid home.

Heels clicked down the steps. A car door. An engine. Another engine. Walt did not move until both had gone, and then he turned the deadbolt, and leaned his forehead against the wood.

Kambria came out of the kitchen shaking.

"She said Francesca," she whispered. "She said it again."

"I heard."

Then Walt opened the door a foot and pointed at the pot with his cane, and Kambria carried it in to the table. He took off the lid.

Chicken and rice. Carrots cut small. A bay leaf riding the top.

Kambria sat down on the floor where she stood. She knew the smell. She had stood at Angela's elbow in the Pattersons' kitchen on a hundred Thursdays while Angela taught her to make it. Angela had written the recipe out for Celeste once, on the sixth floor, and the story that went with it.

"That's Angela's soup," she said.

Walt put the lid back on, gently, as if the pot might wake.

"Don't eat it," he said. And then, after a while, "Pour it out in the yard. It'll take the grass."

\* \* \*

She went out the back, past the grill and the fence, with the empty pot in her hands.

David Levin was at his grill in a down vest and slippers, turning chicken thighs with long tongs, because it was noon and cold and he had a point to prove about turkeys. He lifted the tongs.

"*Baruch haba*!" he called. He always said it. His grandmother used to say it at her door.

*Blessed is he that cometh.* The crowds on the road to Jerusalem had sung it out of Psalm 118; the last thing Jesus said to that city. *Ye shall not see me henceforth, till ye shall say, Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord.* Kambria had never heard anyone offer it so easily, over a grill.

"*Baruch haba*," she said, and he beamed.

Then his eyes moved past her. He nodded toward the street.

"That gray car," he said. "Been parked on our block on and off for two weeks. I called it in. Two officers came out, looked at the plate, and went away white." He turned a thigh. "They said it was federal. Said not to worry about it." He looked at her over his glasses. "I'm worried about it."

"David—"

"My grandmother lit candles every Friday night until the day she died. In the Bronx. She didn't believe half of it. But she lit them. Nobody made her, and nobody could have." He waved his tongs. "They want a law about faith now. Up on that hill. Something about it gives me the creeps, and I'm not even a religious guy."

"That feeling's worth listening to," Kambria said. "Your grandmother's candles were lit for Someone. And He's still coming."

He didn't answer. He turned the chicken. But he said, "Tell Walt I'm bringing him a plate Thursday whether he likes it or not," and when she looked back from the gate he was still standing there with the tongs in his hand, watching the street.

She poured the soup out under the fence, in the dead grass, and rinsed the pot, and set it on Walt's step.

\* \* \*

She did not go home. She drove to Petworth.

The nursing home sat on Georgia Avenue between a check-cashing store and a church with a padlocked gate: three stories of tan brick, a green awning, a handwritten sign inside the glass. *NO VISITORS UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.* She had no badge and no right to be there. Then she thought of Mrs. Bell's hand in hers, and the son turning his cap, and she got out of the car.

The woman at the desk would not let her past the lobby. But a petite aide in purple scrubs, name tag *MARISOL*, stopped in the side hall when she heard the name Clarice Bell, and walked Kambria out to the sidewalk and pointed her up the street toward the bus stop, for anyone watching.

"You're the one who prayed with her," Marisol said, low and fast. "Her son told us." She glanced back at the door. "The Verity people came Friday. Two men in a van. They took every file from the wellness program, every sign-in sheet, every box of the spray. They said it was a recall." Her arms folded tight. "Nobody recalls anything without paper."

"How many residents?"

"Forty-one. They signed everyone up in October. It was free." Her voice dropped. "They're coming back tomorrow morning with the winter doses. They already printed the list. Forty-one names, and a dose beside every one." Her eyes were wet. "Six are in the hospital. Mrs. Bell was the first to die. She won't be the last." She looked past Kambria's shoulder at the street, and her face went blank. "I have to go back in."

"Marisol—"

"Don't come here again."

The door closed.

Kambria turned around.

Across Georgia Avenue, two cars behind her own, the gray Malibu sat at the curb with its engine running.

Then its door opened.

The man who got out was fifty, short gray hair, a heavy ordinary face, gray overcoat. He did not hurry. He crossed the median between two lanes of traffic, stopped on the near sidewalk thirty feet from her, and raised his phone. He took her photograph. He took it slowly, in plain daylight, the way a man photographs a house he means to buy.

Then he lowered the phone, looked at her, and got back into his car.

She walked to her own without running, because running was what they wanted. She locked the doors with the heels of her hands. She was halfway to Florida Avenue before she let the air out of her, and when she did, it came out as prayer.

*Lord. Marisol. The forty-one. The six. Francesca, wherever she is. Me.*

She did not stop at her house. She sat in the parking lot of a church for an hour, and then she drove home the long way, and checked every window twice before she dared to turn on the light.

\* \* \*

That night, by the lamp, she opened the ninth notebook and dated a fresh page. *November 24, 2026.*

*Council for Universal Moral Law. 250. Olivet Crown, Mount of Olives. Arcangeli. Sterling a delegate. Thorne, senator-elect, legal adviser. "Exclusive claims… a root cause of division."* Beside it: *Numbers 16:3. Numbers 16:29–30. Zechariah 14:4.* And in small letters: *The road is paved over the graves.*

On the facing page, the list that frightened her more. *Verity. 41 residents. 6 in hospital. Files gone Friday, no paper. Winter doses tomorrow, A.M. Gray Malibu photographed me. Celeste at Walt's door with Angela's soup. "Francesca sends her love."*

She stopped. She drew a box around the last line, and underneath it she wrote: *She is not guessing. They have her.*

Then one more verse, and a box around it.

*Depart, I pray you, from the tents of these wicked men.*

Moses had warned the people to move away before the ground opened. Enoch had been taken before the flood. Lot had been pulled out of Sodom by the hand before the fire fell. *God hath not appointed us to wrath.* She did not know the day or the hour. Nobody did.

Her phone buzzed on the table.

*Getting a ride home tonight around 9,* Jalen had written. *Grandma says don't forget the mac and cheese Thursday.* And a second later: *Dad said it's ok if you come. He said it himself.*

Kambria read the last line three times. The door she had stood in front of on Thursday night had not been shut all the way.

*I'll be there,* she typed. *Love you.* She sat a while with the phone in her hand.

Under the box in the notebook she wrote one word. *Thursday.*

She checked the oil in the lamp, filled it to the brim, trimmed the wick, and left it burning in the window.

She had one more day before Thursday. She meant to use it well: the macaroni and cheese, the notebook for Ezra's table, the right words if she could find them.
