## Chapter 15: The Leopard's Plaza

> *After this I beheld, and lo another, like a leopard, which had upon the back of it four wings of a fowl; the beast had also four heads; and dominion was given to it.* — Daniel 7:6

**Word study: *leopard* — נְמַר** Aramaic *nemar* (Strong's H5245). A leopard, from a root that means to be spotted. The leopard is the stealthiest of the great cats, and it hunts by waiting, still and patient, until the moment it moves. Daniel saw it with wings on its back, faster still.

In the first year of Belshazzar king of Babylon, Daniel had a dream and visions of his head upon his bed, and he wrote the dream down (Daniel 7:1). He saw the four winds of heaven strive upon the great sea, and four great beasts came up from the sea, diverse one from another (Daniel 7:2-3). The first was like a lion and had eagle's wings (Daniel 7:4). The second was like a bear, with three ribs in its mouth between its teeth (Daniel 7:5). The third was like a leopard, with upon its back four wings of a fowl and four heads, and dominion was given to it (Daniel 7:6). The fourth beast was dreadful and terrible and strong exceedingly; it had great iron teeth, it devoured and brake in pieces, and it had ten horns (Daniel 7:7). Daniel's spirit was grieved in the midst of his body, and the visions of his head troubled him (Daniel 7:15). He kept the matter in his heart (Daniel 7:28).

\* \* \*

Mrs. Clarice Bell was eighty-one years old, and she was dying, and she knew it.

She had come in by ambulance on Friday night from the nursing home in Petworth, the same one, with a fever of a hundred and four and a blood pressure that would not stay up. Kambria had been called in from home at midnight to take the samples herself, her badge and login already dead since six, so a night tech had to let her in. By four in the morning the antibiotics had done nothing at all, and the doctor had come and gone, and the family had been called. Kambria knew why, and could not tell anyone in the room. There were six of them now in the treatment room off the trauma bay: two daughters, a son in a bus driver's uniform, and three grown grandchildren, standing against the walls because there were only two chairs.

Mrs. Bell was a birdlike woman with white hair braided close to her head and a gold cross on a thin chain at her throat. She had not spoken in an hour. At a quarter to five she opened her eyes, and looked around the room at her children, and then she found Kambria by the bed rail, in her mask and gown with the swab kit in her hands, and held out her hand.

Kambria went to her and took it.

"You a praying woman?" Mrs. Bell whispered.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then pray for me, baby. Pray me home."

Kambria looked at the family. The older daughter nodded, already crying. The son took off his cap.

So Kambria prayed. She held Mrs. Bell's hand in both of hers, and she prayed in a murmur, as Angela had prayed over everyone who sat beside her bed. "Father, thank You for eighty-one years of Clarice Bell. Thank You for these children. Take away her fear, Lord, and her pain." She prayed the twenty-third psalm, and the old woman's lips moved with her on *I will fear no evil*. Then she said, "Lord Jesus, You said in Your Father's house are many mansions, and You went to prepare a place for her. Receive her now. In Jesus' name."

"Amen," said Mrs. Bell, and the whole room said it with her.

When Kambria looked up, Celeste was standing in the doorway with a stack of folded blankets in her arms. She stood looking at the family, and at Mrs. Bell, and at Kambria with the old woman's hand in hers. Her face was soft and sad. Then she set the blankets down on the counter and went out without a word.

Mrs. Bell died at six-ten in the morning with her son holding one hand and her older daughter holding the other. Afterward, in the hallway, the son gripped Kambria's hand and could not speak, and the younger daughter hugged her and said, "Thank you for praying with Mama. That's all she wanted."

Kambria walked out to the parking garage at a quarter to seven with the swab kit in a sealed bag for the courier, and sat in her car with the engine off, and did not think about the doorway at all. She thought about the phone call.

Francesca Moretti had called her on Wednesday night, a little after ten, from a New York number Kambria did not know.

It had taken her a moment to place the voice. Francesca had worked two benches down from her in Hyattsville for years: a tall, quick, dark-haired microbiologist from South Philadelphia who hummed old hymns under her breath while she worked and brought a tray of her grandmother's cannoli to every office party. She had moved up to New York years ago, and been loaned from there to the new health security office of the Organization for World Peace. Kambria had sent her one file in all that time: the Ohio isolate, with the patch nobody could explain. Francesca had written back that she had never seen anything like it and would keep looking. Then nothing.

Kambria had written to her on Monday night, three careful lines through the one personal account she trusted, asking only whether anything like Petworth had crossed her desk. She had not expected an answer.

"You asked me Monday if anyone up here had seen a cluster like this," Francesca had said. Her voice was low. "So I went looking. The Petworth report was on a server I'm not cleared for. By this afternoon it was pulled back and sealed. And Kam, somebody saw me open it." A pause. "Kam, I have to see you. Not on the phone. Can you come up?"

"Francesca, I've got work. I can't just..."

"Then come for the weekend." Francesca's voice had shaken, for the first time. "Saturday. Four o'clock. The diner on Second Avenue at Forty-Fifth, the one with the red booths. Get a hotel. Make it look like a weekend in the city. Please."

Kambria had told her she would think about it. She had thought about it for two days, and decided it was too much, and never called back.

Now she sat in the cold car in the hospital garage with Mrs. Bell's swabs in a sealed bag on the passenger seat, and she knew why the antibiotics had done nothing, and she thought of the son who had taken off his cap.

She took out her phone and bought a train ticket.

\* \* \*

She slept for two hours, showered, and packed a bag for one night: a change of clothes, her toothbrush, her Bible, and the ninth notebook. She booked the cheapest hotel she could find near Grand Central, two hundred and eighty-nine dollars she did not have to spare, and told no one where she was going.

The Northeast Regional left Union Station at five minutes past eleven. She had a window seat and her Bible open on her lap to the seventh chapter of Daniel, and she did not read it. She kept seeing Mrs. Bell's hand reaching for her across the bed.

Her phone buzzed as the train pulled out of the tunnel. *Celeste: Heard you're taking a weekend away. Rest, sis. Safe travels.*

Kambria read it three times. She had told the front desk she needed Saturday for a personal day, and nothing more. The front desk must have said something. That was all it was. She typed a thumbs-up, and deleted it, and put the phone face down on her knee.

At half past two the train slid into Penn Station, and she was in New York.

The city was already dressed for Christmas. On the side of a building a screen four stories tall played an ad in which glossy young people danced on a rooftop with drinks in their hands, and nobody's eyes were quite awake. *Lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God,* Kambria thought, and was ashamed, because she did not know their hearts. She prayed for them instead. She left her bag at the hotel, and at ten to four she walked into the diner on Second Avenue.

\* \* \*

Francesca was not there.

Kambria took the last red booth at the back, facing the door, the one Francesca would have taken, and ordered a cup of coffee, and waited. Four o'clock came. Then four-fifteen. Then half past. She called the New York number, and it rang and rang, and went to a recorded voice that said the customer was not available. At five o'clock the waitress came to refill her cup for the third time. She was a heavy, gray-haired woman with reading glasses on a chain, and she looked Kambria up and down over the rims.

"You waiting on the tall girl?" she said. "Dark hair? Talks fast?"

Kambria's heart jumped. "Yes."

"She's been in every day this week. Same booth. Sits facing the door, orders tea, doesn't drink it." The waitress set the coffee pot down on the table. "She didn't come in yesterday, or today. But Thursday she gave me something. Said a lady might come in on Saturday with a notebook and ask for her, and to give it to her if she did." She looked at the notebook on the table beside Kambria's cup. "I'm guessing that's you." She lowered her voice. "Two men in suits came by Friday, asking after her. Flashed a badge so fast I couldn't read it. I told them I'd never seen her. I don't know why. I didn't like how they stood."

She went to the register and came back with a plain white envelope. On the front, in blue ink, was a single letter. *K.*

Kambria waited until the waitress had gone. Then she opened it.

Inside was a key on a ring with a small brass tag stamped *4C*. A business card from the health security office of the Organization for World Peace, with Francesca's name on the front, and on the back, in Francesca's quick, slanting hand, an address: *312 East 43rd. Tudor City.* And a folded sheet of notebook paper with nothing on it but five lines, written in pen.

*Genesis 6:4.*

*Daniel 7:7.*

*Daniel 7:24.*

*Revelation 17:12.*

*Revelation 6:8.*

That was all. No note. No explanation. Five verses, in the order Francesca had written them.

Kambria sat motionless in the red booth with the paper in her hands. Francesca had trusted neither a letter nor the phone. She had trusted that the woman who wrote everything down would know how to read.

She opened her Bible on the table and began.

*There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown.* (Genesis 6:4) The days before the flood. The mighty men. The men who made a name.

*After this I saw in the night visions, and behold a fourth beast, dreadful and terrible, and strong exceedingly; and it had great iron teeth… and it had ten horns.* (Daniel 7:7)

*And the ten horns out of this kingdom are ten kings that shall arise: and another shall rise after them…* (Daniel 7:24)

*And the ten horns which thou sawest are ten kings, which have received no kingdom as yet; but receive power as kings one hour with the beast.* (Revelation 17:12)

*And I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him. And power was given unto them over the fourth part of the earth, to kill with sword, and with hunger, and with death, and with the beasts of the earth.* (Revelation 6:8) The word *death* there, she knew, could be read *pestilence*. She did not think the seal itself had opened, not yet. This was the beginning of sorrows, the birth pangs of Matthew 24:7-8, the shadow of the pale horse before the horse.

She wrote the five verses into the ninth notebook, and under them, one word from each.

*Renown. Ten. Another. One hour. Pestilence.*

Mighty men. Ten of them. And another who would rise after them. And a sickness.

\* \* \*

Tudor City was a cluster of old brick towers on a rise above First Avenue, with gothic windows and stone gargoyles. It was dark by the time she got there. From the end of Forty-Third Street she could see down onto the long row of flagpoles along First Avenue, and beyond them the tall green-glass slab of the International Collective, lit floor after floor.

The doorman at number 312 was watching a game on his phone. He looked up when she came in.

"Four C," Kambria said, and held up the key, and walked to the elevator as if she lived there. He went back to his game.

The hallway on the fourth floor was quiet and smelled of radiator heat. Kambria stood outside 4C longer than she meant to with the key in her hand. She thought about knocking. Then she thought that if Francesca were inside, she would have come to the diner, and she put the key in the lock.

The apartment was compact and scrupulously clean. Too clean. There was no laptop on the desk by the window, only the square of dust where one had been. The desk drawers held nothing. The file box under the desk had been cleared out, its lid set neatly beside it. In the kitchen there was a single mug upside down in the dish rack, and a calendar on the refrigerator from a parish in South Philadelphia, turned to November. Saturday the twenty-first was circled in blue ink. *K, 4 p.m.*

Somebody had been here before her. Somebody had taken everything Francesca had worked on and left the rest exactly where it was, so that anyone who came looking would see a tidy apartment and a woman who had simply gone away.

But they had not taken the Bible.

It was on the nightstand in the bedroom, a worn black Bible with a cracked spine, handed down, by the look of it, from some South Philadelphia grandmother. Kambria picked it up. It fell open in her hands to the place it had been opened most. Daniel, chapter seven. Verse twenty-four was underlined in blue pen, and in the margin, in Francesca's slanting hand, two words: *the ten.*

Two sheets were tucked between the pages, folded small.

The first was a glossy printed program on heavy cream paper. *The Organization for World Peace. Flag Ceremony and Presentation of the High Council of the Technate. Sunday, November 22. Twelve noon. Visitors' Plaza, International Collective Headquarters.* Inside, on the left page, were the speakers: Adrian Crowe, Executive Chairman. Alexander Vale, Secretary to the High Council of the Technate. On the right page, under the heading *The High Council of the Technate*, was a list of names. Kambria counted them. There were ten. Beside each name was a line of small type. The chief executive of the company that ran the power grids for half of Europe. The founder of the largest network of satellites in orbit. The head of the firm that made most of the world's computer chips. The man whose company held the cloud servers of forty governments. The woman who ran the biggest payment network on earth. A food company that fed a billion people. A shipping company. An artificial intelligence lab. A data company whose name Kambria had seen on the side of the cameras going up at every intersection in Washington. And the last one, a health technology firm. *Verity Health. A portfolio company of the Meridian Trust.*

Ten men and women. Not one of them elected. Not one of them a king. And every one of them holding some piece of the machinery the whole world ran on.

*Ten kings, which have received no kingdom as yet.*

The second sheet was a printout of a single screen from some internal system, the header cut off. Kambria read it twice, standing in the bedroom of a woman who was not there.

*Organism designation: PALE. File created: September 30. Distribution: Office of the Secretary to the High Council of the Technate. Field clusters expected: four. Status: monitoring. Public communication: none until framework in place.*

Below it, in Francesca's pen, a list. *Petworth (DC). Care home, Manila. Refugee camp, Kherson. Hospital, Belém (Brazil).* And under that, underlined twice: *Created SEPT 30. First case OCT 19. They knew before it happened.*

Kambria's legs gave, and she sat on Francesca's bed.

September thirtieth. Three weeks before the first resident of the nursing home in Petworth got sick. Somebody had opened a file on a germ that did not exist yet, and named it after the fourth horse of Revelation, and sent it to the office of the man who signed Crowe's papers. *Field clusters expected: four.*

Expected.

She thought of the three old women on Georgia Avenue, and the free nasal spray in October, and the small gray type at the bottom of a clean white website. She thought of Mrs. Bell.

She could not move. Then she put both sheets back inside the Bible exactly where she had found them, and then, after a moment, took them out again and put them inside her own Bible instead, and put Francesca's Bible back on the nightstand, open to the same page. She turned off the light. She locked the door behind her.

In the lobby, a man on the bench by the mailboxes turned a page of his newspaper, and the doorman looked up from his game.

"You a friend of hers?" he said.

Kambria stopped. "Yes."

"Two guys from her office came Thursday night for her stuff. Said she got transferred overseas, real sudden." He shrugged. "Nice lady. Always said good morning. Didn't say goodbye." He went back to his phone. "Funny. They didn't have a moving truck."

Kambria walked back to her hotel through the cold, along Forty-Second Street past Grand Central, with her Bible clutched in both arms, and did not look back until the hotel doors had closed behind her.

\* \* \*

She let herself into the hotel room a little after nine and stopped in the doorway.

The tag she had hung on the knob that afternoon, *Do Not Disturb*, hung reversed. Her bag sat on the luggage rack exactly where she had left it, zipper closed. But the zipper had been open when she left, one corner of the sweater showing, the way she always left it. She had not moved it. Her clothes inside were folded now. Perfectly. In thirds, like a store.

Nothing was missing. That was how she knew they had been looking for something, and that what they wanted was not in the room. It was in her head.

She wedged the desk chair under the doorknob, took the notebook and the Bible onto the bed, and did not sleep.

She sat cross-legged on the narrow hotel bed with the lamp on and everything laid out on the blanket in front of her: Francesca's five verses, the program with its ten names, the sheet with the word *PALE* across it, and the ninth notebook open beside them. Below her window, Forty-Fourth Street hissed and honked all night.

She worked it as she would have worked an outbreak at the bench, one link at a time, and wrote each link down.

*Meridian Trust, Dubai. Pays for the Council in Jerusalem (the sponsor list). Director: A. Vale.*

*Verity Health. Owned by Meridian. The agency's contract lab: every sample I have sent since July went to their bench in Rockville. Free "wellness" program, Petworth, October. Nasal spray.*

*The germ. Nothing kills it. Genes put together. Four clusters.*

*File PALE. Created Sept 30, before the first case. Sent to the Secretary to the High Council of the Technate: Vale.*

*High Council of the Technate: ten. Verity is one of the ten.*

*Francesca: gone. Two men with a badge. Man on the lobby bench: gray overcoat, fifties, newspaper.*

She went down the list once, and then again. Then she wrote beneath the list, and drew a box around it, as Angela used to:

*Not proof. A picture. But the picture has a name in the middle of it every time.*

At two in the morning she got down on her knees on the thin carpet, elbows on the bed, and prayed for Francesca until she could not find any more words. Then she prayed for herself, because she was afraid, and she told God so. *I'm a woman with a notebook. Show me what You want me to see, and give me the courage to see it.*

She fell asleep like that, against the bed, and woke at six with the lamp still on.

\* \* \*

By half past eleven on Sunday she was standing in the crowd on First Avenue, across from the International Collective.

There were police barriers along the sidewalks and news trucks with their masts up. A stage had been built on the Visitors' Plaza, inside the gates, under a white canopy. Along the back of the stage stood a row of ten white chairs, all exactly alike. Beside the tallest flagpole, where the blue flag of the International Collective hung, a second pole had been raised. It was bare.

The crowd was bigger than she had expected. Some had come to cheer, with signs: *ONE WORLD. BUILD THE FUTURE. NATIONS ARE THE PAST.* Some had come to protest, and the police kept them down the block. Most had come the way she had, alone, with their phones held up.

The front rows inside the gates were roped off for invited guests, and she could see them clearly. They were not diplomats. They were the richest men in the world, in overcoats that cost more than her car, a prince from the Gulf, two presidents, a film star. They sat with their legs crossed, cool and silent. They had already paid for what was on the stage.

*The mighty men which were of old,* Kambria thought. *Men of renown.*

At noon, the ten came out first.

They walked onto the stage in a line, in dark suits, and took the ten white chairs, and sat. Kambria checked the program in her coat pocket against their faces, one by one, as far as she could tell from the street. The grid. The satellites. The chips. The servers. The payments. The food, the shipping, the machines that think, the cameras. The last chair, at the end of the row: a thin woman with silver glasses. *Verity Health.*

Then a choir began to sing, an anthem written for the day, enormous, brass and drums and a hundred voices, about light and children and a world without borders. It never named anyone. There was no Lord in it, and no God, and no King. When it ended, Kambria could not remember one line of it.

Adrian Crowe walked out onto the stage, and a thin, dark-haired man in a gray suit walked beside him, half a step behind. They had the same walk, she thought, the same slight lean into every stride, and she could not have said why she noticed it.

Kambria knew the second man before she looked at the program. She had never seen his face. But she had read his initial on a filing from Dubai, and his title in a missing woman's Bible, and she had stared for an hour one morning at a gray sleeve at the edge of a photograph. *Alexander Vale.*

Vale went to the podium first. He did not smile. He read the charter of the High Council of the Technate in an even voice, and did not once lift his eyes to the crowd. When he turned the page, his cuff slid back from his wrist, and Kambria saw it on the big screen when the camera moved in, because she had been waiting weeks to see it: a thin red string, knotted three times. The arm at the edge of the photograph. When he finished he stepped back and to the side, and folded his hands, and was still.

Then Crowe took the podium.

He was taller than she had expected, lean and tanned, in a charcoal suit with no tie, his silver hair cut close. He did not use notes. He put both hands on the sides of the podium and looked out over all of them, the crowd and the princes and the building behind him, and he held his peace until the crowd began to shift its feet.

"For eighty years," he said, "this building has been the place where the weak came to complain about the strong."

There was a murmur in the crowd, and then, from the roped-off rows, a few men laughed.

"It passed resolutions. It held meetings about meetings. It sent letters to dictators and waited for them to write back." He did not turn around to look at the green glass tower behind him. "I am not here to ask its permission. I am here to relieve it of a burden it was never strong enough to carry."

The applause from the front rows was slow, and certain.

"Nations are an old idea," Crowe said. "Elections are a slow one. Every four years you choose between two men who do not understand the machines that keep you alive. The people on this stage behind me understand them. They built them." He swept one hand back toward the ten white chairs. "The power in your walls. The money in your hand. The food on your table. The satellites over your heads. The cure in your medicine cabinet. They did not ask a parliament before they built any of it. And they will not need one to keep it running." He smiled with his mouth only. "Order. Peace. Safety. That is what we are offering the world. And the world, I promise you, is ready to say yes."

He looked down at the front rows, at the men in the expensive coats.

"Some of you already have," he said.

The men in the front rows stood up and applauded him. The crowd behind them followed.

But Kambria was not watching Crowe.

She was watching the ten.

While Crowe spoke, every one of them sat looking straight ahead, politely, at the back of his head. But twice, when the applause rose, she saw it: the thin woman from Verity Health turned her head a few degrees, not toward Crowe, but toward the gray-suited man standing still at the side of the stage. And the man from the satellite company did the same. And then a third. They were not waiting for Crowe to tell them what to do. They were waiting for Vale.

*And another shall rise after them.*

Cold ran down Kambria's arms.

Crowe and Vale raised the flag together, side by side, each with a hand on the rope. It was white, with a gold circle in the middle and a gold olive branch inside it. As it climbed the pole beside the blue flag of the International Collective, the wind came in off the river and caught it, and it opened all at once and stood out stiff and bright, and it was higher. Only by a few feet. But it was higher.

Crowe looked up at it with his chin lifted, a king on a coin.

At the side of the stage, Alexander Vale was not looking at the flag. He was looking at Crowe.

\* \* \*

The crowd broke up slowly. Kambria let it carry her down First Avenue, and then she turned back, and when the gates opened for visitors at three, she walked in.

The stage was coming down. Men in work gloves were stacking the ten white chairs. The two flags hung side by side over the half-empty plaza, stirring in the wind off the water. Kambria walked to the middle of the plaza and stopped, and took out the ninth notebook, and turned to the back.

There was a photograph taped there, printed off a news site years ago and moved from notebook to notebook ever since. It was this plaza. In the photograph, on the very spot where she was standing, stood a sculpture: a great cat, a jaguar, painted in bright patterns of red and gold and blue, crouched on a pedestal, with four enormous wings rising from its back. Under it, in her own handwriting, she had copied the caption. *A gift from the state of Oaxaca, Mexico, presented to the International Collective as a guardian for international peace and security. December 2021.* The sculpture had been taken away months later. But she had kept the picture, because the minute she saw it, she had thought of Daniel.

She opened her Bible and read it standing there, in the wind.

*After this I beheld, and lo another, like a leopard, which had upon the back of it four wings of a fowl… (Daniel 7:6)*

She turned the pages forward to Revelation, to the thirteenth chapter, and read the beast John saw rising from the sea. *And the beast which I saw was like unto a leopard, and his feet were as the feet of a bear, and his mouth as the mouth of a lion: and the dragon gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority. (Revelation 13:2)*

And then the seventeenth, the verse Francesca had written down. *And the ten horns which thou sawest are ten kings… These have one mind, and shall give their power and strength unto the beast. (Revelation 17:12-13)*

She looked up from the page. A winged leopard, set up on this plaza as the guardian of the peace of the nations. A new flag over it today, higher than the old one. Ten chairs on a stage. And a man who stood to the side and watched, while the ten turned their heads toward him.

*One mind.* Kambria wrote it in the notebook. Then, under it: *Give their power unto the beast. Whose beast? Crowe's? Or the one who rises after?*

Four verses above the ones she had copied stood a line she had read a hundred times and never once made sit still. *The beast that thou sawest was, and is not; and shall ascend out of the bottomless pit… and they that dwell on the earth shall wonder, whose names were not written in the book of life from the foundation of the world, when they behold the beast that was, and is not, and yet is.* (Revelation 17:8)

She copied it under the others and set its three small clauses in a column, the way Walt set a column of figures. *Was. Is not. Yet is.* A kingdom that had been, and was gone, and came again? A man? She could not tell, and she did not try to make it fit anything she could see on the plaza.

She did not know. Daniel had not known either, when he first saw it. *I considered the horns (Daniel 7:8),* he had written. He had looked, and kept looking, until he understood.

She turned to a clean page and dated it. *November 22, 2026.* And under it:

*The leopard's plaza. A new flag, higher. Ten chairs. "Order. Peace. Safety."*

*For when they shall say, Peace and safety; then sudden destruction cometh upon them…* (1 Thessalonians 5:3)

*…and by peace shall destroy many… (Daniel 8:25)*

*Francesca. Gone. PALE. Sept 30. They knew.*

When she closed the notebook and looked up, the plaza was nearly deserted. One of the workmen was folding a white chair. A security guard was talking into his radio by the gate.

And by the railing across the plaza, in a dark gray overcoat, a man stood watching her, his hands in his pockets.

She had seen him once before. In the lobby of 312 East Forty-Third Street, the night before, sitting on the bench by the mailboxes, reading a newspaper, when she came down from the fourth floor.

He did not look away when she looked at him. He only watched her, as Vale had watched Crowe.

Kambria put the notebook in her coat, and walked to the gate without running, and kept walking.

\* \* \*

She made the five-oh-five train at Penn Station with four minutes to spare.

She sat in the window seat with her bag on the seat beside her so no one would sit there, and her Bible on her lap with the two folded sheets inside it, and watched the lights of New Jersey go by in the dark. She thought of Francesca alone somewhere, if she was anywhere. The ten chairs. A germ that had a name before it had a victim. Mrs. Bell.

She bowed her head against the cold window and prayed for Francesca again.

Somewhere south of Philadelphia, her phone lit up in her lap. It was an email from the hospital: a meeting, eight o'clock tomorrow morning, fourth floor, mandatory. She read it four times. Then the phone buzzed again.

It was a text from a number with no name. There were no words in it. Only a photograph.

It was a picture of her, taken that afternoon: a woman in a navy coat standing alone in the middle of the Visitors' Plaza, with a notebook open in her hands and the two flags above her head.

A second text came while she was still looking at it.

*Go home, Kambria. And stay there.*

She lifted her eyes from the screen, slowly, the way you lift them in a room where something has moved.

Three rows back down the aisle, a man in a gray overcoat sat with a newspaper open against his chest. He did not turn the page. He did not look up. He did not need to.
