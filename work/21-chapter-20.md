## Chapter 20: The Twinkling of an Eye

> *Behold, I shew you a mystery; We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.* — 1 Corinthians 15:51–52

**Word study: *moment* — ἄτομος** Greek *atomos* (Strong's G823). Uncut. A piece of time so small it cannot be divided again. It is the word our *atom* comes from. Paul used it here and nowhere else in the New Testament: the change will not take an hour, or a minute, or a second. It will take the smallest piece of time there is.

*…behold, there appeared a chariot of fire, and horses of fire, and parted them both asunder; and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven. And Elisha saw it, and he cried, My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof. And he saw him no more: and he took hold of his own clothes, and rent them in two pieces.* (2 Kings 2:11–12)

**Wednesday, 8:20 p.m.**

Mia came down the stairs in her mother's shoes.

Ezra heard her before he saw her: the hollow knock of the low square heels on every step, slow and careful, one foot and then the other, as she had walked in them since she was four. He was on the couch, the television low, his phone in his hand, reading a text he had not answered yet. He thumbed the screen dark.

She came around the corner into the living room in her yellow nightgown with the moons on it, her hair in two puffs, holding on to the doorframe for balance. Angela's black Sunday shoes were so big on her that her whole foot fit in the front half, and the heels flapped loose behind her.

"Baby, you're supposed to be in bed. Jalen read you a story already."

"That was Jalen's story." She climbed onto the couch beside him, shoes and all, and settled against his arm. "This one's mine. I made it up. I told Miss Kambria I was going to tell you."

"You told Miss Kambria."

"On the phone. She's bringing the cheesy noodles." Mia tucked her feet up under her, and one of the shoes fell off onto the floor with a thump. She never noticed. "Are you ready?"

He looked down at her. In the light from the television her face was very serious, and very much her mother's. He put the phone aside.

"I'm ready."

She took a breath, as Jalen did before he played.

"There was a man who built a house with a big strong door," she said. "Every night his little girl put a lamp in the window, because he worked late. One night the King came to bring the family to the big supper at His house. And the little girl went, and she was so happy, because her mommy was already there." She stopped, and thought, and nodded to herself. "But she left her lamp in the window. And she left her shoes by the door."

Ezra smiled. "Why'd she leave her shoes, baby?"

"So he'd know which way she went." She yawned, an enormous yawn that took over her whole face. "The door stays open a little while after." She leaned her head on his arm. "The End. Leave the light on, Daddy."

She said it with her whole heart, and with no idea at all of what she had said.

Ezra kissed the top of her head. Her hair smelled like the strawberry shampoo Angela used to buy. He sat with her for a minute, with the television murmuring, and a warmth came over him that he had not known in years. Then he picked her up, and she let him carry her to the foot of the stairs.

"Wait," she said. "Put me down. I have to do the shoes."

He set her down. She went back for the shoe on the living room floor, and picked up the other one, and carried them both to the front door. She set them side by side on the mat with the toes pointed out toward the door, and squared them with both hands. Then she went to the switch by the door and stood on her toes and turned on the porch light.

"So you can find the door," she said.

He took the shoes as part of the story and did not ask. He carried her up to bed and tucked the blanket around her and turned on the nightlight that threw paper stars on the ceiling, and she was asleep before he reached the door.

**Wednesday, 10:05 p.m.**

Jalen was in the studio when his father's truck started.

He heard it through the window, over the cello, and stopped in the middle of a phrase with the bow resting on the string. He set the cello against its stand and went to the studio door and stood in it, in his socks, in the cold, and watched the headlights back down the driveway.

He knew where his father was going. He had known for months. He had seen the small silver car at the curb, and the texts that lit up the phone on the counter and were turned face down. He had smelled the perfume on his father's jacket in the laundry room. He had not said anything since the night in the studio, the night of the recital, when they had both ended up on the floor. He had thought then that everything had changed. Some things had. His father came to dinner now. He asked about school. He had said, himself, that Miss Kambria could come for Thanksgiving.

But he still left, late, on the nights he thought no one was watching.

The truck turned the corner and was gone. Jalen stood in the studio doorway a little longer, looking at the porch light his sister had turned on. Then he went back inside, and sat down, and played the piece one more time, the whole thing, from the low C to the high G at the end, alone in the room they had built.

When he was done, he took a thumb drive out of the pocket of his hoodie.

He had made it seven months ago, on the night of the recital. Two files: the video he had made at eleven, the summer they finished the studio, and the recital itself, the one his father had missed. He had carried it in his pocket ever since, waiting for a night when his father would sit down and listen. Tonight he had thought, for a minute, at dinner, watching his father laugh at something Mia said, that it might be the night.

It was not the night. But tomorrow was Thanksgiving. And Miss Kambria would be at the table. And his father had said she could come, which was more than he had said in a year.

Jalen tore a strip of masking tape off the roll on the workbench and pressed it across the drive, and took a black marker out of the coffee can of pens, and wrote on the tape in small block letters.

*For Dad, now that you're ready.*

He went into the house. The hall was dark except for the porch light coming in through the glass beside the front door, and his mother's shoes standing on the mat. He eased the hall closet open and moved the winter coats aside. On the shelf behind them, behind a box of Christmas lights, was a black notebook. He had known it was there since the week of the funeral. He had seen his mother give it to his father, and he had seen where his father put it.

He set the thumb drive on top of the notebook, right in the center, where no one could pick up one without the other. Then he closed the closet and went to bed.

He set his alarm for six, so he could be up before Grandma got there with the turkey.

**Thursday, 1:52 a.m.**

Ezra came home with the windows down so the cold would wake him up.

The porch light was still on. He saw it from the corner, and his stomach turned. He parked and sat in the truck a minute with the engine ticking. Then he went in by the side door, as he always did on the nights he knew he should not have gone, and kept his eyes off the front hall.

He poured two fingers of whiskey in the kitchen and took it to the couch and sat down to take off his boots. He got one off. He turned the television on low, because the house was too quiet without it. Celeste's perfume was on his collar. He could smell it every time he turned his head.

He told himself he would go up in a minute.

He was asleep before he finished the glass.

**Thursday, a little before six**

Loretta Patterson knelt beside her bed every morning at five o'clock, and she had done it for twenty years, and her knees told her so.

She lived alone in the white frame house on Old Ferry Road where she had raised Ezra, a mile up the gravel from the church where she had married Calvin and buried him. The radiator ticked. Through the window she could see the dark roofs across the street and, beyond them, the black line of the river. In two hours her neighbor's son would come over and help her carry the turkey down to his car, and drive her across the city to her son's house for Thanksgiving. She had made the dressing last night. It was in the refrigerator in her good blue bowl, covered in foil.

The rolls were rising on the counter under a damp towel, the way they did every Thursday and every Sunday, and the whole kitchen smelled of yeast and butter. She had never written the recipe down. She had been thinking lately that she ought to teach it to Mia, standing on a chair at her elbow with flour to the wrists, and that she would start at Christmas.

She had her Bible open on the bed in front of her and her reading glasses on her nose, and she was praying for Ezra.

She always prayed for Ezra first. For twenty years she had started there, before her own needs, before anyone else's. Some mornings she had prayed with tears. Some mornings she had prayed with nothing left in her but the words. This morning her prayer was light, and she did not know why until she found the word for it. Hope.

He had said Kambria could come. He had said it himself.

"Lord," Loretta said, out loud, because she always prayed out loud, in her empty bedroom, to the One who was always in it. "You know my boy. You knew him before I did. You know where he was last night, and You know where he's going." She took off her glasses and pressed her eyes with her fingers. "I'm not asking You to make him good. I'm asking You to wake him up. Whatever it takes. Wake him up, Lord. Don't let him sleep through it."

She stayed on her knees a while longer, listening, as she always did after she prayed. The radiator ticked. A car passed in the street below.

Then she reached for the edge of the bed, to push herself up, and stopped.

There was a sound. It was very far away, or very low, she could not tell which. It seemed to be coming up through the floor.

Loretta Patterson lifted her face toward the window, and the dark sky beyond it, and smiled.

**Thursday, a little before six**

In a fourth-floor walk-up on Carroll Street in Brooklyn, Sarah Bennett stood in her kitchen in her bathrobe, warming a bottle of milk in a pan of water on the stove, half asleep, and heard the baby monitor on the counter go silent.

It was the silence she noticed, afterward. Lily had been fussing for ten minutes, the thin, building cry of a seven-month-old who is hungry and has decided the world should know it. Sarah had been listening to it with one ear while she tested the milk on her wrist. Then the cry stopped, in the middle, as if someone had picked her up.

"Kyle?" she called. "I've got it. Go back to sleep."

Then the windows began to hum.

She would try to describe it later, and never could. She would say it was like a ship's horn, like the shofar a friend had blown once at a wedding, but bigger than anything, bigger than the city, and coming from above and below at the same time. The pan rattled on the burner. The bottle tipped over in the water. Out in the street a garbage truck stopped dead and its driver climbed down and stood in the middle of Carroll Street with his hands over his ears, looking up.

Kyle came out of the bedroom in his boxers with his face white. "What is that? Sarah, what is that?"

She was already running.

The crib was in the corner of their bedroom, under the window, with the mobile of paper birds over it that Sarah had made from a pattern she found online. The birds were wheeling in the gold light that had begun to pour through the window, though it was not yet six and there should have been no light at all.

The crib was empty.

Lily's pajamas lay on the sheet, the green ones with the little foxes, still zipped up, still holding the small round shape of her. Sarah put her hand on them. They were warm. The pacifier lay beside them on the sheet, still wet.

She did not scream. Not yet. She stood with her hand on the warm green pajamas, hardly breathing, and behind her Kyle was saying her name over and over, and outside, over the whole city of New York, the horn went on, and on, and then it stopped.

The silence after it was worse.

**Thursday, a little before six**

The groundskeeper at the cemetery behind Mount Zion Christian Center on Rhode Island Avenue was a man named Leon Fuller, and he was the only living soul who saw it happen there.

He had come in early to unlock the gates, because the families came on holidays and he liked them to find the paths swept. He was halfway down the center path with his push broom when the ground began to shake under his boots. He thought it was an earthquake. He had been in one once, in California, as a young man in the Navy. He dropped the broom and grabbed the nearest headstone with both hands.

Then the horn came, and the light came, and Leon Fuller saw the earth open.

It did not explode. That was what he would tell the reporter from the *Post* three days later, sitting in his kitchen with his hands around a cup of coffee he could not drink. It opened gently, all over the cemetery at once. The grass split, and the soil folded back on itself, and he heard the sound of stone shifting under the ground, and of tearing, and then the light coming out of the graves was the same light coming down out of the sky, and he could not look at it.

When he could see again, the horn had stopped, and the cemetery was quiet, and there were holes in the ground.

Not all of the graves. Perhaps a third of them. They were scattered all through the old rows and the new, an open grave here and an untouched one beside it, as though someone had walked through with a list. The headstones were still standing. The earth was heaped neatly around each hole. And every one of the holes was empty, down to the broken boards of the coffin lids.

Leon Fuller walked the rows for an hour with his broom in his hand, reading the names. Most of them he knew. He had mowed around them for twenty-two years. Near the back fence, under a young dogwood that had been planted a year ago, he stopped beside a granite stone that was still new enough to shine.

*ANGELA PATTERSON. Beloved wife and mother. "I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith."*

The ground in front of it was open. The coffin was empty. Lying on the white satin, where her head had been, was a soft purple scarf.

**Thursday, a little before six**

Walt Simmons had been up since five, as he was every morning.

He was sitting at his dinner table across the street from Ezra Patterson's house, in his cardigan and his slippers, with his coffee and the three newspapers laid out in order and the small black Bible open beside the *Post*. The blue concordance sat on the shelf over the sink, next to the other blue concordance, exactly as Kambria had told him. He had looked at it a dozen times since she dropped him off. He had not touched it.

He was reading the Dubai story on the front page of the *Post*, with the red pen going in the margin, when his coffee cup began to rattle on the saucer.

Walt put down the pen.

He knew what it was before the sound reached the air. He had known what it would be for most of his life. He had read about it in Paul's letters and in Matthew and in Revelation. He sat straight in his chair with both hands on the table, and the sound came up out of the ground and into the sky and filled his kitchen and his chest and the whole world, and the light came through his window over the sink, gold, from every direction at once.

He stood up. He did not use his cane. He stood up beside the dinner table and lifted his face toward the window and closed his eyes.

He waited.

The horn went on. It shook his teeth and the bones of his face. The light lay warm on his eyelids. He stood in his slippers on the linoleum and waited to be lifted, as the book said, as Kambria had told it at his table a hundred times. *Caught up together with them in the clouds.* He waited for his feet to leave the floor.

The horn rose, and held, and rose again.

And then it stopped.

Silence came down over the house. No furnace. No refrigerator. No traffic. Not one bird. Somewhere very far off, to the south, he heard a plane, too low, and then a sound like a door slamming a mile away.

Walt opened his eyes.

He was standing in his kitchen. His coffee had spilled across the *Post* and soaked into the Dubai story. His cane was hooked over the back of his chair where he had left it. His slippers were on the linoleum, and his feet were in them.

He turned his hands over.

Then he looked out the window, across the street, at Ezra Patterson's house, where the porch light was still burning over the front steps in the dark, and where, a few minutes later, from somewhere behind the house, in the backyard, a man's voice began shouting his children's names into the sky.

Walt Simmons was still here.

**Thursday, 7:14 a.m.**

The night guard on Sublevel 3 found the cell at the shift check, and he did not call it in for six minutes.

He stood in the corridor with the clipboard hanging from his hand and looked through the slot in the door of Holding B. Then he unlocked it and went in, because he could not make himself believe what the slot showed him, and stood on the cold concrete where she had sat.

The bench. The ring in the floor. The chain from the ring ran under the bench and up, in a slack gray loop, to the cuffs.

The cuffs were locked.

That was what he could not get past. Both wrist cuffs still closed, the little teeth set in the ratchet exactly as he had set them himself at two in the morning, the steel still holding the small round shape of two wrists. The ankle cuffs, the same. Both pairs lay on the bench like something dropped there in the instant it was used. Beside them lay a gray nightgown, folded in the shape of a woman who had been sitting, with the sleeves still half-raised.

The door had been bolted from the outside. Two bolts. He had thrown them.

Down the corridor a second guard started shouting. Then a third. Then the whole sublevel was shouting, and he heard a man at the far end of the hall begin to pray in Spanish, and somebody else vomit, and the older officer who had been on the arrest team came pounding down the stairs and stopped in the door of Holding B with his mask pushed up.

He looked at the cuffs for a long time.

"That's the lamp lady," he said. His voice cracked. "That's the one with the lamp."

---

The Director arrived at 7:14, by her own key.

Celeste Moore came down the stairs with her hair unpinned and last night's makeup smeared and a coat thrown over the clothes she had put on in the dark. She had been awake for twenty-six hours. She had watched twenty-two seconds of her own face on her phone four hundred times. She had gotten the call from the sublevel on the drive in, and she had told the voice on the phone that it was impossible, and she had said it again at the checkpoint, and she was saying it now, under her breath, as she stopped in front of Holding B.

She looked at the locked cuffs.

She did not say it was impossible again.

"Get me Vale," she said.

It took four minutes to raise him. The monitor on the corridor wall cleared to a view of a hotel suite, pale gold, the window behind him full of Dubai and a sky the color of brass. Vale was in his shirtsleeves. A glass of water stood on the table at his elbow. His face was perfectly still, and his eyes were on the cell behind her before she could say a word.

"Show me," he said.

She stepped aside. A technician turned a tablet to the screen. Vale watched the footage from Holding B at 5:55 and 5:56 that morning, once, and then said, "Again." Then, "Slow."

She was sitting on the bench with her head bowed and her chained hands in her lap. At 5:56:11 she lifted her face, looking up at something the camera could not see. She smiled. Her lips moved. The light on her face went gold. The cuffs dropped, empty, onto the steel.

He watched it a third time.

"Seven of the eleven cells," the guard said from the doorway, hoarsely. "Same thing. Every one of the ones we picked up this month. The ones from the churches."

"I understand you, Officer." Vale did not raise his voice. He had not raised it once. "Nobody enters this sublevel. Nobody leaves it. Not you, not the day shift, not the Director. Seal the stairwell and remove the badge readers." He looked at the tablet again, at the cuffs falling open and shut. "This is not a security breach."

"Alexander," Celeste said.

"No." He lifted one hand. "It is a confirmation." For the first time something moved behind his face. It was not fear. It was arithmetic. "Everything we planned for assumed this would not happen. It has happened. It will not wait for us to finish." He turned the glass a quarter turn. "I have nine days of work and I have the Chairman asking me for an account of this morning. I cannot afford the Chairman learning that his Director of Special Operations stood where you are standing, and watched what you watched, and understands it."

Celeste's face changed.

"You filed under your own name," Vale said gently. "You locked her out of the system. You sat in that room in Baltimore and read the Petworth files. You have followed this woman for three months, and tonight you watched a locked cell empty itself, and you know what it means. I saw your face. You know it better than I do." He spoke to the technician, without looking away from her. "Is the intake chamber on this level still sealed?"

"Holding B is the intake chamber, sir."

"Of course it is."

Celeste took a step back. "Alexander. I built this. I got you the Petworth lot. I got you in the door at the CDC. I am the only person in this country who—"

"Yes."

"I haven't said a word! I would never—"

"I know." His voice was almost kind. "That is the difficulty. You would not. And in a week you will be unable to stop." He nodded to the guards. "I'm sorry, Celeste."

They took her by the arms. She fought them. She was stronger than they expected, and for a few seconds she got an elbow free and caught one of them across the mouth, and the corridor filled with the sound of her voice, high and raw, calling Ezra's name and then another name and then nothing. They dragged her across the threshold of Holding B. The older officer who had spared the lamp turned his face to the wall.

The cuffs were still locked. The technician had to key them open to seat her.

They closed on her wrists with a click. They closed on her ankles. The chain ran taut from the ring in the floor under the bench.

"Don't," Celeste said. She had gone very quiet. She was looking up, at the grille above the drain, and the flat nozzle behind it. "Alexander. Please. It isn't ready. You said it wasn't ready."

"It was never going to be," Vale said from the screen. "That is why I need to know that it works."

He nodded.

The door shut. Two bolts. A third was thrown from the corridor, and a red lamp came on in the frame.

In the control booth, the technician pressed a key under a taped label that read, in block capitals, **PALEHORSE LOT 3**. It was the first time that name had ever been spoken aloud in that building.

Through the slot in the door, the nozzle opened. It made a sound like a kettle.

Celeste Moore screamed once.

On the monitor, in a hotel suite in Dubai, Alexander Vale turned off the sound. He watched the picture for ninety seconds, as a man watches a gauge, until the vapor had taken the camera. Then he picked up his glass of water and drank.

"Log it as a decontamination test," he said. "Date it Wednesday."

He ended the call.
